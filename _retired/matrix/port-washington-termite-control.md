## What is under a Port Washington foundation, and why does nobody know?

Because a lot of this peninsula's ground surface is a construction product, and construction products do not come with drawings.

Port Washington was a sand town for well over a century. Extraction ran on the western shore of Hempstead Harbor from 1865 until 1989, and something in the order of 140 million cubic yards left the peninsula over that period. Some of the excavated ground was later redeveloped — Harbor Links, the golf course opened in 1998, sits on former pits — and other parts carry housing and commercial development. Beyond the mines themselves, a hamlet with roughly 270 feet of relief between Beacon Hill and the Town Dock has been cut and filled continuously to make buildable lots out of slopes.

The underlying geology is the Harbor Hill Moraine, the terminal moraine of the last glaciation, which is why the natural soils here are coarse, sandy and free-draining. That matters because it sets an expectation that placed ground frequently does not meet. Fill can be finer than what it replaced. It can be compacted unevenly, so that one part of a footing bears on dense material and another on loose. It can be layered, which produces a perched water condition where water moving down through a permeable layer meets a tighter one and travels sideways instead.

For this particular insect, the consequence is specific and it is the reason this page begins with soil rather than with biology. Eastern subterranean termites live in the ground and depend on a reliable source of moisture; Cornell Cooperative Extension's homeowner guidance is direct about that requirement, and about the fact that any wood at or near soil contact is susceptible. A foundation sitting in ground that holds water — for reasons that are invisible because they are two feet down and forty years old — is a foundation with the precondition permanently satisfied.

The practical signature is one that turns up on survey after survey here: a corner or a wall section that is persistently damp when nothing about the visible grading explains it. The gutters are fine. The grade falls away. The leader discharges properly. And the masonry is still wet. On this peninsula, that finding points downward, and the correction is a drainage one rather than anything that comes out of a sprayer. The [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile covers the insect; the approach in general is under [termite control](/pest-control/termite-control/).

## How does 270 feet of relief decide which corner gets it?

By delivering other people's water to one point on your building.

On flat ground a house deals with the rain that lands on it. On a slope it deals with that plus whatever arrives from above, and on this peninsula "above" can mean several properties. Runoff converges. It follows the line of least resistance through gardens, along drives, down the side of a retaining wall, and it collects at whichever point on the downhill property offers the lowest path. That point is frequently a foundation corner, and it will be wet regardless of how well the house itself is drained.

That is the first thing a survey establishes here, and it is done by walking the property in rain rather than in sunshine. Where does water arrive from? What is above this house, and what did the people above do with their drainage? Is there a swale, a kerb line, a drive that acts as a channel? On steeply built streets it is entirely normal to find a property with immaculate gutters, correct grading and one saturated corner that has nothing to do with the building at all.

The slope produces a second effect that changes the inspection itself. On a hillside lot a foundation is commonly buried at the uphill side and substantially exposed at the downhill side. So the relationship between soil and timber is not one measurement — it is four, and they can differ by feet. Anyone surveying a sloping property from a single elevation has read a quarter of it.

And a third, at the boiler. Cornell's guidance notes that termites favour heat from furnaces, chimneys and hot water pipes, particularly in winter. On a hillside house the mechanical room is very often at the low end of the building, tucked into the buried side, which is also the wettest corner. Warm and damp at the same point is the highest-probability location on many houses here, and it is worth going to first.

## Why are retaining walls and terraces the largest single risk here?

Because they put a great deal of wood into wet soil, on purpose, near the house.

You cannot build on 270 feet of relief without holding ground back. Terracing is the defining landscape feature of this hamlet: stepped gardens, raised beds cut into a bank, garden stairs, driveway edges, planted terraces above and below a house, and a great many of them built in timber because timber was cheap, fast and looked right.

A timber retaining wall is, from the insect's point of view, an unusually generous structure. It is wood in continuous soil contact along its whole buried face. It has damp fill packed against the back of it. It has ties or deadmen running horizontally into that fill, which are more wood, further from air, wetter. It has been there for twenty or forty years. And it is frequently within a few feet of a foundation, or holding a bank directly above one.

Pressure treatment does not settle this. Treatment has a service life, that life is shortest at the ground line where wetting and drying alternate, and a wall built in the 1970s or 1980s has generally passed it. What is left is a large body of softening timber that will support a colony, positioned uphill of the building where the water is already going.

The same logic covers the rest of what a terraced garden accumulates: sleepers used as steps or bed edging, timber stair stringers with their feet in soil, deck posts set into the ground rather than on piers, fence posts against a foundation, and the buried remains of an earlier wall that was left in place when the current one was built in front of it.

The corrective work here is genuinely large and worth being honest about at the proposal stage. Replacing a timber retaining wall in masonry or engineered block is a landscaping project, not a pest control item. Where it is not going to happen, the alternative is to treat the wall as a known colony resource, monitor it deliberately, and manage the route between it and the building — which is a smaller claim than removing the risk, and it should be stated as one.

## What did the post-war ring get built with?

Slab and shallow crawlspace construction that hides its routes, on lots that were graded to fit.

The census area's median construction year is 1953. About a third of the units predate 1940 and roughly 29 per cent went up in the 1950s, so the shape of the hamlet is a pre-war harbour village with a substantial post-war ring built around and above it. Port Washington North's Soundview section, which began construction in 1959, is a clean example of the later build type.

Houses from that period share a set of features that change termite work:

**The sill starts low and gets lower.** Timber that began eight inches clear of soil has, after six decades of mulch top-ups, a patio, a raised bed and general landscape creep, frequently been reduced to two inches or to nothing. Once soil touches siding the insect does not need to build a shelter tube at all, and there is nothing to see from the garden.

**Slabs conceal the route.** In a cellar you can read the inside face of the wall and the sill above it. A slab offers no such surface. The routes are internal to the concrete — expansion joints, the cold joint where a garage slab meets the house slab, plumbing and conduit penetrations, and the settlement cracking that any slab of that age has somewhere.

**The attached garage is the blind spot.** It has a slab, a step down, a sill line hidden behind stored goods on three walls, and it is where the services gather.

**Later additions multiply the junctions.** A family room off the back, a sunroom over a former patio, a dormer. Each meets the original structure where framing does not align and where a new slab was poured against an old foundation.

On the hillside versions of these houses there is one further complication. A 1950s split built into a bank has a lower level that is partly below grade on one side, and that lower level is usually finished — panelling, a dropped ceiling, a carpet. Finished basements are the single most common reason an inspection here cannot answer the question that was asked. The wall is behind the panelling and nobody wants it removed on a hunch.

## How do termites reach a heavy masonry building at Sands Point?

Through the joints and the embedded timber, because mass is not a barrier.

The estate fabric at the northern end of the peninsula is unlike anything else in this market. The Sands Point Preserve holds Castle Gould, completed in 1902; Hempstead House, a fifty-thousand-square-foot Tudor with a sixty-foot entry foyer; and Falaise, the French-Norman house built on the bluff in 1923. Private properties around Sands Point and Harbor Acres are in the same family of construction if not the same scale — thick masonry walls, complex rooflines, extensive grounds and outbuildings.

People assume a stone building is immune. It is not, because these buildings contain a lot of wood and the wood is where you cannot see it:

**Embedded timber.** Older masonry construction routinely includes wooden lintels over openings, bond timbers laid within a wall, and nailing grounds set into the masonry to fix joinery to. All of it is inside the wall.

**Floor framing let into masonry.** Joist ends bear in pockets within the wall. The end grain of each joist is the most absorbent surface on the member, sitting in a pocket in a wall that wicks moisture from the ground.

**The wall-to-terrace interface.** Grand houses have grand terraces, and the junction where a stone terrace meets a wall is exactly the concealed soil-to-structure contact that matters.

**Service penetrations.** A century of different trades bringing services through a thick wall leaves a large number of holes packed with whatever was to hand.

**Outbuildings.** Garage blocks, garden stores, stables converted to other uses, pool houses. These are usually of lighter construction, less well maintained, and closer to soil, and a colony is frequently established there before anything reaches the main house.

Work on buildings of this kind is slower and the survey is longer, because the accessible surface area is enormous and the evidence is subtle. It also has a specific documentation requirement: on a property with this much fabric, a report that does not say clearly which parts were reached is close to useless.

## What can actually be established on a cut-and-fill lot?

Less than a brochure implies, and knowing that in advance is what makes the plan sensible.

The two general approaches are a continuous treated zone in the soil around the structure, or a monitored in-ground system that works with the colony's own foraging. On this peninsula both run into the same two obstacles.

**Access.** On a terraced hillside lot, a large share of the perimeter is under something: a terrace, a set of steps, a drive, a retaining wall, a paved courtyard, an apron. Establishing continuous treated soil there means drilling and patching, and on a property where the hard landscaping is a substantial investment, that is a conversation to have honestly and early rather than on the morning of the work.

**Uncertainty about the soil itself.** A treated zone assumes a reasonably predictable substrate. Where ground has been cut, filled and layered, the assumption is weaker: material may be coarser or finer than expected, and there may be buried debris, a former wall, an old pit edge, or a services trench backfilled with something else entirely. It is not that the approach cannot work. It is that the site has to be read rather than assumed, and that a plan drawn from a standard perimeter detail is a plan for a different lot.

That is why the monitored approach earns its place on a lot of properties here — it does not require access to soil that has been built over, and it uses the insect's own foraging to find the stations rather than requiring the treatment to find the insect. The condition attached to it is discipline. Stations do something while they are serviced on a schedule and nothing at all when they are not.

In practice the two are combined more often than they are chosen between: treated soil where the perimeter is genuinely open, stations where it is under hard landscaping. What decides the mix is the survey — how much perimeter is reachable, where the water arrives from, whether there is a well on the property, how close the drainage runs, and what is beneath the paving.

Alongside either, the physical corrections do the durable work: lowering grade back off cladding, removing buried timber, dealing with the retaining walls, extending a leader so it stops discharging into the corner where the tubes were found, and replacing framing that has lost section. On a Port Washington property that list is usually longer and more expensive than the treatment, and it is the half that changes the risk rather than managing it. ## Which parts of the peninsula behave differently?

Enough of them that a hamlet-wide statement about termite risk here is not worth much.

**Manorhaven.** Compact housing on low ground close to the water table, on smaller lots. Short distance from footing to water, high summer humidity in crawlspaces, and framing that runs at elevated moisture content for much of the year. The risk here is moisture-driven and it is site-wide rather than concentrated at a corner.

**Baxter Estates.** Small, historic and overlooking Manhasset Bay, with old fabric and old ground. The Baxter Homestead of 1673 stood at Central Drive and Shore Road until fire destroyed it in 2017. Pre-war and earlier construction with the foundation types and buried-alteration history that go with it.

**Beacon Hill and the high ground.** Free-draining coarse material, quick shedding, framing that dries between wettings. Activity here tends to be tied to a single identifiable fault — a leader discharging at a wall, an irrigation head aimed at the foundation, a stump, a timber wall — rather than to a general condition.

**Soundview and the late-post-war tracts.** Repeatable construction, which makes the risk list predictable: low sill, slab or shallow crawl, attached garage, sleeved penetrations, later additions.

**Sands Point and Harbor Acres.** Estate-scale masonry with embedded timber, outbuildings, and enough fabric that the survey is measured in hours.

**Port Washington North and the Mill Pond area**, designated a historic district in 1973 with eleven sites landmarked in 1982, mixes older fabric with the 1950s development around it.

## When in the year does a hillside property tell you the truth?

In the wet weeks, which is the opposite of when most people arrange to have a house looked at.

A survey on a dry August afternoon reads a building at the point when it is disclosing least. The soil is dry, the masonry has had eight weeks to give up its moisture, the swale that runs three times a winter is a shallow depression in a lawn, and the corner that floods is simply a corner. Everything the slope does to this property is invisible.

The informative period runs from the late-winter thaw through the spring rain. Frozen ground sheds almost everything that falls on it, so a thaw with rain on top of it produces the largest surface flows of the year and shows you exactly where water converges. Snowmelt at the base of a bank shows where drifting and shading keep ground saturated longest. A hard spring downpour shows which leader is undersized, which drain backs up, and which part of a drive turns into a watercourse.

Then the biological calendar overlays it. Reproductives fly between March and June, in daylight, often on a mild day after rain — the same weather that is telling you where the water goes. So the weeks when the evidence is most likely to appear are the weeks when the building's moisture behaviour is most legible. That is not a coincidence; the two are the same phenomenon observed twice.

The rest of the year has its uses. **Summer** is the window for the physical corrections — regrading, replacing a timber retaining wall, cutting a swale, extending a leader, rebuilding a terrace edge — because the ground is workable and the result can be tested by the first serious autumn rain rather than discovered the following March. **Autumn** is when leaf fall blocks the drainage that was working perfectly in August, so a check after the leaves are down is worth more than a check before. **Winter** puts the interior evidence in play: in an unheated or partly heated lower level, the warm corner near the boiler is the part of the building the insect prefers in cold months, and a cold morning makes the temperature differences obvious.

What all of this argues for is a survey booked around the weather rather than around the calendar, and a willingness to go back and look at a property twice in different conditions before writing a plan for it.

## What has to be re-checked afterwards, and why does the ground make that harder?

Because on this peninsula the ground keeps changing, and a treated perimeter is only as continuous as the last person to dig in it left it.

A zone established in soil works while it is intact. On a hillside property, the events that break it are routine: a French drain retrofitted after a wet spring, an irrigation line trenched in, a retaining wall rebuilt, a bank replanted, a fence renewed, a new patio. Each of those removes or disturbs treated ground, and nobody thinks to mention it because nobody connects a landscaping job to a termite treatment.

There is a version of this that comes from next door. On a slope, work uphill changes what arrives downhill. A neighbour who regrades, paves a drive, cuts a bank or installs a new drainage run can alter the water regime at your foundation without any change on your own property at all. That is a genuinely local failure mode and it is the strongest practical argument for periodic re-inspection here rather than a one-off exercise.

The other reason to look again is that this insect is built to avoid discovery. The colony is underground, the workers stay enclosed, damaged framing keeps an intact outer surface, and the one visible annual event — reproductives flying between March and June — lasts a few hours in the middle of a working day. What people usually find afterwards is a scatter of small translucent wings on a sill, and the location of that sill is worth more than the insects: outdoors near a stump means a colony on the property, indoors at a window or a baseboard means the colony is in contact with the structure.
