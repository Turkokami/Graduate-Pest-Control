## Why does a village above salt water still carry termite pressure?

Because the insect wants soil moisture, and a shoreline village on falling ground supplies more of it than an inland lot does, not less.

Eastern subterranean termites live in the ground. Workers forage outward from a colony in the soil, travel in covered runs to protect themselves from drying out, and consume wood along the grain while leaving the outer surface intact. Nothing in that biology requires a pond, a creek or fresh surface water. It requires soil that does not dry out, and timber it can reach without breaking cover.

Northport Bay runs a tidal range of about seven feet, which means a large twice-daily exchange of saturated air across the shoreline and a water table that sits high on the lower ground. Above that, the village occupies the low, steep hills of the Harbor Hill Moraine, and a single parcel can fall twenty feet from a ridge street to the water. Coarse glacial material sheds water quickly — but water that moves quickly still has to arrive somewhere, and on a built-up lot it arrives at whichever face of the house the grading sends it to.

That is the local mechanism in one sentence: the ground does not stay uniformly wet, it stays reliably wet in one place. The three-inch minimum clearance between wood and soil that Cornell Cooperative Extension names is a village-wide requirement; the place it has actually been lost is usually a single elevation, and finding that elevation is most of the diagnostic work. The [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile covers the insect in detail. What changes from town to town is the ground and the building, and here both are unusually specific.

## What does a bimodal housing stock do to a termite survey?

It splits it into two jobs that share a name and very little else.

The census area records a median construction year of 1957 across roughly 3,230 units, and that median describes almost nothing. About 920 units — 28.5 per cent — date from 1939 or earlier. A further 584 went up in the 1950s. The Skidmore House of 1761 still stands. So the number is the arithmetic midpoint between two substantial populations of buildings with very little transitional stock between them.

**On the pre-1940 half**, the foundation is rubble, fieldstone or early block, and the wooden sill plate bears directly on it with no capillary break. Stone and old mortar are capillary materials: ground water travels into them from the soil side and moves through them, and the sill draws moisture out of the masonry continuously. Timber at that elevated moisture content is both attractive to a foraging population and hospitable to the decay fungi that soften it further.

Finding evidence on that wall is genuinely difficult. A shelter tube on smooth block is unmistakable — a raised line of soil cemented to the surface, running from floor toward framing, anywhere from the width of a wheat straw to wider than a thumb. On rubble the same tube tucks into a mortar recess and reads as one more irregularity in a wall that consists of nothing but irregularities. It is found with a light held close to the stone and raked along it, not pointed at it, and it is found only if the stored goods have come off the perimeter first.

**On the post-war half**, the wall is poured concrete and a tube on it is obvious. The difficulty moves elsewhere. The approach is hidden by the slab, the stoop, the garage apron and the attached terrace, and the timber the insect is heading for is the band joist, which on a great many of these houses is behind a finished basement. The wall reads clean and the junction above it cannot be seen at all.

The third condition, and it belongs to the older half, is joist pockets. Floor framing let into masonry rather than carried on a ledger puts every joist end inside a pocket in a damp wall. That end grain is the most absorbent surface on the member and the least visible one. A survey that reads the sill line and stops has skipped them.

## What has a working waterfront put into the ground?

Timber, in permanent soil contact, in quantities you do not find on an inland lot.

By 1874 the village carried three ship yards and five sets of marine railways. Waterfront ground here has been built on, filled, launched from and rebuilt for the better part of two centuries, and each phase left something below grade. On properties near the water, the recurring finds are specific to the place:

**Bulkhead and seawall tiebacks.** A timber bulkhead is anchored back into the ground behind it with deadmen and rods. The anchor timbers are buried, wet and structural, and they are usually closer to the house than anyone assumes.

**Launch ways and rail beds.** Marine railway construction was heavy timber laid into wet ground. Where a yard has been converted to residential use, some of it stayed.

**Cradle and blocking timber.** Boat storage over the winter means blocking, chocks and cradle framing sitting on ground for months at a time, often in the same position year after year, which produces a permanently shaded, permanently damp strip of soil holding wood.

**Decking sleepers and ramp framing.** Any structure that steps down toward the water is bearing on something, and on older properties that something is timber.

**Retaining and terrace structures on the fall.** Railway sleepers and landscape timber used to hold a bed or step a slope. Treatment does not last indefinitely at the ground line, and these are frequently within a few feet of the foundation.

None of that is visible on a walk-round, which is why the history of the property matters as much as its present condition. When somebody mentions that the garden was regraded when the terrace went in, or that there used to be a shed where the parking area is now, that is survey information. It says which side of the house to be sceptical about.

The corrective work is unglamorous and it is often the largest single line on a proposal: dig out the buried timber where it can be reached, drop the grade back off the siding, remove the raised bed built against the wall, redirect the leader that has been discharging at the same corner for a decade. None of that kills an insect. All of it removes the reason the insect was interested.

## Where does the fall of the ground put the treated zone at risk?

At every point where the original grade was interrupted to make the lot usable, which on this terrain is most of it.

A property that drops twenty feet has been terraced. There is a level sitting area somewhere, a set of steps, a retaining wall holding a bed, a paved apron running to a garage, and a path cut into the slope. Each of those interrupts the soil, and a soil treatment works only while it is continuous.

Three practical consequences follow.

**Part of the perimeter is not soil at all.** Under a bluestone terrace, a poured stoop or a garage apron, reaching the ground means drilling. That is invasive, dusty and audible, and the patches will be visible afterwards. It is a legitimate approach and it belongs in the conversation at the proposal stage rather than on the morning of the work.

**Fill behind a retaining wall behaves differently from undisturbed ground.** It is looser, it holds water, and it frequently contains whatever was on site when the wall was built. A treated zone established in that material is not the same as one established in native soil, and the honest scope says so.

**Ground moves here.** Slopes settle, walls lean, steps drop, and freeze and thaw work on everything. A zone that was continuous when it was installed may not be continuous three winters later, and the way you find out is by looking rather than by assuming.

Where a perimeter is largely paved or terraced, a monitored in-ground system becomes attractive for a straightforward reason: it does not require access to soil that is under concrete. It is worth exactly what its servicing schedule is worth, though — installed and forgotten, it is a row of caps in a lawn. Anyone weighing competing approaches should read [choosing a pest control contractor](/compliance/choosing-a-pest-control-contractor/) before comparing on price, because on this terrain the differences that matter are access and scope rather than product. What the applicator has to hold is set out at [New York State pesticide applicator requirements](/compliance/nys-pesticide-applicator-requirements/), and where a neighbouring property is close enough to be affected, [the New York neighbor notification law](/compliance/ny-neighbor-notification-law/) is the relevant framework.

## What has the window replacement exemption got to do with termites?

More than it sounds, because it decides where the wet timber is on the older stock.

Northport's Board of Architectural and Historic Review examines applications for construction, reconstruction, alteration and demolition, and its criteria name red common brick, clapboard, shingles and stone as preferred materials. It explicitly exempts painting and door and window replacement. That exemption is the reason the openings on a historic village house may have been changed two or three times over the decades with no record of it.

For rodent and air-leakage purposes the consequence is a gap between the old rough opening and the new frame. For termites the consequence is different and slower. Where an opening was replaced without correcting the flashing, the sill pan or the drainage path behind the trim, water gets into the rough opening and stays there. Over years that produces exactly the condition the insect exploits — a body of consistently damp timber inside a wall, connected to the framing below it, with no visible symptom on the face of the building.

On a house where the ground floor sits low, or where a porch deck, a bulkhead door or a terrace brings finished level close to a window or door sill, that damp timber is within the reach of a covered run. It is one of the few places on a frame house where the insect can arrive at a target that is already softened.

The relevant point for an owner is practical rather than regulatory: correcting the rough opening around a replaced window is generally the least restricted work available on a listed-material elevation, and on this stock it is frequently the most productive. Where a repair goes further — replacing a decayed sill, rebuilding porch framing, altering a visible elevation — the village is the office to ask, before the scope is fixed rather than after.

## What does the March-to-June flight look like on the harbour side?

A brief, easily missed event that is worth a great deal once you know how to read where it happened.

Winged reproductives take flight between March and June, in daylight, most often on a mild day following rain. They are dark and roughly rice-grain sized, with two pairs of equal-length wings noticeably longer than the body, and they shed those wings within minutes of landing. What people actually find is not insects but a scatter of small translucent wings on a sill, in a window well, or caught in a cobweb in a corner of a cellar.

The location of the emergence is the diagnostic, and this stock adds the complication described above. In the pre-1940 frame construction the wall cavity runs from the sill plate to the top plate with no blocking. Reproductives released into a wall at sill level travel up the cavity toward light and appear at a second-floor window, at a light fitting, or through a gap at the top of a casing. An upper-floor finding says nothing about where the colony reached the building. It usually says the cavity is open, which is separately useful.

Emergence from a stump, a landscape timber, an old cradle position or open soil at the bottom of the garden tells you a colony is on the property. Emergence from inside the building — a cellar post, a crack at a hearth, a baseboard, a window casing — tells you the colony is in contact with the structure. Those are different conversations with different scopes.

The spring window also generates confusion, because carpenter ants resume activity across the same weeks and also produce winged forms. That distinction is quick once you have looked at one of each, and the [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) profile shows the difference. Keeping a few specimens in a sealed bag costs nothing and settles the question before anybody quotes anything.

## How do you tell a live route from an abandoned one?

By breaking a tube and coming back, which is the cheapest diagnostic in this trade and the one most often skipped.

A shelter tube is built to keep a worker in contact with the humidity of the soil while it crosses an exposed surface. If the route still matters to the colony, damage to it gets repaired, usually within a few days. If the route has been abandoned, nothing happens. So the technique is to break a short section cleanly, note where and when, and look again about a week later. Repaired means active. Untouched means the run was disused, which does not mean the colony has gone — it means that particular path has been given up.

That distinction changes the scope substantially. A cellar wall carrying a dozen old, dry, unrepaired tubes describes a history. One repaired tube on the same wall describes a live problem with a known location, and it tells you which side of the building the colony is approaching from, which is the single most useful piece of information a survey can produce on this housing stock.

Two Northport-specific complications are worth knowing. On rubble and fieldstone the tube is hard to see in the first place, so the break-and-return method depends on having found it at all — which is why the raking light matters more than the return visit does. And on a falling lot the approach may be entirely under a terrace, a stoop or a paved apron, in which case there is no exposed run to break and the finding has to come from elsewhere: from the framing above, from a probe, or from the wet corner the grading created.

The other evidence people bring in is the wings. Discarded wings are about a quarter of an inch long, translucent, and found in small drifts rather than singly. A handful on an interior sill in April is a location. A handful outside on a step, in a village with mature trees and old landscape timber all around, is a great deal less specific.

## What does a Northport termite survey actually cover?

The relationship with the soil, the whole of the cellar, and every seam where the building was altered.

**The grade line, measured at every elevation.** Where does soil sit relative to the top of the foundation and the bottom of the siding? On falling ground this is never consistent all the way round, so it gets taken at the terrace, the bed, the walk, the garage apron and the shoreline face separately.

**The full interior face of the foundation wall.** Floor to sill, which means stored goods come off the perimeter first. On rubble, with a raking light. Joist pockets individually. Pier tops in any crawlspace section. The band joist all the way round, and an honest note where a finished basement means it could not be seen.

**Sounding and probing.** Tapping accessible framing and listening for the dull note that means lost section, then testing with an awl where it sounds wrong. Because these insects leave the outer skin of the wood in place, this is the only reliable way to find damage in reachable timber, and it is why a real inspection takes hours.

**The alteration seams.** Where a porch was enclosed. Where a kitchen was pushed out. Where a bathroom was added above a room whose floor was never designed to carry plumbing. Where new sheathing meets old. Those junctions have mismatched framing, improvised flashing and voids left because closing them would have meant opening more of the house.

**The outbuildings and the shoreline structures.** Boathouses, gear stores, garden buildings, steps, decks and anything that touches the water. These are the wettest timber on the property and the least often examined.

**The water.** Gutters, leaders and where they discharge; any crawlspace; condensate lines; and plumbing that has been weeping quietly inside a wall. On this stock the water almost always explains the location of the activity.

What comes out is a drawing and a written finding: where evidence was seen, what kind, what could not be reached and why. What does not come out is proof of absence.

## Why does a Northport termite job come back?

Four reasons, and the product is rarely among them.

**The moisture fault was never corrected.** The activity was dealt with and the terrace still turns water back at the same corner. That building remains the most attractive target on the street, decay fungi are still working in the same timber, and the conditions now suit carpenter ants as well. This is the failure that presents as a different problem two years later.

**Something buried stayed buried.** Cradle blocking, a bulkhead anchor, a stump beside the foundation, sleepers holding a bed. Those are colony-supporting resources within feet of the house.

**The perimeter was opened up afterwards.** Treated soil only does its job while the ring is unbroken. An irrigation trench, a new fence line, a drainage retrofit or a bed dug over and replanted all remove treated soil, and nobody thinks to mention it to a pest contractor.

**Nobody came back to look.** Everything about this insect resists being found — the colony sits underground, the workers travel enclosed, damaged timber keeps its face, and the single visible event of the year passes in a few hours on a weekday afternoon. Re-inspection is the only mechanism by which a problem is found in year three rather than year nine, and on framing that has already carried a house for a century that difference is structural.
