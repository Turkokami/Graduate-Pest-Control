## Why does a termite job here begin with a question about the ground?

Because most of what termite treatment does is put something into soil, and in parts of this city soil is governed.

From 1942 to 1985 various companies processed metals at the 26-acre Li Tungsten facility on Garvies Point Road along Glen Cove Creek, disposing of wastes into surrounding areas including a municipal and industrial dump. EPA cleared radioactive materials, chemicals and storage tanks in 1989 and 1990, added the site to the National Priorities List in 1992, and closed the cleanup out in 2008 — contaminated soil and sediment removed, the facility demolished, a dewatering facility constructed for Glen Cove Creek, and land use restrictions imposed. Of the 214 acres in the waterfront redevelopment area, 68 acres sit within state and federal Superfund sites.

Land use restrictions govern soil disturbance. Now list what a conventional subterranean termite programme involves: trenching around a foundation, rodding through soil, drilling and treating beneath abutting slabs, excavating to expose a footing, and installing in-ground monitoring or bait equipment. Every one of those is soil disturbance.

So on or beside the restricted acreage, the sequence is not negotiable: establish what the parcel's restrictions actually permit, in writing, before scoping anything that goes into the ground. This is a matter of doing the work lawfully. It is not a reason to be vague about it in a proposal, and it is not a reason to avoid the work — on a restricted parcel the scope moves onto the structure, into the crawlspace or cellar, and into the drainage and grade corrections that can be made above ground.

It is also not a citywide condition, and saying so matters. Most of Glen Cove's 11,462 housing units sit nowhere near that acreage, on ordinary residential ground, and the question resolves in a phone call. What is not acceptable is quoting a trenching programme on a waterfront parcel without having asked.

The redeveloped ground itself is now Garvies Point: over 1,000 LEED-certified housing units, 75,000 square feet of commercial space, a ferry terminal opened in 2016, a marina, a beachfront park and an amphitheatre, with The Green at Garvies Point adding 55 affordable apartments.

## What does 175 feet of relief do to a building?

It delivers water to one elevation with momentum, and the termite evidence follows that elevation almost without exception.

Average elevation across the city is about 45 feet, and the range runs from sea level at the shore up to 175 feet at a point between Harbor Hill and Carpenter Street. Level ground is interspersed with steep knolls and slopes, and steep slopes and bluffs sit along Hempstead Harbor, along Glen Cove Creek and around Garvies Point. The hills along the shore belong to the Harbor Hill Moraine, left by the glaciers of the last ice age.

On that kind of ground a building does not get wet evenly. It gets wet on the uphill face, for as long as the grading stays as it is, and the consequences compound in a specific order.

**Soil moisture on the uphill face stays high.** That is the condition subterranean termites need, and it is present there and frequently nowhere else on the same lot.

**Grade tends to be highest on that face too.** Where a slope meets a building, the usual response over a century has been to build the ground up against it — a terrace, a retaining wall, a raised bed, a set of steps — which closes the clearance Cornell Cooperative Extension asks for: a minimum of three inches between wood siding or skirting and soil, eight inches of clean concrete between skirting and soil.

**Runoff arrives with debris.** Leaf litter, mulch and soil wash to the base of the uphill wall and stay there, raising the effective grade a little more each year without anybody deciding to.

**The downhill corner is where it leaves.** On a falling lot the low corner collects everything the rest of the site sheds, and that corner is where a splash zone, a soft step or a rotted skirt detail usually turns up.

The survey consequence is that a Glen Cove termite inspection should not be reported as a perimeter average. It should be reported elevation by elevation, with the grade height measured against those two figures on each face, because on this relief three faces can be entirely compliant while the fourth is buried.

## Which half of this housing stock actually gets termites?

The older half and the low half — and Glen Cove has an unusually wide spread of both.

The city's median construction year is 1960, but that number conceals the distribution rather than describing it. About 2,155 units, 18.8 per cent, predate 1940. Some 2,842 went up in the 1950s and 1,518 in the 1960s. A further 1,152 have been added since 2010, which is more recent construction than the rest of this stretch of the North Shore has seen combined.

Those three groups present three different problems.

**Pre-1940, on rubble or early block.** Mortar joints that were never a continuous seal, with eighty years or more of freeze-thaw cycling to open them, and a hand-cut sill bearing on top. There is no single crack to find because the wall is a matrix of routes. Cornell describes mud tubes varying in diameter from the size of a wheat straw to wider than a thumb, and on this masonry a tube can run inside a joint for a considerable distance before it becomes visible. Detail on reading one is in the [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile.

**Post-war, on poured concrete.** A defined wall with discrete penetrations, which is a far more legible surface. What complicates it here is not the wall but what has been built against it, because a 1950s house on a Glen Cove slope has usually acquired terracing, steps, a patio and a retaining wall in the decades since.

**Post-2010, tight and modern.** Detailed for soil contact from the start, with capillary breaks, proper clearances and drainage designed rather than improvised. The exposure is not zero — no building's is — but it is a maintenance question rather than an inherited one, and the useful work on that stock is keeping the landscaping from undoing what the detailing achieved.

The tenure split cuts across all three. About 5,656 units are owner-occupied and 5,439 renter-occupied, 49.3 per cent against 47.5 per cent, with only 48.8 per cent of units detached single-family houses. Termite work needs access to the cellar, the crawlspace and the grounds, and in half this city the person who notices the problem has access to none of those.

## Who authorises a termite treatment in a building of fifty units?

Whoever holds the building — and that has to be established before anything else happens, because the work touches parts of the property no occupant can reach.

About 1,461 Glen Cove units sit in buildings of 50 or more, 820 in buildings of 20 to 49, and 990 in three- and four-unit buildings. Add the 1,513 units in two-family buildings and the city's shared-building stock is substantial.

Termite work on a shared building differs from rodent or cockroach work in one important respect: almost none of it happens inside the dwelling where the problem is reported. The evidence may appear at an apartment window as a scatter of wings. The route is in the cellar, the crawlspace, the podium slab or the ground outside, and the corrective work is to the grade, the drainage and the structure.

That produces a predictable sequence of frustrations if the scope is addressed to the wrong person.

**The occupant reports swarmers and expects an appointment.** What they need is for a report to reach the managing agent, with the location and a sample.

**The agent can authorise a treatment but not always a structural repair.** Where the finding is a wood-to-soil contact at a terrace or a decayed sill section, that is a capital item and a different decision.

**The owner controls the grounds.** Grade correction, terrace works and drainage are landscape and building decisions, and on a large parcel they may already be under a maintenance contract that has been raising the grade for years.

So the useful first deliverable on a shared building is not a treatment proposal. It is a short factual note: where swarmers appeared, what was found in the cellar and around the base of the building, which elevations carry excess grade, and which parts of the property could not be accessed. That document is what allows the right person to make a decision, and on a building with several tenancies it is also the record the next contractor will be checked against.

## What does a pre-war cellar in this city hide?

A hundred years of alterations to services, laid through a wall that was never sealed to begin with.

Glen Cove's older cellars were dug for storage and for coal. Everything else in them arrived later: waste and supply lines, a heating plant that has been replaced several times, electrical distribution, and on a divided house a second set of services for the upper dwelling. Each of those was cut through the wall or the slab by whoever installed it.

For termite work the important features are these.

**The interior face of the wall.** On rubble or early block, tubes travel inside the joints, and the productive places to look are the top of the wall where the sill bears, the corners, behind the plant, around every pipe sleeve, and anywhere stored material has been standing against the masonry for years. Cornell notes termites favour heat from furnaces, chimneys and hot water pipes, particularly in winter, which puts the plant corner at the top of the list.

**The sill and the joist ends.** The sill plate bears directly on the masonry, and the joist ends bear into pockets in it. Both are the point where a route arrives at timber, and both are frequently obscured by later insulation, a suspended ceiling or a finished wall.

**Anything wooden touching the floor.** Old shelving, a workbench, stored timber, a stair stringer bearing on the slab, and the remains of coal-era joinery. Each is a soil-adjacent timber contact inside the building.

**The areaway and the exterior cellar entrance.** Where one exists, the ground level around it is usually higher than anywhere else on that elevation, and the framing around the hatch or door is at grade.

The honest limitation on this stock is access. A cellar that has been finished, partitioned, filled with storage or converted has hidden the very surfaces the survey needs, and a report should say which faces of the wall were seen rather than implying the whole cellar was read.

## Where does the March-to-June flight get noticed in a city?

Indoors, and usually by somebody with no control over the building — which is why the reporting route matters as much as the observation.

Eastern subterranean termites swarm March through June. In a market of detached owner-occupied houses, a swarm is seen by the person who can act on it. Here it is frequently seen by a tenant, by a superintendent, or by a shopkeeper on a downtown ground floor with living space above.

Three things are worth doing at the moment of the sighting, and none of them requires expertise.

**Keep a sample.** Cornell describes discarded wings about a quarter of an inch long, gathered at windows and light fittings where the swarmers collected. A few wings in an envelope, with a note of the room and the date, settles the identification question that otherwise consumes a first visit. Winged ants and winged termites are routinely confused and they lead to completely different work.

**Record where they emerged, not just where they ended up.** Swarmers gather at light. The useful information is the joint, the crack, the wall base or the window frame they came out of.

**Send it upward the same day.** On a shared building the observation is only useful once it reaches whoever can commission a survey of the cellar and the grounds.

The seasonal note that applies specifically here: a swarm in a downtown building with a commercial ground floor is unusually likely to be reported late, because the emergence happens in a service area, a cellar or a rear yard that nobody looks at, and by the time anyone connects it to a complaint upstairs the flight is over.

Winter has diagnostic value for the opposite reason. Activity that persists near a plant room in January says the route has a reliable supply behind it, and on a large building heated from one plant location that concentrates the evidence in a findable place.

## What does the estate fringe put against a foundation?

A century of landscaping, on ground that has never been cleared, a few streets from apartment blocks.

Glen Cove is unusual in holding great estates immediately around a built-up core. Welwyn, the 204-acre former Harold Pratt estate. Morgan Memorial Park, 40 acres of former J.P. Morgan Jr. land. Winfield Hall, the F.W. Woolworth estate. The Braes, now the Webb Institute campus. Killenworth. A resident can walk from a fifty-unit building to closed mature canopy in a few minutes.

Estate ground carries a specific termite inheritance, and it is different from the one a suburban lot carries.

**Buried timber, in quantity.** Cornell Cooperative Extension's advice to remove wooden debris from a property, including buried stumps, is easy to read past. On grounds landscaped continuously for a century, buried stumps, old fence posts, landscape timbers, derelict edging, the remains of cold frames and construction offcuts are genuinely common, and each is a colony site within foraging range of a building.

**Terraces and retaining structures built against the house.** Estate architecture put stone terraces, balustrades, steps and raised planting directly against elevations, which is precisely the condition Cornell's clearance figures are written against.

**Outbuildings with their own soil contact.** Garages, stables, gate lodges, potting sheds, greenhouses and garden stores are frequently on slabs or low walls, frequently untouched for decades, and frequently the first structure on the property to show activity.

**Old drainage.** Leader lines laid generations ago and never opened since do not announce a blockage. They quietly saturate one corner of a building for years.

The survey consequence is that on the estate fringe the perimeter of interest is not the house. It is the holding, and a report that covers the residence and skips the ancillary structures has read a fraction of the property.

## What can a treatment establish beside a channelized creek?

A defined and recorded treated zone, provided the interruptions are named — and beside this creek there are more of them than usual.

Glen Cove Creek was channelized in the early twentieth century by the US Army Corps of Engineers and emerges at the Mill Pond. Dosoris Pond and small marsh areas sit at the northern end of the city between West Island and East Island, with wetlands mainly at Welwyn Preserve and along Long Island Sound. Hempstead Harbor's inner reaches are shallow and its tidal range is approximately seven feet.

Near water, three things complicate a treated zone.

**A high water table limits what soil can hold.** The zone has to be designed for the conditions rather than applied to a standard depth and reported as complete.

**Made ground behaves unpredictably.** Fill, demolition debris and old below-grade fabric mean the material at one corner of a building is not the material at another, and continuity cannot be assumed from a single test.

**The perimeter is interrupted.** Terraces, retaining walls, steps, patios, areaways, attached garages and neighbouring structures all cross the line. On a downtown building the perimeter may not even be continuous property — an attached building's foundation carries on into somebody else's cellar.

The workable position is a scope that lists the interruptions explicitly, states how each was addressed, and names the ones that could not be. Combined with the corrections that are always available above ground — grade reduced to restore clearance, roof drainage taken away from the base of the wall, wooden debris and buried timber removed, crawlspaces kept ventilated — that is a durable arrangement. What is not durable is a perimeter treatment reported as continuous when three terraces and a party wall cross it.

## What should a Glen Cove termite report contain?

The elevations, the access, the interruptions and the restrictions — in that order, and in writing.

For a house: the grade height measured against Cornell's clearance figures on each of the four faces; where each downspout terminates and what the ground does there; every wood-to-soil contact found, with a location precise enough for somebody else to find it; which parts of the cellar or crawlspace wall were visible and which were not; the condition of terraces, steps and retaining walls where they meet the building; and any buried or stacked timber on the lot.

For a shared building, all of that plus: how many tenancies the building holds, who holds the freehold, where the swarm was reported from, which parts of the property were inaccessible on the day and why, and whether the parcel carries land use restrictions.

The re-check asks a short set of questions a year on. Has the landscaping put the grade back up? Has anything new been built against the building — a terrace, a planter, a bin store, a deck? Has any excavation crossed the treated line? And has anything changed on the wet elevation, which on this relief is the only one that was ever likely to.
