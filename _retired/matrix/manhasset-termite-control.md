## Why does a hamlet with a median build year of 1942 have its own termite problem?

Because eastern subterranean termites exploit routes, and old buildings have more routes than new ones.

Manhasset's census area records a median construction year of 1942, and roughly 47 per cent of its housing units date from 1939 or earlier. That is the oldest profile on this part of the North Shore, and it is not a small difference in practice. A house from that period was built before capillary breaks under sill plates were standard, before continuous foundation coatings, before slab construction became the volume method, and before anyone drew a wall assembly with drainage in mind. None of that makes the buildings poor. It makes them permeable in ways the insect can use.

The species itself is undramatic. Colonies live in the soil, workers forage outward from it through covered runs, and they consume wood along the grain while leaving the outer surface intact, so a structural member can lose most of its section and still look sound to the eye. Everything about that biology resists early discovery. The [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile covers the insect in detail; what changes from town to town is the building.

Here the building is usually a detached frame house — around 72 per cent of the stock is detached — sitting on a foundation of rubble, fieldstone, early block or early poured concrete, with a wooden sill plate laid across the top of it. That single junction is the whole game. Everything a termite has to accomplish on a Manhasset house is getting from soil to that sill without drying out on the way, and the older the masonry beneath it, the shorter the trip.

The second Manhasset-specific factor is what has happened to these houses since. A property that has stood for eighty or a hundred years has been altered repeatedly, and every alteration is a chance for somebody to have put wood in contact with ground. That is where most of the activity we would expect to find on this stock actually originates — not in the original construction, but in what was added to it in 1958, 1974 and 1996.

## What does an open-jointed stone foundation do that a poured wall does not?

It carries water upward into the timber and it hides the evidence of the insect that follows the water.

A rubble or fieldstone foundation is a stack of irregular stone with mortar packed into whatever gaps the mason could reach. It was never a monolithic barrier and was never intended to be one. After eight decades of freeze and thaw, a good proportion of the joints have opened, some of them all the way through. On a cold day you can often stand in one of these cellars with the lights off and see daylight at the top of the wall.

Two consequences follow that matter for this particular insect.

The first is moisture. Stone and old mortar are capillary materials. Ground water moves into them from the soil side and travels through them, and there is usually no membrane between the top of that wall and the wooden sill lying on it. So the sill draws moisture out of the masonry continuously, and it does not get a dry season. Timber at that elevated moisture content is exactly what a foraging population is looking for, and it is simultaneously hospitable to the decay fungi that soften it further.

The second is concealment. A shelter tube on a smooth block wall is unmistakable — a raised line of soil cemented to the surface, running up from the floor toward the framing. Cornell Cooperative Extension's homeowner guidance describes these tubes as ranging from about the width of a wheat straw to considerably wider. On rubble, that same tube tucks into a mortar recess and reads as one more irregularity in a wall that is nothing but irregularities. Finding it requires light held close to the wall and raked along it rather than shone at it, and it requires the perimeter to be clear of the stored goods that fill most cellars of this age.

There is a third condition specific to older cellars here: joist pockets. Where floor framing is let into masonry rather than carried on a ledger, the end grain of each joist sits inside a pocket in a damp wall. That end grain is the most absorbent surface on the whole member and the least visible. A survey that reads the sill line and stops has skipped the pockets, and on this housing stock the pockets are frequently where the activity is.

## Why does ground that drains this well still produce termite work?

Because good drainage does not mean no water. It means the water all arrives in the same few places.

Manhasset sits on the Harbor Hill Moraine at around ninety-five feet, on the ridge that forms the major drainage divide in Nassau County. Ground here is coarse glacial material and it sheds rather than ponds. In principle that is a favourable condition — foundations are not standing in saturated soil for weeks, framing dries between wettings, and there is no site-wide dampness of the kind you find on low flat ground.

What actually happens on a hundred-year-old lot is more specific. Water that moves fast has to be going somewhere, and on a property that has been landscaped and re-landscaped since the 1920s, that somewhere is usually a place nobody chose. A bluestone terrace laid in the 1960s sits proud of the original grade and turns a slope back toward the house. A retaining wall built to hold a bed creates a pocket of saturated fill against the foundation. A leader that once discharged into a dry well now discharges onto a paved apron that directs it to a corner. Ninety years of mulch top-ups have raised the bed six inches above the top of the foundation.

The result is a house that is dry on three and a half elevations and permanently wet at one corner. That corner is the highest-probability location on the property, and it is not necessarily the corner anyone would guess. It is found by walking the perimeter during or immediately after rain rather than on a dry afternoon, which is one of the few genuinely useful things a homeowner can do without any equipment at all.

The moraine has one more consequence worth knowing. Because the natural soils are so free draining, an area of persistent dampness usually has a built cause rather than a natural one. On flat, fine ground a wet foundation might just be the water table. Here, a wet foundation is normally something somebody built, and that means it can be un-built.

## What has a century of alteration buried against these houses?

Timber, mostly, and it is the most under-appreciated risk on the older stock.

Cornell Cooperative Extension names the conditions that encourage infestation directly: insufficient clearance between wood and soil, cracks in foundations, leaking pipes and buried wood debris. On a house built in the last thirty years those conditions are exceptions. On a house that has been continuously occupied and modified since the Coolidge administration they are close to routine, because each generation of work left something behind.

The recurring finds on properties of this era, in rough order of frequency:

**Form boards.** When a stoop, a terrace, a garage apron or a set of steps was poured against the house, timber forms were built to hold the concrete. Stripping them from the buried face is awkward, so they were frequently left. That puts sound softwood in permanent soil contact directly against the foundation, under concrete, invisible and unreachable.

**Fill behind walls.** Retaining walls on graded lots were commonly backfilled with whatever was on site during construction, which included offcuts, broken sheathing and old fencing.

**Stumps and roots.** A specimen tree removed in the 1970s left a root system that is still down there. Stumps are a genuine colony site and they are often within a few feet of a foundation because the tree was planted close to the house when it was small.

**Old porch structures.** Where an open porch was enclosed or removed, its posts and their footings sometimes stayed in the ground and got paved over.

**Landscape timber.** Railway sleepers and timber edging installed decades ago to hold a bed or step a slope. Even where the material was treated, treatment does not last indefinitely at the ground line.

None of this is visible on a walk-round, which is why the history of the building matters as much as its current condition. When somebody says the sunroom was built in the early eighties and the terrace went in when the kitchen was done, that is survey information. It tells you which junctions to open up and which side of the house to be sceptical about.

The corrective work here is unglamorous and it is frequently the largest single line on a proposal: dig out the buried timber, drop the grade back off the siding, remove the raised bed built against the wall, redirect the leader. None of it kills an insect. All of it removes the reason the insect was interested, and on this housing stock it usually outperforms anything applied to the soil.

## Where does a Plandome Heights stucco house hide what is happening?

Behind an intact-looking wall, which is the whole difficulty with rendered construction.

Plandome Heights was developed on the former Benjamin Duke estate and retains a group of Spanish-influenced houses — white stucco under red tile. Stucco over frame is a reservoir cladding: it absorbs water and gives it up slowly, and it cannot dry outward at any speed. Where it cracks, and it cracks predictably at changes of plane, at window heads and at parapets, water gets behind it and stays against the sheathing.

For termites, the relevant detail is not the cracking but the bottom edge. Where a rendered finish carries down past the top of the foundation to grade, or below it, the junction between soil and wall is covered. An insect can move up behind the render from soil into the framing with no exposed run and therefore no visible tube. The correct detail is a termination bead with a clear gap above finished grade so the junction can be seen. On a house of this era that detail is often absent, or it was there and has since been buried by a raised bed.

Which means the findings on a stucco house are usually indirect. A baseboard that feels damp on an interior partition. A section of render that sounds hollow when tapped and blisters slightly in a warm month. A hardwood floor with a soft area near an exterior wall. A window casing with a hairline separation that was not there last year. On clapboard you look for the route; here you look for the consequence, and then you work out where the route must be.

The same wall behaves the same way for the other structural insect on this stock, which is why the [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) work described under [ant control in Manhasset](/pest-control/manhasset/ant-control/) so often turns up on the same properties. Damp sheathing is a shared resource.

## How is treatment designed on a graded, planted, ninety-year-old lot?

Around access, and access here is worse than average.

The general options are described under [termite control](/pest-control/termite-control/): establishing a continuous treated zone in the soil around the structure, or installing a monitored in-ground system that works with the colony's own foraging, or a combination matched to the site. What decides which applies is the perimeter, and a long-established Manhasset property has a difficult one.

Consider what is typically wrapped around a house here. A bluestone or brick terrace across the rear, laid on sand or on a slab. A front walk and a poured stoop with steps. A driveway apron running to an attached or detached garage. Foundation planting that has been in place since the war, with root systems occupying the first two feet of soil all round. A dry-laid stone edging that nobody wants disturbed. In Munsey Park, where the Olmsted Brothers' 1927 plan set the streets on the contours and the building standards specified Colonial Revival, the landscape treatment is part of what people bought and they are protective of it.

So the practical question is not which method is theoretically superior. It is: how much of this perimeter can actually be reached, what has to be drilled, what has to be cut back, and what does the owner want left alone. A property with three open elevations and one terraced one may warrant treated soil where the ground is workable and stations where it is not. A property that is paved or terraced most of the way round tilts toward the monitored approach for the straightforward reason that it does not require access to soil that is under concrete.

The honest caveats belong at the proposal stage, not on the morning of the work. Drilling through a terrace to reach the soil beneath it is invasive, dusty and audible, and the patches will be visible. A monitored system is only worth what the servicing schedule is worth; installed and forgotten, it is a row of caps in a lawn. Anyone weighing proposals should read [choosing a pest control contractor](/compliance/choosing-a-pest-control-contractor/) before comparing them on price, because the differences that matter here are in scope and access, not product.

One Manhasset-specific advantage is worth naming. Because Munsey Park's houses were built to a common standard within a few years of each other — 162 of them complete by 1930 — the failure points repeat from house to house on the same streets. Where the surrounding hamlet is heterogeneous, having never been shaped by a single village review board, those blocks are unusually predictable. Scoping is faster and the estimate of what will be found behind a given detail is better founded.

## What does a survey of a pre-war house here actually cover?

The soil relationship, the whole cellar, and the seams where the building changed.

**The grade line, at every elevation.** Where does soil sit relative to the top of the foundation and the bottom of the siding or render? Eight inches of clearance is a different building from one inch. This is measured, not glanced at, and it is measured at the terrace, at the bed, at the walk and at the garage apron, because it is rarely consistent all the way round.

**The full interior face of the cellar wall.** All of it, floor to sill, which means the stored goods come off the perimeter first. On rubble, with a raking light. Joist pockets individually. Pier tops in any crawlspace section. The band joist all the way round.

**Sounding and probing.** Tapping framing with a tool handle and listening for the dull note that means lost section, then testing with an awl where it sounds wrong. Because these insects leave the outer skin of the wood in place, this is the only reliable way to find damage in accessible framing, and it is why a real inspection takes hours rather than twenty minutes.

**The alteration seams.** Where the sunroom meets the original wall. Where the kitchen was pushed out. Where a porch was enclosed and its floor structure became interior framing. Where a bathroom was added over a room never designed to carry plumbing. These junctions have mismatched framing, improvised flashing and voids that were left because closing them would have meant opening more of the house.

**The water.** Gutters, leaders and where they discharge; the crawlspace if there is one; condensate lines; and any plumbing that has been weeping quietly inside a wall. On this stock the water almost always explains the location of the activity, and correcting it is what makes the rest last.

What comes out is a drawing and a written finding: where evidence was found, what kind, what could not be reached and why. What does not come out is proof of absence. A visual and probing survey cannot see inside a closed wall, behind a finished cellar or under a poured stoop, and any report implying otherwise is claiming more than the method supports.

## What does the March-to-June window show on an eighty-year-old house?

A brief event that is easy to miss and worth a great deal once you know how to read where it happened.

Reproductives fly here between March and June, usually in daylight and often on a mild day after rain. They are dark, roughly rice-grain sized, with two pairs of wings of equal length that are noticeably longer than the body, and they shed those wings within minutes. What people find is not insects but a scatter of small translucent wings on a sill, in a window well, or caught in a cobweb in a corner of a cellar.

The location of the emergence is the diagnostic, and this housing stock adds a wrinkle that catches people out. In much of the pre-1940 frame construction here, the wall cavity is continuous from the sill plate to the roof — there is no blocking at each floor of the kind later framing methods introduced. So reproductives released into a wall at sill level do not emerge at sill level. They travel up the cavity toward light and appear at a second-floor window, at a light fitting, or through a gap at the top of a casing. An upstairs finding on a house of this era says nothing about where the colony reached the building. It usually says the cavity is open, which is separately useful to know.

Emergence from a stump, a landscape timber, or open soil at the end of the garden tells you there is a colony on the property. Emergence from inside — a cellar post, a crack at a hearth, a baseboard, a window frame — tells you the colony is in contact with the structure, which is a different conversation.

The other thing the spring window does is create confusion, because carpenter ants resume activity across the same weeks and also produce winged forms. The distinction is quick once you have looked at one of each: a winged ant has a pinched waist, elbowed antennae, and forewings longer than the hind pair. Keeping a few specimens in a sealed bag, or taped to a card, settles it in seconds and costs nothing.

## What makes a Manhasset termite job come back?

Four things, and the product is not usually among them.

**The moisture fault was never corrected.** The activity was addressed and the terrace still turns water back at the same corner. That building remains the most attractive target on the street, decay fungi are still working, and the conditions now also suit carpenter ants. This is the failure that produces what looks like a different problem two years later.

**Something buried was left buried.** Form boards under a stoop, a stump beside the foundation, sleepers holding a bed. These are colony-supporting resources within feet of the house, and treating the structure while leaving them in place is treating the symptom in the most literal sense.

**The perimeter got disturbed afterwards.** A treated zone works while it is continuous. An irrigation trench, a new fence post, a French drain retrofitted the following summer, or a bed dug over and replanted all remove treated soil, and nobody thinks to mention it.

**Nobody looked again.** This insect resists discovery by design: the colony is underground, the workers stay enclosed, the damaged wood looks intact, and the one visible annual event lasts a few hours in the middle of a working day. Re-inspection is not an upsell on this housing stock. It is the only mechanism by which a problem gets found in year three rather than year nine, and on framing that has already carried a house for eighty years, that difference is structural.

Sealing work does not stop termites — they do not need a hole — but the same survey that finds their routes finds everything else's, which is why this job and [structural exclusion in Manhasset](/pest-control/manhasset/structural-exclusion/) are usually specified together.
