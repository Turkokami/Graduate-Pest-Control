## What does a colony use for water in a hamlet that has none?

Whatever the property provides — and on this ground that is a short, specific list that belongs to somebody.

Commack's twelve square miles hold no harbour, no creek, no pond and no shoreline. Beneath the hamlet sits the Upper Glacial aquifer, part of the sole source system serving Nassau and Suffolk, and precipitation averaging around 44 inches a year infiltrates through hundreds of feet of closely packed sand, gravel and pebbles to reach it. On undisturbed soil that means water leaves the surface quickly.

Sixty years of construction has interrupted that in thousands of small ways. Driveways, patios, pool decks, walks, extensions, garage aprons and construction fill sit on top of a drainage path that used to work, and water that once soaked away now runs to the lowest point of a paved surface and stops. The lowest point of a paved surface is very often against a foundation wall.

Eastern subterranean termites need soil contact and moisture. In a village on a tidal creek those are given conditions; you manage around them. Here they are made conditions, and that reframes the entire job. When a Commack wall is wet, it is wet because of a downspout terminating two feet from a corner, an irrigation head watering the siding, an air conditioning condensate line dripping onto a border, a patio pitching back toward the house, a border built four inches higher each decade, or — on an unsewered property — a lateral run keeping a strip of lawn soft in August.

Every one of those has an owner and a fix. That is the good news and it is also the standard this work should be held to. On this ground a treatment applied without correcting the moisture path has removed the insects from a building that will produce the same conditions again, and it will do so on the same elevation.

Roughly 74 per cent of Suffolk County remains unsewered, with about 360,000 cesspools and septic systems countywide. Where a Commack property has one, the buried structures — tank or ring, risers, covers, distribution box, leaching field — are the same age as the house and worth locating before anyone scopes a perimeter. A settled cover holds water. A failing field keeps ground damp on a schedule nothing else on the property explains. Neither is a pest contractor's work to repair, and both belong in the report.

## Why does 1964 construction behave differently from 1954 construction?

Because the details changed, and Commack sits on the later side of that change.

Most post-war Long Island markets have a median build year in the 1950s. Commack's is 1964, with 44.1 per cent of its roughly 11,878 units raised in the 1960s alone and 28.2 per cent in the 1950s — 72.3 per cent of the hamlet in two decades. That decade of difference is not trivia. It shows up in three ways that matter to a termite survey.

**More split-levels and raised ranches.** Both stack a floor structure against a foundation at more than one height, and both put habitable space over a garage or a partly buried lower level. The result is more linear feet of wood-to-masonry junction per house, at more than one elevation above the soil, some of it much closer to grade than a conventional ranch would put it.

**More slab-on-grade.** Garages, breezeways, rear wings and later additions on this stock sit on slabs poured separately from the main foundation. Every one of those creates a joint between two pours, and a joint between two pours is a path from soil to whatever the slab supports. The expansion joint at a garage-to-house junction is the single most commonly missed item on this housing type.

**More services, run in bundles.** A 1964 house was built for more circuits, more plumbing and, soon after, central air conditioning. Sleeves through a foundation wall and penetrations at the sill were cut in greater numbers, and each one is a break in the continuity of whatever protection sits around the building.

Set against those, the era gives something back. A poured concrete foundation has no open mortar joints to travel through, unlike the rubble and early block in the pre-war North Shore villages. Cracks in a poured wall are discrete, findable and closable. The wall is usually accessible from inside where the basement is unfinished. And because the stock is so uniform, the survey order can be established before the van door opens.

That is the trade this hamlet makes: fewer inherent weaknesses in the wall, more junctions and penetrations around it.

## Where does a split-level put wood against soil?

At the step, at the garage joint, and at the lower-level border that nobody thinks of as being near the house.

Cornell Cooperative Extension's guidance is specific and it maps directly onto this stock. Wood sitting close to soil, or touching it, counts as susceptible. Three inches is the minimum clearance asked for between wood siding or skirting and soil, with eight inches of clean concrete where skirting meets the ground. A wood girder is asked to keep an inch of free air on either side and at its bearing end. Wooden debris, including buried stumps, should come off the property, and downspouts should be directed farther from the house.

On a split-level or raised ranch, the clearance question has to be asked several times per building rather than once.

**At the lower level.** Half a storey below the main floor, the siding starts closer to the ground than it does anywhere on a ranch. A border built up against that elevation over six decades — mulched every spring, edged with timber, planted with shrubs that have doubled in width — will have closed three inches of clearance without anybody making a decision.

**At the step in the foundation.** Where the foundation steps up to carry the higher floor level, there is a short return wall and a corner. Grade tends to be highest at exactly that point, because it is where the landscaping was used to disguise the change in level.

**At the garage slab joint.** The garage floor and the house foundation are separate pours with a joint between them. On many Commack split-levels there is a timber floor structure directly above that joint. A route up through the joint arrives inside the building without appearing anywhere on the exterior.

**At the deck.** Deck posts set straight into the ground, and deck ledgers bolted to the band joist, are two separate problems in the same structure. The posts are wood in soil by design. The ledger is a wet interface on the wall, and the framing behind it is what carpenter ants use as well — which is why the same repair serves two services and is covered from the insect side at [ant control](/pest-control/ant-control/) and in the [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) profile.

**At the porch and step.** Concrete steps poured against a house and later re-faced, and porch structures with a void beneath them, both hide a soil-to-timber contact under something that looks solid.

## What did thirty farms leave in the ground?

Not much that a modern survey can rely on, and everything a colony needs.

About thirty farms worked this ground before the subdivisions, growing cabbage, potatoes, corn, broccoli and cucumbers, with pickles a major crop in the 1920s. Farm ground carries a specific inheritance: hedgerow stumps, fence posts, buried timber, the remains of outbuildings, old field drainage, and the debris of demolition when a farmstead was cleared for houses.

Then the subdivisions added their own. Construction on a fast build-out is not tidy. Offcuts, form boards, stakes, packaging and stumps get buried on site because burying them is quicker than removing them, and eleven thousand houses in twelve years is a great deal of burying. Sixty years later, that timber is exactly what Cornell means by wooden debris including buried stumps — a colony site inside foraging range of the building, which no exterior inspection will ever see.

There is a specific local reminder of what the pre-suburban fabric looked like. The Marion Carll Farm, whose 1860 farmhouse and outbuildings — a privy, garage, smokehouse, milk house, horse barn and carriage house, sheep barn and four smaller barns — was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1979 and subsequently fell into disrepair. The Harned Brothers Sawmill of the 1840s and the Carll S. Burr Mansion of about 1830 survive in the same way, as pre-subdivision fabric inside a post-war suburb. Those are landmarks, not typical properties, but they are a useful picture of the scale of timber that was standing on this ground before the houses arrived.

The practical points for a survey are narrower than the history:

Old field boundaries frequently became rear property lines, so a hedgerow stump line often runs along the back fence of a whole row of houses. A tree removed decades ago and ground rather than dug leaves a stump below grade. Landscape timbers used for raised borders and edging in the 1970s and 1980s are still in the soil on many lots, often within a few feet of the wall. And a woodpile stacked directly on the ground against the garage is not a colony site so much as a bridge to one.

Removing buried and stacked timber is the least glamorous part of this work and one of the more effective. It does not need a contractor.

## What does the March-to-June flight look like on a street of identical houses?

Synchronised, alarming and mostly uninformative about your own building.

Eastern subterranean termites swarm March through June. On a street where every house is roughly the same age, sits on the same soil, was landscaped in the same decades and drains the same way, a warm still afternoon in April can produce a flight that several neighbours notice at once.

That coincidence is worth explaining because it is routinely misread in both directions. Some owners conclude the street has an outbreak; others conclude that because everyone saw it, nothing is specific to them. Neither is right. A swarm is a colony reproducing, and swarmers seen outdoors near a property tell you a colony is in the vicinity, not that it is in the building. Swarmers emerging indoors — from a basement wall, a floor joint, around a window frame — are a much stronger indication, because they have come from something with a route inside.

Cornell's list of primary evidence is the one to work from. Mud tubes varying in diameter from the size of a wheat straw to wider than a thumb. Discarded wings about a quarter of an inch long, collected at windows and light fittings where the swarmers gathered. Wood that sounds hollow and that a screwdriver enters easily. Detail is in the [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile.

On this housing type, the productive place to look for a tube is the inside face of the poured foundation wall in an unfinished basement — top of the wall, corners, behind the plant, and around any pipe sleeve. Tubes on a poured wall are visible in a way they are not on rubble, which is one of the few genuine advantages of post-war construction.

The counterpart is the finished basement, and a great many Commack basements are finished. Framing, insulation and wallboard against the foundation wall hide the entire interior face, which means the most legible evidence in the building has been covered. On those houses the survey has to work harder on the exterior, on the mechanical room, and behind any access panel, and an honest report says which parts of the wall were never seen.

## Why does the warm corner of the basement stay busy in January?

Because heat is a resource, and on this construction it is concentrated in one place.

Cornell Cooperative Extension notes that termites favour heat from furnaces, chimneys and hot water pipes, particularly in winter. On a house with a full basement and a single plant location — boiler or furnace, hot water cylinder, and the runs leaving both — that observation has a specific address. The utility corner is warmer than the rest of the perimeter, the soil outside it is warmer too, and a route that reaches it does not go quiet when the weather turns.

Three consequences follow for a Commack survey.

**The plant corner is the first interior wall to read, not the last.** It is also, unhelpfully, the corner most likely to be obscured by equipment, shelving, stored material and pipework. Getting a light behind the plant is worth the awkwardness.

**Heated slabs and pipe runs extend the effect outward.** Where a hot water line runs under a slab to a rear addition, or where a furnace flue passes through a chase, the warm ground follows it. Activity found in an addition that has no plant of its own frequently traces back to a service run from the one that does.

**Winter findings are stronger evidence than summer findings.** Activity in a cold perimeter in January means a route with a reliable supply behind it. The same finding in July says considerably less, because in July the whole perimeter is hospitable.

The practical use of this is scheduling. A property with a suspected problem and an unfinished basement is worth a deliberate look in the coldest part of the year, because that is when the difference between a live route and an abandoned one is easiest to see, and because the rest of the perimeter has stopped competing for attention.

## Which town do you file with when the repair becomes construction?

Whichever of the two the property sits in — and the corrective work here crosses that line more often than rodent work does.

Commack straddles the Town of Huntington and the Town of Smithtown, roughly along Townline Road. Two houses on the same street can answer to two different building departments for permits, property standards and code enforcement.

Chemical treatment and monitoring are not permitted activities. Cutting mulch back, moving a woodpile, extending a downspout, repitching a small area of border and improving crawlspace ventilation with existing openings are all maintenance. But the corrections this hamlet actually needs are frequently larger than that:

Replacing a decayed sill plate or a section of band joist. Rebuilding a porch or a set of steps whose void has been holding soil against timber. Lowering grade with a retaining wall so a lower-level elevation gains its clearance back. Cutting or altering foundation vents. Rebuilding a deck whose posts run straight into soil. Repouring a garage apron that has settled toward the house.

Each of those is a construction filing, and the desk depends on the side of Townline Road. Establishing that before the repair is priced is straightforward. Discovering it afterwards costs weeks and sometimes a second quotation. ## What does a treated zone actually reach on this ground?

A continuous treated zone is achievable here more often than in most of the markets nearby — provided the perimeter is genuinely continuous, which on this stock it frequently is not.

The soil helps. Free-draining sand and gravel is workable, predictable and uniform across a lot, and it does not present the boulder-and-clay unpredictability that made the old moraine villages so difficult. The foundation helps too: a poured wall is a defined surface with defined penetrations rather than a mass of open joints.

What interrupts the zone is everything built against the building since.

**Attached slabs.** Patios, walks, garage aprons, porch pads and stoops laid against the foundation break the soil continuity. Treating beneath them requires working through the slab rather than around it, and a proposal that quietly omits them has left a gap the width of the patio.

**Attached structures with their own footing.** Decks, sunrooms, conservatories, screened porches and rear additions each bring their own foundation, their own junction with the original wall, and their own soil line.

**Utilities.** Buried lines, sleeves, irrigation trenching and, on unsewered properties, the wastewater runs are all routes through the treated soil and all places where the zone has to be re-established after any excavation.

**Existing hardscape depth.** Where a border has been built up over sixty years, the soil against the wall may sit well above the original grade line, which changes where the treated zone actually needs to be.

The honest position is this: a defined, recorded treated zone plus corrected drainage and clearance is a durable arrangement on Commack ground. A treatment applied to a perimeter that has three slabs, a deck and an addition crossing it, with no note of where the gaps are, is not, and the gaps will not announce themselves for years.

## What should a report on a 1964 house contain?

The interruptions, not just the treatment — and the clearance figures the property is actually failing.

A report worth having on this stock reads as a set of located items. Where the grade sits against each elevation, measured, against the three-inch and eight-inch clearances rather than described as "acceptable". Where each downspout terminates and what the ground does there. Which slabs, decks and additions cross the perimeter and how each was addressed. The condition of the garage slab joint. Whether the interior face of the foundation wall was visible, and on which elevations it was not. Any wood-to-soil contact found, with a photograph. Any buried or stacked timber on the lot. The location and condition of the on-site wastewater structures where the property has them. And the elevation with the wettest ground, because that is where the next problem will appear.

The re-check a year later asks a shorter set of questions, and on this housing type they are always the same four. Has the mulch gone back up against the siding? Is the downspout still where it was put? Has anything new been built against the house — a deck, a patio, a shed, a raised bed? And has the interior evidence changed anywhere the wall is visible?
