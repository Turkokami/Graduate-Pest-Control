## What does a century of estate landscaping bury against a foundation?

More timber than anybody expects, at exactly the depth a colony can use.

Cornell Cooperative Extension's guidance for this insect reads, on an estate-belt property, like a list of things that have already happened. Wood near soil, or in contact with it, is treated as susceptible. There should be a minimum of three inches of clearance between wood siding or skirting and soil, and eight inches of clean concrete between skirting and soil. Wood girders want an inch of free air space on both sides and at the end. Remove wooden debris from the property, including buried stumps. Increase drainage away from the foundation by directing downspouts farther from the house. Keep basements and crawlspaces well ventilated.

Now set that against grounds that have been landscaped continuously since before the First World War.

**Terraces and retaining structures built against the building.** Estate architecture put stone terraces, balustrades, steps and raised planting directly against elevations, because that is what made a level outdoor room on rolling ground. Every one of those holds wet soil against masonry, and several of them are older than anybody living.

**Landscape timbers and edging.** Timber edging, sleepers, raised bed frames and step risers have been installed and replaced in waves across a century. Most of the earlier ones are still in the soil.

**Buried stumps.** Trees removed decades ago and ground rather than dug leave a mass of timber below grade. On a property with a hundred years of tree work, that is not a hypothetical.

**Fence posts and old structures.** Post lines from field boundaries, kitchen garden frames, cold frames, derelict outbuilding footings and the remains of glasshouse benching.

**Construction offcuts.** Every alteration to every building on the holding buried something.

Each of those is a colony site, and the Northeastern IPM Center's foraging figure gives the geometry: carpenter ants forage as far as 300 feet from the nest, and while subterranean termites work differently, the practical point is the same — on an estate lot, a source can be well away from the building and still reach it.

That reframes the job. On a suburban lot the survey is a perimeter. Here it is a holding, and the most productive hours are frequently spent nowhere near the house.

## Why does the oldest stock here give a colony the easiest access?

Because pre-1940 masonry is a matrix of joints rather than a wall with holes in it.

Locust Valley records a median construction year of 1945 across roughly 1,254 housing units, with about 534 units — 42.6 per cent — built in 1939 or earlier, a further 186 in the 1940s and 178 in the 1950s. The 2000s produced 133 units and the 2010s thirteen. Some 92.6 per cent are detached single-family houses.

What that stock puts at ground level is a hand-cut sill bearing on rubble or early block. The mortar was never a continuous seal, it has had eighty years or more of freeze-thaw cycling to open its joints, and settlement has widened the junction between the two materials.

For termite work that produces four difficulties at once.

**A route arrives at timber directly.** The sill is in contact with the masonry along its whole length, so a tube reaching the top of the wall reaches wood.

**The evidence hides inside the wall.** Cornell describes mud tubes varying in diameter from the size of a wheat straw to wider than a thumb. On a poured concrete wall a tube is a visible object on a flat surface. On rubble it travels inside the joints and may run a considerable distance before emerging. Reading one is covered in the [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile.

**The cladding conceals the timber.** Shingle sheathing over the frame means the condition of the sill, the bottom of the studs and the sheathing behind is not visible from outside without removing material.

**Repairs have been made in unknown materials.** A century of patched pointing, in mortars of varying hardness, produces a wall that holds water in some joints and sheds it from others.

Against that, the age brings one real advantage: cellars on this stock are frequently unfinished, and where the interior face of the wall is visible the survey has somewhere to work. Cornell notes termites favour heat from furnaces, chimneys and hot water pipes, particularly in winter, so the plant corner is the first interior wall to read rather than the last — and it is usually the one most obstructed by equipment, shelving and stored material.

## Why does the stable get termites before the house does?

Because it was built closer to the ground, maintained less, and nobody has looked at its sill in thirty years.

Estate-scale parcels carry detached garages, stables, gate lodges, potting sheds, pool houses, greenhouses, garden stores and boathouses. For rodent work those buildings matter because they hold populations. For termite work they matter for different reasons, and the difference is worth setting out.

**Soil-to-timber contact is frequently by design.** A stable, a potting shed or a garden store may have a timber sill on a low masonry wall, timber posts bearing on pads, or a floor structure very close to grade. Some were built with a stud wall standing on a slab with no capillary break at all.

**They sit lower in the landscape.** Ancillary buildings were placed where they were convenient rather than where the ground was best, which frequently means downhill, in a hollow, or against a bank.

**The ground has risen around them.** A hundred years of leaf litter, mulch and gardening has raised grade against these buildings more than against a residence that gets attention.

**Their drainage was never much.** Where there are gutters they are often original, and where a leader line exists it has probably not been opened since it was laid.

**Their contents are timber.** Stored lumber, fencing, stakes, crates, old furniture and firewood inside an unheated building on or near a slab.

The survey consequence is straightforward: on a Locust Valley holding, the ancillary structures are surveyed as buildings, not glanced at. A report that covers the residence and mentions the outbuildings in a sentence has read a fraction of the property.

The treatment consequence is a decision that has to be made explicitly. Not every structure on a large holding is worth a full treated perimeter, and pretending otherwise inflates a proposal. The honest approach ranks them: which buildings have active evidence, which have conditions that will produce it, which are close enough to the residence to matter, and which are best addressed by removing the timber contact and monitoring rather than treating. That ranking is a conversation, and it should appear in writing.

## What does a shallow impoundment and a tidal creek do to the water table?

They keep the ground wet on one side of the hamlet, which removes the moisture variable on the properties near them.

Beaver Lake is a freshwater impoundment of about 60 acres and roughly three feet deep, draining into Mill Neck Creek through a weir along Cleft Road. Mill Neck Creek is a narrow coastal bay emptying into the western side of Oyster Bay Harbor between Oak Neck and Mill Neck, and the habitat takes in about 120 acres of relatively undisturbed salt marsh and tidal creeks at Oak Neck Creek, bordered by dense residential development on the north and east and by large estates and undeveloped woodland to the west and south. Mill Neck Preserve accounts for much of Oak Neck Creek as undeveloped county parkland, while Beaver Lake is in private ownership.

The terrain around all of it is the rolling hills of the North Shore, formed as terminal moraines by the receding glaciers of the last ice age roughly ten thousand years ago, and the climate is classified humid subtropical — cool wet winters and hot humid summers.

For termite work near that water, three things follow.

**Soil moisture is not a variable you can remove.** On a property near the lake or the creek, the ground stays favourable regardless of what the owner does. The controls that remain are clearance and timber condition rather than drying the soil.

**Grade correction matters more, not less.** Where the moisture is fixed, the three-inch and eight-inch clearances become the primary defence rather than a supplementary one.

**Deterioration runs faster.** Timber at the base of a building on permanently wet ground goes soft sooner, and soft timber is both easier to work and less able to hold a repair. On a shingled house that deterioration is concealed.

Away from the water, on the higher estate ground, the picture is different and better. There the moisture is genuinely local: a blocked leader line, a terrace holding a puddle against a wall, an irrigation zone that has been watering a shingle field for years, a canopy that keeps a north elevation from drying. Every one of those is fixable, and where they are fixed the conditions become marginal.

Establishing which of those two situations a property is in should happen early, because it decides what the work can honestly promise.

## How do you build a treated zone around a house that has grown three times?

By mapping the growth first, because each addition brought its own foundation and its own soil line.

Buildings here have been altered repeatedly. A structure that has been a farmhouse, then a staff cottage, then a family home accumulates seams, and the estate era added wings, service ranges, porches, conservatories and garages to buildings that were already old.

For a treated perimeter that means the line is not a rectangle.

**Each addition has its own foundation,** frequently of a different construction and depth from the original — rubble under the old part, block or poured concrete under the newer.

**Each junction between old and new is a discontinuity.** Two foundations meeting are two materials with a joint between them, and the joint runs from grade downward.

**Attached slabs cross the line.** Terraces, porch pads, garage floors, greenhouse bases and paving laid against the building all interrupt soil continuity, and treating beneath them means working through the slab rather than around it.

**Crawlspaces under later wings are their own environments.** A mid-century wing on a crawlspace, added to a pre-war house with a cellar, has a different ventilation regime, different access and frequently different evidence.

**Buried services cross the perimeter.** Water, gas, electrical, irrigation, pool circulation, and on older holdings abandoned runs nobody has a drawing for.

The workable position is a scope that maps the building's phases, lists every interruption in the perimeter, states how each was addressed, and names the ones that could not be. Combined with the corrections that are always available — clearance restored, roof water taken away from the base of the wall, leader lines opened, buried and stacked timber removed from the grounds, crawlspaces kept ventilated — that is a durable arrangement on this ground.

What is not durable is a perimeter reported as continuous on a building that has three additions, two terraces and a garage crossing it, with no note of where the gaps are. The gaps will not announce themselves for years.

The crawlspaces deserve a separate note, because they are where this stock most often goes wrong quietly. Cornell asks for basements and crawlspaces to be kept well ventilated, and on a property under closed canopy that instruction is doing real work: a crawlspace that has had its vents blocked — to stop draughts, to keep animals out, or because somebody insulated the floor above and thought the vents redundant — becomes a permanently humid chamber with timber in it. Every joist end, every girder bearing and every post base in that space is then sitting in conditions the insect prefers, and none of it is visible from outside the building. Where vents have to keep animals out, they get durable corrosion-resistant screening fitted so it can be inspected, never blocking, and where a crawlspace has been closed the honest recommendation is to reopen it rather than to treat around the consequence.

## Where does the swarm get noticed on a property occupied part of the year?

Late, and frequently in a building nobody uses — which is the reason to look deliberately rather than wait.

Eastern subterranean termites swarm March through June. On an occupied suburban house a flight is noticed the day it happens: swarmers at a window, wings on a sill, a scatter on a floor.

On an estate holding two things interfere with that.

**The flight may happen in an outbuilding.** A stable, a pool house or a garden store is exactly the sort of structure where a colony has conditions, and exactly the sort of structure nobody is standing in on a warm April afternoon. Wings can sit on a windowsill in a potting shed for a year.

**The house may be empty.** Where a property is occupied seasonally or visited weekly, a spring flight can come and go without a witness.

So the practical instruction is a deliberate spring walk of every building on the holding, looking at the windows and the light fittings, because that is where swarmers gather. Cornell describes discarded wings about a quarter of an inch long. A few in an envelope with a note of the building and the date settles the identification question that otherwise consumes a first visit, since winged ants and winged termites lead to entirely different work.

Two further points on evidence here. Swarmers seen outdoors near a building indicate a colony in the vicinity; swarmers emerging indoors indicate a colony with a route into that structure, and on a large holding the difference decides which building gets worked on. And winter has its own diagnostic value: activity that persists near a heat source in January says the route has a reliable supply behind it, which on a property with several buildings is a way of narrowing the search.

## Which building authority governs the repair?

Whichever one the parcel sits in — and the postal address does not answer it.

Locust Valley is an unincorporated hamlet and census-designated place in the Town of Oyster Bay, with no mayor, no village board and no architectural review board of its own. Permits, zoning and code enforcement sit with the Town of Oyster Bay.

But the census place covers only 0.9 square miles, and as the commercial centre with the railway station serving the surrounding Gold Coast communities, the geographically small Locust Valley became the name of reference for all the surrounding areas between Glen Cove and Oyster Bay. That one school district reaches into three incorporated villages, parts of three more, and the unincorporated hamlet itself.

So two properties with the same postal address can sit under two different authorities, and termite work crosses into construction more often than most pest disciplines do:

Replacing a decayed sill plate or a section of framing. Rebuilding a porch, a terrace or a set of steps that has been holding soil against timber. Lowering grade with a retaining wall to restore clearance. Cutting or altering foundation and crawlspace vents. Rebuilding a deck whose posts were set into the ground. Repairing or replacing a structure whose timber has failed.

There is a second consideration on a property inside an incorporated village or on a building of local significance: what may be altered and in what material. Three NRHP properties carry the Locust Valley name — the Cock-Cornelius House, the Matinecock Friends Meetinghouse and the George Underhill House — and the estate belt holds a great deal of locally significant fabric that is not federally listed. Establishing the position before a repair is specified is a short task; discovering it mid-job is not.

## What has to be re-checked, and when?

The corrections, annually, and the answer is almost always that the landscape put something back.

A report worth having on a Locust Valley holding sets out: the buildings on the property and which were surveyed; the grade height measured against Cornell's clearance figures on each elevation of each surveyed building; where roof water goes on every roof, including the ancillary ones; every wood-to-soil contact found, with a location precise enough for a stranger to find it; which foundation faces were visible and which were obscured; the condition of terraces, steps and retaining structures where they meet a building; the buried and stacked timber found on the grounds; and the interruptions in any treated perimeter.

The re-check asks four questions, and on this stock the answers repeat.

**Has the landscaping rebuilt the grade?** A bed cut down to restore clearance gets topped up by whoever maintains the grounds, and on an estate property that is a contractor with no knowledge of the scope. Telling them is more effective than telling the owner.

**Has new timber gone into the ground?** New edging, new steps, a new fence line, a new raised bed, a delivery of firewood stacked against a wall.

**Has anything moved over the winter?** Terraces, steps and retaining walls on rolling ground shift, and a joint that opens against a building is a new path for water.

**Are the leader lines still working?** A blocked leader does not announce itself. It quietly saturates one corner of a building for a decade.
