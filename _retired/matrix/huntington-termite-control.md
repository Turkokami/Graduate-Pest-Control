## Why does termite exposure in Huntington change with elevation?

Because the insect needs moisture and the town supplies it unevenly.

Huntington is not a flat market. The harbour end — Halesite, the streets dropping down New York Avenue toward Mill Dam Road — sits at roughly twenty-six feet above the water. Behind and above it the ground rises onto the glacial ridge that forms this whole stretch of the North Shore, coarse sandy material dumped at the edge of the ice sheet and left there. Water behaves completely differently on those two grounds, and eastern subterranean termites are governed by water.

On the ridge, rain moves down through the sand and gravel fast. A foundation there is rarely sitting in saturated ground for long, framing dries between wettings, and the failure points that concentrate activity — a chronically damp corner, a crawlspace that never dries, a sill wicking off wet masonry — are simply fewer. That does not mean the colony is absent. It means the building is a harder target and the activity, when it appears, is tied to something specific: a downspout discharging at the wall, an irrigation head aimed at the foundation, a buried stump from a tree taken out thirty years ago.

Down at the harbour the arithmetic reverses. There is less distance between a footing and the water table. Crawlspaces ventilate into air that has come off Huntington Harbor already carrying its moisture, so opening more vents imports damp rather than removing it. Framing at that end of town runs at a higher moisture content for more of the year, which does two things at once: it keeps decay fungi working, and it keeps the humidity that foraging workers require. Workers have thin cuticles and die quickly in dry open air, which is the entire reason they build shelter tubes. A damp lower storey lets them operate with less construction and less exposure.

So the same species produces two different jobs within three miles. On a Halesite property the question is where the water is and how long the underside of the building stays wet. On a West Hills property it is where the single wood-to-soil contact is, because there is usually one and it is usually deliberate — a deck post, a landscape timber, a porch pour, a raised bed built against the wall. Cornell Cooperative Extension's homeowner guidance lists insufficient clearance between wood and soil, foundation cracks, leaking pipes and buried wood debris as the conditions that encourage infestation. Huntington's two halves supply them in different proportions, and the survey has to know which half it is standing on.

## What does a median build year of 1956 do to the risk list?

It puts most of the town's houses in a construction window that concealed the routes rather than removing them.

The Huntington CDP's median construction year is 1956. Roughly a fifth of the housing dates from 1939 or earlier and about a third went up in the 1950s alone, which describes an old harbour village wrapped in a very large post-war ring. Around four fifths of the stock is detached single-family. That ring — Huntington Station, South Huntington, the subdivisions off Jericho Turnpike and the streets filled in behind the older hamlets — is where the volume is, and it is built the way the 1950s built: slab-on-grade or a shallow crawlspace, an attached garage, a low sill, and utility penetrations sleeved through the foundation because a sleeve was required and left unsealed because a seal was not.

Each of those has a termite consequence that a pre-war house does not have.

**The sill sits close to grade.** On a 1950s ranch the timber may have started eight inches above soil. Six decades of mulch top-ups, regrading for a patio, a raised bed put in around a foundation planting and the general upward creep of a landscape have often reduced that to two inches, or removed it. Once soil touches siding, the insect does not have to build anything at all. It simply moves from ground into wood, and nothing about that route is visible from the garden.

**The slab conceals the evidence.** In a cellar you can read the interior face of a foundation wall and the sill line above it. A slab house offers no such surface. The routes are inside the concrete — expansion joints, the cold joint where the garage slab meets the house slab, plumbing and conduit penetrations, and the settlement cracking that every slab of that age has somewhere. Activity travels up through those into a bottom plate, and the first external sign is often a swarmer emerging along a baseboard or a bathroom that has a soft spot in the floor.

**The attached garage is a blind spot.** It has a slab, a step down from the house, a sill line hidden behind stored goods on three walls, and it is where the utilities gather. It is also the room nobody inspects because nobody thinks of it as part of the house.

**Additions built in the 1970s and 1980s multiply the joints.** Huntington's post-war stock has been extended relentlessly — a family room off the back, a dormer, a sunroom over a former patio. Every one of those meets the original structure at a junction where framing does not line up, where a ledger was bolted to a rim joist, and where the new slab was poured against the old foundation. Those junctions are the most reliable place to find activity on a house from this era.

## Where do termites enter the village's pre-1940 houses?

At the foundation, and at the seams between everything that has been done to the building since.

The oldest fabric in Huntington predates any modern framing convention. The town was settled in 1653; the Old Town Green and Old Town Hall historic districts, both listed on the National Register in 1985, hold colonial and nineteenth-century buildings around Park Avenue, West Main Street and Stewart Avenue. The 1816 Whitman farmhouse out at West Hills is cedar shingle over hand-framed timber with no sheathing membrane behind it. Buildings framed that way are not defective. They are simply built to a different logic, and it is a logic with generous cavities in it.

Underneath them are rubble, fieldstone or early block foundations. A fieldstone wall is a stack of irregular stone with mortar filling what it can, and after a century of freeze-thaw those joints have opened. That matters for termites in two specific ways. First, a mud tube on rubble is genuinely hard to see — it follows the mortar line and reads as a slightly proud ridge of dried soil rather than the obvious pencil-wide tube people picture. Second, the sill plate frequently bears directly on the stone with no capillary break between them, so it wicks moisture out of the masonry indefinitely. A permanently damp sill on a wall with open joints is close to an ideal target.

Then there are the alterations. A house that has stood here since the nineteenth century has had porches enclosed, kitchens pushed out, bathrooms added over former sculleries and stone steps replaced with a concrete stoop poured hard against the wall. That last one deserves its own mention, because a poured stoop or porch against an older house very often contains fill, sometimes contains the remains of the wooden forms, and hides the wall behind it entirely. It is a standing finding on village properties and it cannot be assessed without either drilling or demolition.

The practical result is that inspection of the old stock is an archaeology exercise before it is a pest exercise. You work out what was built when, where the additions meet, and where somebody buried something. The [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile covers the biology, our general approach to the insect is set out under [termite control](/pest-control/termite-control/), and the village houses supply the geometry.

## How is a post-war slab house actually treated here?

By reaching the soil beneath concrete that was never designed to be reached.

On a house with a cellar, a continuous treated zone in the soil is largely trench-and-rod work along the foundation, with drilling only where a patio, walk or porch caps the ground. On the 1950s Huntington ranch or split there may be no accessible soil against a substantial fraction of the perimeter: the slab runs to the foundation edge, an attached garage covers one whole side, a patio was poured across the back in the 1970s, and a walk wraps the front. Treating the soil in those conditions means drilling through the concrete at intervals, treating below it, and patching. It is invasive, it is noisy, and it is honest to say so at the proposal stage rather than on the morning.

The alternative approach is a baiting system: in-ground stations installed around the structure at intervals, containing material that foraging workers take back through the colony. On a Huntington slab property the argument for it is straightforward. It does not require access to soil that is under concrete, it uses very little material, and it works with the colony's own foraging behaviour rather than against the building's construction. The argument against it is time and discipline — workers have to find the stations, feed and transfer, and the stations have to be serviced on a schedule. A bait system installed and then forgotten has not been used, it has been sold.

In practice the two are not rivals. A property with a readable perimeter on three sides and a concrete apron on the fourth is a candidate for a treated zone where the soil is open and stations where it is not. What decides it is the site: how much of the perimeter is accessible, where the water goes, whether there is a well on the property, how close the drainage runs, and what is under the slab.

Alongside either approach sits the physical correction, and this is the part that is skipped most often. Lowering grade back off the siding. Pulling mulch and raised beds away from the wall. Getting rid of the buried form boards and the stump nobody removed. Extending a downspout so it stops discharging into the same foundation corner where the tubes were found. Replacing framing that has lost section. None of that kills anything. All of it changes how attractive the structure is to the next colony, and on a post-war Huntington property it is usually cheaper than the treatment.

## What does the aquifer under Huntington change about this work?

It changes the care taken with everything that goes into the ground.

All of Long Island's drinking water comes from the aquifer system beneath it. There is no reservoir upstate feeding Huntington; the water in the tap came out of the ground under the town. That single fact is why Nassau and Suffolk are treated differently from the rest of New York State in what may be applied to soil and under what conditions, and why a soil application here is a decision about a site rather than a standard procedure.

For a homeowner the practical consequences are specific. Where a property has its own well, setbacks apply and they are not negotiable. Where a lot drains toward a watercourse, a wetland at the head of the harbour, or a leaching structure, the placement of any treatment has to account for it. Where the soil is the coarse, fast-draining material of the ridge, the behaviour of anything applied to it differs from its behaviour in the finer, wetter ground down at the harbour edge. These are not marketing distinctions. They are the reasons two houses a mile apart can warrant different approaches to the same insect.

There is a second consequence that is easy to miss: it strengthens the case for the building work. Correcting grade, removing wood-to-soil contact and fixing drainage reduce termite pressure without adding anything to the ground at all. On a property where application is constrained, the physical corrections stop being the optional half of the proposal and become the main event.

Applications of this kind are governed by product labels and by state regulation, and by who is licensed to carry them out. Anyone commissioning the work should understand that framework before signing, and [New York State pesticide applicator requirements](/compliance/nys-pesticide-applicator-requirements/) sets it out. On a residential lot in a town of this density, [the New York neighbor notification law](/compliance/ny-neighbor-notification-law/) is also worth reading, because both Long Island counties adopted it.

## What does the swarm look like in this town, and what should you do?

It looks like a small, brief, easily-missed event that tells you something serious.

Eastern subterranean termites swarm here between March and June, in daylight, and often on a warm day after rain. The swarmers are dark, roughly the size of a grain of rice with wings noticeably longer than the body, and they fly badly. Indoors they head for light, which is why they turn up at windows, at cellar window wells, at a sliding door, or at a bathroom window on a slab house. Then they shed their wings almost immediately, so what is usually found is not insects but a scatter of small pale translucent wings on a sill or caught in a cobweb in a corner.

Where they emerge is the diagnostic. Swarmers coming out of a stump, a landscape timber or the soil at the far end of the garden tell you there is a colony on the property. Swarmers coming out of a crack in a slab, from a baseboard, from around a cellar post or from a window frame came out of your building, which means the colony is in contact with the structure. In a town where a third of the housing is on slab or shallow crawlspace, that indoor emergence is frequently the first and only notice anyone gets.

What to do is short. Do not vacuum everything away — keep a few in a sealed bag or taped to a card, and photograph where they were. Note the date and the room. Do not spray them; killing a few hundred reproductives that were mostly going to die anyway changes nothing about the colony and destroys the specimen. Then get the structure looked at, because a colony producing winged reproductives has been growing for several years.

The Huntington-specific note: the swarm window overlaps almost exactly with the period when carpenter ants resume activity, and the two get confused constantly. A winged ant has a pinched waist, elbowed antennae and forewings longer than the hind pair. If the answer is ants, the job is a completely different one and it is described under [ant control in Huntington](/pest-control/huntington/ant-control/).

## What does a Huntington termite survey actually cover?

The ground, the perimeter, whatever is under the house, and the places the building's own history says to look.

**Outside**, the full foundation line with the grade relationship checked at every elevation: where does soil sit relative to the top of the foundation and the bottom of the siding? Porch and stoop junctions, deck posts and ledgers, the garage slab edge, hose bibs, condensate discharge, downspout outlets, and any timber in ground contact. On a harbour-side lot, particular attention to the elevation that faces the water, because wind-driven rain there wets the back of the cladding rather than the face and the damage shows late.

**Inside**, in a cellar or crawlspace: the whole interior face of the wall, the sill and band joist all the way round, pier tops, joist pockets where framing enters masonry, and the underside of the floor system. Stored goods have to come off the perimeter walls for that to mean anything, which is worth doing the day before rather than during. In a slab house the equivalent work is the perimeter of every room at floor level, the garage step-down, and the plumbing penetrations in bathrooms and the kitchen.

**By hand.** Sounding framing with a tool handle and listening for the dull note that means lost section, then probing where it sounds wrong. Subterranean termites eat along the grain and leave a thin intact surface, so damaged framing looks sound and gives way under an awl. This is the part that separates a survey from a walk-round, and it is why a proper inspection is slow.

**And the water.** Where it comes from, where it goes, and where it stops. Gutters, leaders, grade, crawlspace condition, the vapour barrier if there is one, condensate lines, and any plumbing that has been weeping quietly behind a finished wall. On this town's housing stock the water almost always explains the location of the activity.

What comes out of it is a diagram and a written finding: where activity was found, what it was, what could not be reached and why. What it cannot produce is proof of absence. No visual survey sees inside a closed wall or under a slab, and a report that implies otherwise is claiming more than the method allows.

## Why does termite work fail on Huntington properties?

Four reasons, and none of them is the product.

**The treated zone gets broken afterwards.** A continuous zone in the soil only works while it is continuous. New landscaping, a trench for an irrigation line, a fence post dug in, a patio extension, a French drain retrofitted two summers later — each of those removes or disturbs treated soil, and nobody tells the pest company. On the post-war ring, where garden projects are constant, this is the commonest cause of a return.

**The bait stations stop being serviced.** A system that is not monitored is a row of plastic caps in a lawn. The value is entirely in the schedule.

**The moisture was never corrected.** The colony was reduced, the tubes were knocked down, and the crawlspace at Halesite still runs at the same humidity it always did. That building is still a good target, decay fungi are still softening the framing, and the same conditions that suited termites now suit [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) as well. This is the failure that produces a second problem two years later that the owner does not connect to the first.

**Nobody looked again.** Termite damage on this housing stock is routinely found during a renovation or a sale rather than during ownership, because everything about the insect resists early discovery: the colony is underground, the workers stay enclosed, the wood looks fine, and the one visible annual event lasts a few hours while everyone is at work. Annual re-inspection is not an upsell. It is the only way this particular problem gets caught in year three rather than year eight, and the difference between those two is measured in framing.
