## Where does a colony find water 217 feet above the harbour?

On the property, in a handful of places, all of which somebody installed.

This is the part of the Town of Huntington that sits up off the water. The census area holds no harbour, no tidal creek and no shoreline, and the ground stands at about 217 feet, so the tidal humidity that drives so much of the wood-destroying insect work on the town's northern edge simply is not a factor. Rain falls, and on undisturbed outwash and moraine ground it goes down rather than across.

What has interrupted that is a century and a half of building. Roofs, drives, aprons, walks, patios, parking bays, extensions and made ground have all been laid over a drainage path that used to function, so water that once soaked away now travels to the low point of a hard surface and sits there. On a suburban lot the lowest point of a hard surface is very often against a foundation.

So a wet wall here is a diagnosis rather than a weather report, and the shortlist is always the same.

**A leader terminating at a corner.** The commonest single finding on the post-war streets, and the cheapest to correct.

**A border that has grown upward.** Mulched every spring since about 1960, edged with timber at some point in the 1980s, planted with shrubs that have doubled in width. Nobody decided to bury the bottom of the siding; it happened four inches at a time.

**A condensate line on a bed.** Central air came to this stock as a retrofit, and the drain from it was put where it was convenient.

**An irrigation head on the wall.** Watering the same section of cladding four mornings a week for a decade.

**A private drain run.** On the unsewered blocks, a lateral, a distribution box or a leaching field that keeps a strip of ground soft in August.

All of them have an owner and all of them can be put right. That is the standard the work here should be held to, and it is why a treatment applied without correcting the path has removed insects from a building that will manufacture the same conditions again on the same elevation.

## Why does one median build year hide four different problems?

Because the median is 1961 and almost nothing here was actually built in 1961.

The stock spreads: roughly 1,318 units from before 1940, about 1,173 from the 1940s, some 3,039 from the 1950s and 2,365 from the 1960s, inside a total of around 11,414. Add the 1,316 attached units, the thousand-odd in two-to-four-unit buildings, the 766 in buildings of five or more, and the recent podium construction, and you have four distinct construction technologies sharing a street grid.

For a wood-destroying insect survey that means four separate inspection routines rather than one.

**Pre-1940.** Rubble, fieldstone or early block below grade. Hand-cut sills bearing directly on masonry with nothing between the two, so the timber is already damp before an insect is involved. Joist ends pocketed into masonry, which is the wettest, darkest, least ventilated position in the whole building. On this construction the wall itself is permeable, so the classic question of where a route crosses an exposed surface has a different answer: it does not always have to.

**Post-war tract.** Poured concrete foundations, which is a genuine advantage — a poured wall has discrete, findable, closable cracks rather than a network of open joints. Against that, more penetrations, more attached slabs, and a grade line that has been climbing for six decades.

**Small conversions.** Almost always a pre-war or early post-war house with additional plumbing cut through it. The relevance here is water: a second bathroom stacked over a bedroom, a kitchen added to a floor that never had one, and drainage improvised through a floor structure. A slow leak inside a converted wall is a moisture source nobody owns.

**New slab construction.** Discussed below, and different again.

The practical instruction is to establish the building's era and its alteration history before walking it. On this hamlet's streets you cannot assume the house next door is the same problem.

## What does an old masonry foundation do that a poured wall does not?

It offers travel space inside itself, which changes both where you look and what a repair means.

On a poured wall, the interior face in an unfinished cellar is the most legible surface in the building. Against smooth grey concrete a mud tube climbing from soil level toward the timber above shows clearly, and the field test takes seconds: knock out a short length, leave the remainder standing, and return in a few days to see whether it has been repaired.

A rubble or fieldstone wall gives you none of that. The face is irregular, the mortar is recessed and crumbling in places, and a route can run inside the wall between stones for several feet without ever presenting itself on the surface. Where the mortar has failed at the top of the wall, the arrival point is directly under the sill, which is exactly where you least want it and hardest to see. On early block, the cores are hollow and continuous, so activity can travel up the inside of the wall with nothing visible at all.

That has three consequences on the older streets here.

The survey is tactile rather than visual. Sounding the sill and the band joist along their length, and probing where the note goes dull, finds what looking will not.

The evidence is often above the wall rather than on it. Blistered or dimpled paint on trim, a faint wandering line under a finish, a section of skirting that gives under an awl, a scatter of small uniform wings on a cellar window well in April.

And the repair is masonry work. Repointing failed joints and closing the head of the wall under the sill is part of the corrective scope, not an optional extra, because until the wall is a wall the moisture and the route both persist. The same masonry work does most of the [structural exclusion in Huntington Station](/pest-control/huntington-station/structural-exclusion/) job on those buildings, which is one of the few places on this stock where two services genuinely share a line item.

## Where does new construction meet soil?

At the slab edge, at the joints through it, and — increasingly — in planters that hold real soil over structure.

The recent buildings are not incidental here. Avalon Bay put up 303 apartments completed in 2013. Gateway Plaza carries 66 apartments over a commercial floor of 16,000 square feet. Northridge has
      16 above 6,500 square feet of retail. Country Pointe put up 76 townhomes, Columbia Terrace
      14 condominiums. That is a meaningful quantity of new fabric dropped into an old hamlet inside about a decade.

Concrete is not food and a modern building is not a soft target. But every one of these structures presents the same small set of soil-to-structure interfaces.

**The slab edge and the perimeter joint** where a floor slab meets a foundation wall or a separately poured element.

**Expansion and cold joints** running through slabs at parking level and at grade.

**Cored penetrations** for water, sprinkler, gas, electrical and communications, sleeved and rarely sealed against anything smaller than a rodent.

**Landscaped terraces and planters.** A planter over structure is a container of permanently irrigated soil sitting against a building, often with timber edging, decking or a bench detail in contact with it. It is the one place on a modern building where soil and wood meet by design.

**The fit-out.** The concrete frame is not the vulnerable part; the timber studwork, the joinery, the door frames and the flooring installed inside it are. Where a route through a joint reaches the fit-out, the finding looks exactly like a finding in a house.

The people who need to know this are the managing agents rather than the residents, because the inspection has to include the parking level, the plant rooms, the planter details and the tenancy fit-outs on the ground floor. That sits inside the building-wide model described under [property management pest control](/commercial/property-management-pest-control/).

## What does an unsewered block put into the soil?

A reliable damp strip, in a hamlet that otherwise has to manufacture its moisture.

The southern portion of the downtown is not sewered. A county approval of $1.25 million is funding engineering and design for a comprehensive sewer
      plan covering the blocks below the station, with construction itself estimated at $30 to $40
      million. Until that is delivered, a dense commercial and residential core runs on on-site systems and private laterals.

For rodent work that is a route question. For termite work it is a water question, and a more interesting one.

A functioning leaching field discharges below grade continuously. A lateral run in poor repair does the same in a narrower line. Either produces a strip of ground that stays soft when the rest of the lot has dried out, and reliable soil moisture within foraging distance of a building is the condition that supports a colony through a dry August. Among the conditions Cornell Cooperative Extension names as sustaining the soil moisture a colony needs are leaking pipes and dripping faucets. A failing lateral is that same fault at a larger scale and further out from the building.

The instruction is short. Where a property runs an on-site system, locate it before anybody scopes a perimeter. Walk the run between the building and the field. Note where the ground is soft, where a cover has settled below grade, and where the lawn is green in a line. Then record it and say who should look at it, because that repair is a drainage trade's work and pretending otherwise wastes the owner's money.

There is a second-order point for the blocks awaiting sewers. When that scheme is built, it will involve substantial excavation across the densest part of the hamlet. Excavation moves soil, breaks treated ground and changes drainage. Anyone treating a perimeter down there in the next few years should know the scheme exists and should note on the report where the treated zone would be interrupted by it.

## What does the April flight tell you, and what does it not?

That a mature colony is nearby. Where it emerged decides everything else.

Eastern subterranean termites swarm from March through June. On a street where the houses are of different ages but the soil, the weather and the landscaping habits are shared, a warm still afternoon in April will produce flights on several properties at once. That coincidence gets read in both wrong directions — either the street has an outbreak, or nothing seen by everybody can be specific to anyone.

The distinction that matters is location. Emergence in the open — from a stump, a length of landscape timber, a fence line or bare ground —
      places a colony on or beside the parcel. Emergence inside the house, through a slab crack,
      at a skirting, round a window frame or at the base of a cellar post, places it in contact
      with the structure, since the insects came out of the fabric itself.

Two things about this hamlet complicate the reading.

The first is that a swarm is very often unobserved. Flights are brief and frequently happen on a weekday afternoon. What is generally left is wing litter: dozens of identical translucent wings, each about a
      quarter-inch, along a sill, snagged in a corner web, or settled in a cellar window well. In a rented unit or a converted house, that scatter is regularly swept up by whoever cleans before anybody with a reason to care sees it.

The second is that a swarm in a multi-unit building arrives as a complaint from one apartment about a problem in a structure. The unit is not the boundary. Wing scatter in a ground-floor flat in a converted house is a finding about the whole building and belongs in a survey of the whole building.

If you find them, keep some. A few taped to a card or dropped in a sealed bag is the most useful thing you can hand over, and the identification against a carpenter ant alate — pinched waist, elbowed antennae, unequal wings — is set out in the [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile.

## Why is the cellar the least-inspected room in the hamlet?

Because in a large share of these buildings nobody with a reason to look has been down there in years.

Damage of this kind is generally discovered during a renovation or a sale rather than by anybody setting out to find it, and this hamlet piles several extra reasons on top of that.

**Tenure.** Around 2,394 units are rented. A tenant with no access to a cellar does not inspect it, and an owner who does not live in the building visits for a reason rather than on a schedule. The room where the sill line, the band joist and the interior face of the foundation all present themselves is the room nobody enters.

**Conversion.** In a two-to-four-unit building the cellar is frequently shared service space holding the boilers, the meters and the stored possessions of whoever has been there longest. Boxes and furniture stacked against a perimeter wall put the most legible surface in the building behind a barricade, and moving them is a precondition of an inspection meaning anything.

**Finished basements.** Framing, insulation and wallboard against a foundation cover the sill line from both sides. That leaves the plant room, any access hatch, the space beneath the stair and the outside face, and the report should name the elevations nobody saw rather than reading as a clean bill.

**Deferred maintenance.** The corridor went through decades in which very little was spent on the fabric. Buildings that stood through that carry the consequences below grade as much as above it — a cracked apron, a failed lateral, a cellar window well that has been holding soil against a frame since the 1970s.

What that adds up to is a practical instruction rather than a lament. On any building here where the cellar is shared, let or finished, the inspection has to be arranged rather than attempted: access agreed in advance, stored material moved off the perimeter walls beforehand, and the plant room opened. Half an hour of preparation converts a walk-through into an inspection.

The best month for it is a cold one. Warmth is a resource, and Cornell Cooperative Extension records the pull of furnace, chimney and
      hot-water-pipe heat as strongest during winter. So a route that reaches the plant corner of
      a cellar keeps working while the rest of the perimeter falls quiet. In a converted house where two heating plants sit in one cellar, that warm zone is larger than usual and worth reading first.

## What interrupts a treated zone on a narrow lot?

The neighbour, mostly — and that is the specific difficulty of working in the densest hamlet in the town.

A continuous treated zone in the soil depends on continuity, and continuity depends on being able to reach the soil. On a large lot the interruptions are the owner's own hardscape. Here they are frequently on the other side of a boundary that is a few feet from the wall.

**Attached and semi-detached housing.** Around 1,316 units in the hamlet are attached. Where two dwellings share a wall, they share the foundation beneath it and the soil under that. Treating one and not the other leaves half of a shared structure untreated, and the same applies to the corrective work: a grade line that has been raised on the neighbour's side is against your timber as much as theirs.

**Narrow side yards.** Where the gap between a house and a boundary fence is a few feet, the trenching route runs along a line that carries the fence, the meters, the service run and the neighbour's planting.

**Shared drives and aprons.** Common on the older streets, and hard surface across the perimeter of two properties at once.

**Attached slabs.** A stoop, a walk, an apron, a patio or a porch pad laid against a foundation cuts the soil continuity, and the only way beneath one is through it. Where a proposal passes over them without saying so, the untreated gap is as wide as the slab.

**Later additions on their own footings.** A rear extension, a sunroom, an enclosed porch or a converted garage each adds a second foundation, a seam against the first, and a soil line of its own.

Stated honestly: on this ground a recorded, defined treated zone alongside corrected drainage and restored clearance will hold, and on an attached or narrow-lot property the paperwork must say where that zone ends and for what reason. A neighbour who will not permit access is a real finding and belongs on the paperwork, not in a conversation nobody wrote down.

## What should a report on a mixed-stock property contain?

The interruptions and the measurements, not a verdict — and on a let or converted building, a note of what was never opened.

A useful report here reads as a list of located items. The measured height of grade on each elevation, reported against the three-inch and eight-inch
      figures instead of being summarised as acceptable. The end of every leader, and what the ground does where it lands. Which slabs, decks, additions and shared surfaces cross the perimeter, and how each was addressed. Whether anyone actually saw the inside face of the foundation, and where they did not — a question with real force in a hamlet holding this much finished cellar and this many tenanted units. Any wood-to-soil contact, with a photograph. Any buried or stacked timber on the plot. The location and condition of on-site wastewater structures where they exist. And whichever elevation carries the wettest ground, because the next finding will be on it.

Twelve months on, the re-check comes down to four questions. Has the mulch climbed back up the siding? Does the leader still discharge where it was left? Has anything been built tight to the house since — a deck, a patio, a shed, a planter, a condenser pad? And has anything been dug through the treated ground, which on these blocks includes utility and sewer work in the street.
