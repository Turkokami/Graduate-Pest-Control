## Why does flat, sandy ground change where termites reach a house?

Because it removes the differential, and the differential is what usually tells a survey where to look.

On a graded lot with relief, water leaves in one direction and collects at one or two places. That produces a house that is dry on most of its perimeter and reliably damp at one corner, and that corner is where the survey spends its time. It is an efficient way to work, and it is not available here.

Garden City sits on the Hempstead Plains, a flat glacial outwash plain. Nothing tidal, nothing incised and no ridge of any kind falls within the village boundary. The soils are coarse, sandy, well-drained meltwater deposits and they behave the same way all the way round a house. The village lies within the Mill River watershed but has no natural surface water inside it, so there is no groundwater story, no stream, no hillside runoff arriving from four properties uphill.

What that leaves is a perimeter of broadly uniform condition. Every elevation has similar soil, similar drainage and similar exposure. Which sounds benign and is not, because it means:

**There is no low-probability side of the house.** Every elevation gets examined to the same standard, which makes an honest survey here longer rather than shorter.

**The soil warms early.** Well-drained sandy ground gains heat faster in spring than heavy or saturated ground does, which brings foraging activity on earlier in the season.

**Whatever water there is, somebody made.** With no natural source, the moisture that matters comes from a downspout discharging at a wall, an irrigation zone, a leaking supply or waste line, a patio that pitches back toward the house, or a bed built up against the siding. That is genuinely good news, because every one of those has an owner and a fix.

**Drainage is unforgiving.** Water needs a gradient to leave. On a flat lot a leader discharging two feet from the wall has nowhere to send its water except into the soil beside the foundation, where it stays long enough to matter. On sloping ground the same discharge would be carried off.

The insect itself is ordinary and well documented. Eastern subterranean termite colonies live in the soil, workers forage outward through covered runs, and they consume wood along the grain while leaving the outer surface intact, so a member can lose most of its section and still look sound. The [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile covers the biology. What changes from village to village is the building and the ground, and here both are unusual.

## What is different about a village built in two waves eighty years apart?

Two entirely separate risk lists, on the same soil, sometimes on the same street.

Garden City's housing has a median construction year of 1951 and a distinctly two-part profile. About 35 per cent of units date from 1939 or earlier; roughly 56 per cent were built between 1940 and 1969, the bulk of that in the 1950s. Around 83 per cent are detached single-family houses.

The older wave includes the A. T. Stewart Era Buildings, listed on the National Register in 1978 — a thematic group of fifty structures, with 44 residences built between 1871 and 1878 to designs by John Kellum and Henry G. Harrison. They are Italianate and Italianate vernacular, from modest cottages up to three-storey frame villas, and the best-known of them carry mansard roofs and cupolas. Garden City Estates, from 1907 and folded into the village at incorporation in 1919, gives the western side another pre-war layer.

The later wave is the post-war expansion, which is the larger half of the village by volume and arrived with a standard kit of parts.

For this insect, the two waves fail in different places, and a contractor working from one template will scope the other one wrong. The sections below take them separately, because that is how the work actually divides.

## Where do termites get into an 1870s frame villa?

At the bottom of the frame, at the porch, and at whatever has been built against the house since.

These are timber-framed buildings on nineteenth-century foundations — brick or stone laid in lime mortar, in some cases rubble, in some cases early concrete where a later repair went in. A wooden sill plate sits across the top of that masonry. That single junction is the whole exercise: everything the insect has to accomplish is getting from soil to sill without drying out on the way, and old masonry is a short trip.

The specific features that matter on this stock:

**The veranda or porch.** Italianate houses of this era carry substantial porches, and a porch has its own footings, its own steps and its own floor structure, frequently at a lower level than the main foundation and frequently built later or rebuilt since. Porch posts bearing on or near grade, stringers let into the ground, and a porch floor with timber close to soil are the classic finds.

**Open mortar joints.** A hundred and fifty years of freeze and thaw opens lime mortar joints, some of them all the way through. A shelter tube in a mortar recess on an irregular masonry wall reads as one more irregularity, which is why these walls are surveyed with a light held close and raked along the surface rather than pointed at it.

**Joist pockets.** Where floor framing is let into masonry rather than carried on a ledger, the end grain of each joist sits inside a pocket in a damp wall. End grain is the most absorbent surface on the member and the least visible.

**The alteration seams.** A house standing since the 1870s has been altered many times. A kitchen pushed out. A porch enclosed and its floor structure become interior framing. A bathroom added over a room never designed to carry plumbing. Every one of those junctions has mismatched framing, improvised flashing and a void somebody left because closing it meant opening more of the house.

**Concealed roof volume, which matters indirectly.** Mansard roofs and cupolas hold large unventilated cavities where a failed flashing wets timber for years. That is primarily a carpenter ant problem — see [ant control in Garden City](/pest-control/garden-city/ant-control/) — but wet timber anywhere in a building is a condition, and a house with chronic concealed moisture at the top usually has other water faults lower down.

Any work on this stock is careful and material-specific. Mortar and hydraulic cement rather than modern patching over a moving joint. Nothing that traps water in masonry that needs to breathe. And the first item on nearly every proposal is clearance: cutting back mature planting and lowering beds so that the line where foundation stops and timber starts can actually be seen.

## What does a 1950s slab do that a cellar does not?

It hides the junction that matters and removes the surface you would have inspected.

The post-war expansion arrived with a repeatable set of parts: attached garage, shallow foundation or slab, simple roof geometry, and utility penetrations sleeved through the wall for water, gas, electrical service, air-conditioning line sets and irrigation. For rodent work that repeatability is a gift, because the failure list is finite — see [structural exclusion in Garden City](/pest-control/garden-city/structural-exclusion/). For termite work it cuts the other way.

**A slab has no inspectable interior face.** In a house with a cellar, a foraging population coming from the soil has to cross a wall you can look at, and a shelter tube on that wall is visible to anyone who raked a light along it. In slab construction the soil-to-timber junction is inside the wall assembly at floor level, behind finishes on the inside and behind siding or veneer on the outside. There is nothing to look at.

**Slabs have penetrations by design.** Plumbing comes up through the slab. So do electrical conduits in some construction. Each penetration is an opening from the soil below into the building above, and it is normally concealed inside a wall or a cabinet base.

**Slabs crack.** Shrinkage cracking is normal, not defective, and a crack is a route.

**Expansion joints are joints.** Where a slab meets a foundation wall, a garage slab meets a house slab, or a patio meets the building, there is a designed gap filled with something compressible that ages out.

**Attached garages have their own slab and their own junction,** typically at a different level, with the wall between garage and house frequently open at the top plate.

The consequence is not that slab houses are more vulnerable. It is that they give less warning. On this stock the first evidence is often damage found during unrelated work — a floor that has gone soft near an exterior wall, a door casing that no longer sits right, a section of trim that sounds hollow when tapped — rather than a tube somebody saw. That in turn is the argument for periodic inspection on a house type that looks, superficially, like it has nothing to inspect.

## What does an irrigation system on a flat lawn actually do?

Waters the perimeter on a timetable that ignores the weather, which is precisely the condition nobody planned for.

Automatic irrigation is close to standard on the village's lawns, and it is the single most under-considered structural risk on the post-war stock. Consider what it does.

**It runs on a clock.** A controller set in April waters through the season whether or not it has rained. Sandy outwash drains fast, which is exactly why lawns here need irrigation — and it also means the system is asked to deliver a lot of water.

**Heads throw at buildings.** A head positioned when a bed was smaller, or a rotor whose arc has drifted, puts water directly onto siding, onto a foundation, into a window well or against a garage door. Water on a wall repeatedly through a season is a wetting event repeated fifty times.

**Leaks are invisible.** A cracked lateral, a weeping valve or a head that does not seat leaks underground, into soil, at a rate nobody notices because the lawn above it looks better than the rest. Cornell Cooperative Extension's list of conditions that encourage infestation includes leaking pipes; a buried irrigation lateral is a leaking pipe that nobody will ever see.

**Trenching cuts treated soil.** Installing or repairing a system means trenching around a property, frequently within a few feet of the foundation. A continuous treated zone works while it is continuous, and an irrigation trench dug the summer after a treatment removes a section of it. Nobody thinks to mention it, because nobody connects a sprinkler repair with a pest treatment.

**It supports the planting that hides the wall.** Well-watered foundation beds on flat ground grow vigorously, hold water against masonry, block drying airflow along the base of the wall, and gradually raise the soil line until the clearance between grade and timber has gone. Cornell's guidance names landscaping that blocks ventilation and insufficient wood-to-soil clearance among the conditions that encourage infestation. A mature, irrigated Garden City foundation planting supplies several at once.

The corrections are simple and they are worth more than most of what gets sold as termite work: turn heads away from the building, fit a rain sensor or run the controller responsively, get somebody to run each zone and watch where the water actually lands, cut beds back off the wall, and lower any bed that has risen above the top of the foundation.

## What did the post-war build-out leave in the ground?

Timber, generally, and the tract-building era was efficient about burying it.

Cornell names buried wood debris directly among the conditions that encourage infestation. On a house built in the last thirty years that is an exception. On a 1950s tract house, and on a Victorian house that has been altered for a century and a half, it is close to routine, because each phase of work left something behind.

The recurring finds, in rough order of frequency:

**Form boards.** When a stoop, a patio, a garage apron or a set of steps was poured against the house, timber forms held the concrete. Stripping them from the buried face is awkward, so they were often left. That is sound softwood in permanent soil contact against the foundation, under concrete, unreachable.

**Construction scrap.** On a fast tract build-out, offcuts, broken sheathing, crate timber and packaging were frequently buried on site during backfill and grading rather than carted away. It is now two to four feet down, a few feet from a wall.

**Stumps and roots.** A tree removed in the 1970s left a root system that is still there. Stumps are a genuine colony site and they are often close to a house, because the tree was planted near it when it was small.

**Landscape timber.** Railway sleepers and timber edging used to hold a bed or step a change of level. Treatment does not last indefinitely at the ground line.

**Old fencing and posts.** Let into the ground beside a wall, cut off at grade when the fence was replaced, and still down there.

None of this shows on a walk-round, which is why the history of the property is survey information. When somebody says the patio went in when the kitchen was done in the nineties, and the garage was extended before that, they have told you which junctions to open and which side of the house to be sceptical about.

The corrective work is unglamorous and it is frequently the largest single item in a proposal: dig out the buried timber where it can be reached, drop grade back off the siding, remove the raised bed built against the wall, redirect the leader, extend it six or eight feet to somewhere with even a slight fall. None of that kills an insect. Every item on it withdraws the reason the insect took an interest, and on these two build eras that generally beats anything put into the ground.

## What can a treatment actually achieve on this soil?

More than on most Long Island lots, and the reason is the soil rather than anything anybody sells.

The general approaches are set out under [termite control](/pest-control/termite-control/): establishing a continuous treated zone in the soil around the structure, installing a monitored in-ground system that works with the colony's own foraging, or a combination matched to the site. What decides which applies is the perimeter and the ground.

Garden City's ground is unusually cooperative. Uniform sandy outwash is diggable, consistent and predictable, without the glacial cobbles and mixed till that make a moraine lot a lottery. Flat terrain means no retaining walls to work around and no terracing that puts three feet of fill against one elevation. A continuous zone is genuinely achievable here in a way it is not everywhere on the Island.

What limits it is not the soil. It is what has been built over the soil.

**Patios and terraces.** Poured or laid against the house, often the full width of a rear elevation.

**Walks, stoops and steps** across the front.

**The driveway apron and the attached garage slab,** which together can account for a long run of perimeter on a post-war house.

**Mature foundation planting** with root systems occupying the first two feet of soil all round.

**The irrigation system**, whose laterals run where a trench would have to go.

So the practical question is never which method is theoretically superior. It is: how much of this perimeter can be reached, what has to be drilled, what has to be cut back, and what does the owner want left alone. A property with three open elevations and one paved one may warrant treated soil where the ground is workable and a monitored system where it is not. A house paved most of the way round tilts toward the monitored approach for the plain reason that it does not require access to soil under concrete.

The honest caveats belong in the proposal, not on the morning of the work. Drilling a patio to reach the soil beneath it is invasive, dusty and audible, and the patches will be visible. A monitored system is worth exactly what its servicing schedule is worth; installed and forgotten, it is a row of caps in a lawn. Anyone weighing proposals should read [choosing a pest control contractor](/compliance/choosing-a-pest-control-contractor/) before comparing them on price, because on this housing stock the differences that matter are in access and scope rather than in product.

## What does the swarm look like here, and where does it get reported from?

A brief daylight event in spring that most people miss, and the location of the wings is worth more than the insects.

Reproductives fly between March and June, usually in daylight, often on a mild day after rain, and sandy soil that warms early tends to bring the season on at the front of that range rather than the back. They are dark, roughly rice-grain sized, with two pairs of wings of equal length noticeably longer than the body, and they shed those wings within minutes. What anyone actually finds is a scatter of small translucent wings on a sill, in a window well, or caught in a web in a corner.

Where they are found is the diagnostic, and the two waves of housing produce different readings.

**In a 1950s slab or shallow-foundation house,** an emergence indoors is a short story. There is no cellar for reproductives to travel up from, so the emergence point is usually close to where the wall meets the floor: a baseboard, a door casing, a window sill at ground level, or the junction between garage and house.

**In an 1870s frame villa,** it is not. Much of the pre-1940 frame stock has wall cavities that run continuously from the sill up toward the roof with no blocking at each floor. Reproductives released at sill level travel up the cavity toward light and emerge at a second- or third-floor window, at a light fitting, or through a gap at the top of a casing. An upstairs finding on one of these houses says nothing about where the colony reached the building. It usually says the cavity is open, which is separately worth knowing.

**Outdoors,** emergence from a stump, a landscape timber or open soil at the end of a garden tells you there is a colony on the property. Emergence from inside the structure tells you the colony is in contact with the building, which is a different conversation.

Spring also produces the confusion, because carpenter ants resume activity across the same weeks and produce winged forms too. The distinction takes seconds once you have seen one of each: a winged ant has a pinched waist, elbowed antennae, and forewings longer than the hind pair. Keeping a few specimens in a sealed bag settles it and costs nothing.

## What does a survey have to cover in each of the two eras?

The soil relationship in both, plus a different second half depending on which house you are standing in.

Common to both:

**The grade line at every elevation.** Where does soil sit relative to the top of the foundation and the bottom of the siding? Measured, not glanced at, and measured at the patio, the bed, the walk and the garage apron, because it is rarely consistent all the way round. On flat ground it is rarely generous anywhere.

**The water.** Gutters and where every leader discharges. The irrigation zones, run and watched. Condensate lines. Hose bibs. Any plumbing that has been weeping quietly inside a wall.

**Buried timber and its history.** What was built, when, and by whom, going back as far as anybody knows.

Specific to the pre-war stock:

**The full interior face of the foundation wall,** floor to sill, with stored goods pulled off the perimeter first, examined with a raking light. Joist pockets individually. Any crawlspace section and its pier tops. The band joist all the way round.

**Sounding and probing.** Working along accessible framing with a tool handle, listening for the flat note that betrays missing section, and following it up with an awl wherever the note changes. Because the outer skin of the wood is left in place, this is the only reliable way to find damage in accessible framing, and it is why a real inspection takes hours.

**The porch and its structure,** including under it wherever access exists.

Specific to the post-war stock:

**The slab-to-wall junction wherever it is visible,** which in practice means the garage, any unfinished utility space, and the base of the wall behind removable trim.

**The plumbing penetrations through the slab,** at every fixture where a cabinet base or an access panel allows it.

**Expansion joints:** house slab to garage slab, slab to patio, slab to walk.

**The garage,** which is the one place on this house type where structure is usually exposed.

What comes out of either version is a drawing and a written finding: where evidence was found, of what kind, what could not be reached and why. What does not come out is proof of absence, because a visual and probing survey cannot see inside a closed wall, under a poured patio, behind finished basement panelling or beneath a slab. Any report implying otherwise claims more than the method supports.

## Why does a job on this stock come back?

Four reasons, and the product is rarely one of them.

**The water fault was never corrected.** The activity was dealt with and the leader still discharges at the wall, or the irrigation zone still throws at the siding, or the patio still pitches back toward the house. That building remains the most attractive target on the street.

**Something buried stayed buried.** Form boards under a stoop, construction scrap in the backfill, a stump beside the foundation, sleepers holding a bed. Treating the structure while leaving those in place is treating the symptom in the most literal sense.

**The perimeter got disturbed afterwards,** and on this housing stock the usual culprit is specific: an irrigation trench. Also a new fence line, a patio poured the following summer, a drainage retrofit, a bed dug over and replanted. A treated zone works while it is continuous, and nobody connects a landscaping job with a pest treatment.

**A slab penetration was reopened.** A plumbing repair under a slab, a line set replaced for new air conditioning, a conduit pulled. The repair gets made, the hole gets patched by whoever was there, and the seal that mattered is gone.

**And nobody looked again.** This insect resists discovery by design: the colony is underground, the workers stay enclosed, the damaged wood looks intact, and the one visible annual event lasts a few hours on a spring afternoon. Re-inspection is not an upsell on this stock. On a slab house it is very nearly the only mechanism by which anything gets found at all.
