## Why does a hamlet with no surface water have a termite problem at all?

Because the insect needs soil moisture rather than surface water, and here every bit of the soil moisture around a foundation was put there by somebody.

Syosset covers 5.0 square miles, all land, with a water area of zero. It is the only market on this stretch of the North Shore with no shoreline, no harbour and no tidal influence, and there is no pond, creek or salt marsh inside the CDP.

That fact is usually presented as reassurance and it is not. Eastern subterranean termites are a soil organism. The colony lives in the ground, workers forage outward from it inside covered runs that keep them in contact with soil humidity, and they consume wood along the grain while leaving the outer surface intact — which is why a member can lose most of its section and still look sound. What they need is ground that does not dry out and timber they can reach without breaking cover. Neither requirement involves surface water.

What Syosset's dryness actually changes is diagnostic, and it is genuinely good news. On a property with a high water table, a damp foundation may simply be a condition of the site. Here it never is. A damp foundation in this hamlet was produced by a leader discharging beside a wall, an irrigation zone hitting the building when it runs, a condensate line running all summer, a patio that pitches back toward the house, a bed built up above the top of the foundation, a downspout extension that has silted up, or a slow leak inside a wall.

Every one of those has an owner and a repair. That is a materially better position than a shoreline property, because there is nothing here that has to be lived with. The corrective list is finite and it is entirely within the property boundary. The [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile covers the insect itself; what this hamlet supplies is a completely artificial set of conditions that can be dismantled.

## Why do 3,385 near-identical houses share one risk list?

Because the same trades built the same details in the same few years, and seventy years of the same gardening has been done to all of them.

About 3,385 of Syosset's roughly 6,164 housing units — 54.9 per cent — were built between 1950 and 1959, with a further 926 in the 1960s. Just 222 units — 3.6 per cent — go back to 1939 or before. Around 91.9 per cent of the stock is detached and 92.4 per cent owner-occupied. The Locust Grove section alone, absorbed into Syosset for the 1990 census and covering about 1.074 square miles, is 98.7 per cent detached and holds roughly 1,465 units built in the fifties.

Those houses share a construction that removes some risks and concentrates others.

**A poured foundation with a shallow basement or a crawlspace.** Not rubble, not fieldstone, so there is no open-mortar-joint problem and a shelter tube on a poured wall is unmistakable rather than hidden in the irregularity of stone. That is a real advantage over the pre-war stock.

**Minimal clearance to begin with.** Houses of this era were not built with generous exposed foundation. The distance between finished grade and the bottom of the siding was modest when new, and Cornell Cooperative Extension calls for a minimum of three inches between wood siding or skirting and soil.

**A band joist at the top of the foundation wall,** which is the timber target, and which on a great many of these houses is now behind a finished basement.

**Crawlspaces under wings, extensions and garages,** entered once a decade, with construction debris still in them and vents that have corroded or been blocked.

Then the seventy years of gardening, which is the part that actually creates the exposure and which is remarkably consistent across the hamlet: mulch beds topped up annually, foundation planting grown in and never cut back, landscape timber used to edge a bed or step a slope, railway sleepers holding a driveway edge, a deck built in the eighties with posts bearing at grade, a patio extended, and a raised bed built against a wall. Each of those puts wood nearer soil, or soil nearer wood, or both.

The consequence of the uniformity is that a finding on one house is a reason to look at the one next door. It is not a diagnosis. Two identical houses on the same street can have completely different histories, and the difference is always what has been done to them since.

## Where has seventy years of gardening put wood against the wall?

In half a dozen places that repeat from property to property.

Cornell names the encouraging conditions directly: insufficient clearance between wood and soil, cracks in foundations, leaking pipes and buried wood debris. On a mature 1950s lot the recurring finds are:

**Mulch beds.** An annual top-up adds a small amount of depth each year. After forty years the bed sits well above the top of the foundation and against the siding, holding moisture permanently at the one junction that most needs to stay dry and visible.

**Landscape timber and sleepers.** Used to edge a bed, step a slope or hold a driveway margin. Treated timber does not stay treated forever where it meets the ground.

**Deck posts and stair stringers.** A deck built in the seventies or eighties commonly has posts bearing on pads at or near grade, a floor frame close to the ground, and steps landing directly on soil. It is the lowest, wettest timber on the property and it is attached to the house.

**Fence posts and trellis** set into the ground within a few feet of a wall.

**Stumps.** A specimen tree removed decades ago left a root plate that is still down there. Stumps are a genuine colony site and they are usually close to buildings because the tree was planted close when it was small.

**Formwork.** When a patio, stoop, garage apron or set of steps was poured against the house, timber forms held the concrete. Stripping them from the buried face is awkward, so they were frequently left — sound softwood in permanent soil contact under concrete, invisible and unreachable.

**Firewood.** Stacked against a garage or a foundation, which is both a colony resource and a bridge.

The corrective work is the unglamorous half and it is frequently the largest single line on a proposal: drop the beds back below the top of the foundation, cut the planting off the wall so the base of the building can dry and be seen, replace or remove landscape timber that sits in soil, deal with the deck posts, dig out what can be dug out, and redirect the leader that has been discharging at the same corner since the eighties. None of it kills an insect. All of it removes the reason the insect was interested, and on this stock it generally outperforms anything applied to the soil alone.

## What did the farm ground leave under the subdivisions?

Buried timber, an altered drainage pattern and planting that predates every house on the street.

This ground was farmed for centuries before anybody put a house on it. A pickle factory operated south of the railway station by 1890, with farmers profiting from cucumber and cabbage crops until a blight ended the trade, and Gold Coast estates occupied much of the rest until the Depression. The Long Island Rail Road opened a station on Jackson Avenue in 1854, and the subdivisions were laid over that ground from the 1940s onward.

A 1950s subdivision was graded, roaded and built on. It was not stripped and remediated the way a modern one is. Three legacies show up on surveys here.

**Buried timber.** Old fence lines, orchard stakes, outbuilding sills, formwork left in place and construction debris pushed into a trench are all wood in soil contact, and Cornell names buried wood as an encouraging condition. Where a house sits on ground that carried structures before it, a colony may have been established there before the foundation was poured. That is a materially different starting point from a colony that arrived afterwards, and it explains properties that have had activity for as long as anybody can remember.

**Drainage that was changed and never replaced.** Agricultural land was drained by grading and open ditch. Roads and driveways were then laid across those patterns with nothing put in their place, so water that once followed a field boundary now runs into a garden and stops against a foundation. Seventy years later nobody remembers why one corner of one lot is always wet, and on ground with no natural water source that anomaly is worth chasing rather than accepting.

**Mature planting from a previous use.** Specimen trees, hedgerows and orchard remnants that predate the houses are now very large, sit close to buildings that were not designed to have them there, and load gutters and roofs built for open sky. A blocked gutter is a moisture source at the foundation as surely as a broken leader is.

## Why does winter concentrate the activity near the plant?

Because heat and moisture stay together, and in a shallow basement the heat is in one place.

Cornell notes a preference for heat from furnaces, chimneys and hot water pipes through the cold season. On this housing type that produces a specific and useful pattern.

A 1950s house has a shallow basement or crawlspace with the heating plant in one corner, a hot water distribution run across the ceiling, and, on many houses, a chimney serving the appliance. Around those, ground and structure temperatures stay workable through the cold months while the rest of the foundation cools. That is where a winter inspection starts, and it is the opposite of where instinct sends people, which is the coldest corner.

Two other winter conditions on this stock are worth knowing.

**A finished basement changes what you can see.** Once framing, insulation and wallboard have gone up against the foundation wall, the band joist and the top of the wall are invisible. The accessible points become the mechanical room, the space behind an access panel, the underside of a stair, and anywhere a service passes through a finish. On a house like that, the exterior grade line does more of the diagnostic work than the interior does.

**An unheated corner is a comparison, not a target.** A garage bay, an unheated crawl under a wing or a store at the far end of the basement will read differently from the plant area, and the contrast is informative: it tells you whether what you are seeing is a seasonal concentration or a permanent one.

The scheduling consequence is that the interior half of this work is comfortable in January and thoroughly disruptive in July, while the exterior half is the reverse. Splitting a job across the year rather than trying to do it all in one visit is normal on this stock.

## What does an addition on a slab change?

Where the approach is, and whether you can see it — which is usually not.

Almost every house in the hamlet has been extended, dormered or altered, and a good proportion of those additions sit on a slab or on a shallow thickened edge rather than on a basement. That introduces conditions the original house does not have.

**A slab edge at or near grade,** where the finished level outside may be within an inch or two of the timber above it.

**A joint where the new slab meets the original foundation,** which is a cold joint, which moves, and which is a covered route from soil to structure with no exposed run to see.

**Plumbing penetrations through the slab,** where a pipe passes through concrete and the annulus around it was never sealed.

**A sill plate laid directly on concrete** with no capillary break on many of these installations.

Because none of that produces a visible shelter tube, the findings on a slab addition are usually indirect. A baseboard that feels damp on an interior partition. A section of floor that has gone slightly soft near an exterior wall. A door casing with a hairline separation that was not there last year. A blistered or hollow-sounding patch low on a wall finish. On the original basement portion you look for the route; on the addition you look for the consequence and then work out where the route must be.

Treating a slab section is also different in practice. Where the perimeter is soil the approach is straightforward; where the addition is surrounded by patio, walk and driveway apron — which on this housing type it frequently is — reaching the soil means drilling. That is invasive, dusty and audible, and the patches will show. It belongs in the conversation at proposal stage rather than on the morning of the work, and it is one of the situations where a monitored in-ground approach is worth considering, precisely because it does not need access to soil under concrete.

The general methods are set out under [termite control](/pest-control/termite-control/). What decides the answer here is how much of a particular property's perimeter is concrete, and that is a survey outcome. ## What does the March-to-June window show here?

A short daylight event and, on a stock this uniform, a signal about the street rather than just the house.

Reproductives fly between March and June, usually in daylight and often on a mild day after rain. They are dark and roughly rice-grain sized, with two pairs of equal-length wings noticeably longer than the body, and they shed those wings within minutes. What people find is a scatter of small translucent wings on a sill, in a window well, or caught in a cobweb.

Where they appear is the diagnostic. Emergence from a stump, a landscape timber, a deck post or open soil at the edge of a garden says a colony is on the property. Emergence from inside — a basement post, a crack at a hearth, a baseboard, a window casing — says the colony is in contact with the structure. Those are different conversations with different scopes.

The hamlet's uniformity adds something that does not apply in a mixed-era market. When alates appear at a window on one street here, the conditions on that street support a colony, and the houses either side were built to the same details on the same ground with the same seventy years of gardening applied to them. That is a reason to have the neighbouring properties looked at rather than to wait, and it is a rare instance where the neighbourhood is genuinely informative about the individual building.

The spring window also generates confusion, because carpenter ants resume activity across the same weeks and produce winged forms. A winged ant has a pinched waist, elbowed antennae and forewings longer than the hind pair; a termite reproductive has a broad waist, straight antennae and four wings of equal length. The [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) profile shows the difference, and keeping a few specimens taped to a card settles it before anybody quotes anything.

## What does a survey cover on a house you have effectively seen before?

The same list every time, done properly, plus the things that make this particular property different from its neighbours.

**The grade line, measured at every elevation.** At the bed, the patio, the walk, the garage apron and the deck. On this housing type it is rarely consistent even on flat ground, and it is the measurement that decides most of the corrective scope.

**The full interior face of the foundation wall,** floor to sill, with stored goods off the perimeter. On poured concrete a shelter tube is obvious, so this part is fast — which is exactly why it gets rushed and why the pier tops, the crawl sections and the area behind the boiler get missed.

**Every crawlspace, individually.** Clearance, debris left from construction, the condition of the vents, the state of any ground cover, and each pier top as a separate soil-to-timber junction.

**Sounding and probing** on accessible framing. Because these insects leave the outer skin of the wood in place, tapping and listening for the dull note of lost section is the only reliable way to find damage in reachable timber, and it is why a real inspection takes hours rather than twenty minutes.

**The retrofit seams.** Where the extension meets the original wall. Where a dormer was cut in. Where a garage was converted. Where a bathroom was added above a room whose floor never anticipated plumbing. Each has mismatched framing and improvised flashing.

**The deck, the patio and any structure attached to the house,** which on this stock is where the wood-to-soil contact usually is.

**The water inventory.** Gutters and where each leader discharges. The condensate line. Each irrigation zone, run and watched, because a head that hits the siding for twenty minutes three times a week is a moisture source nobody counts. Any slab that ponds. Any plumbing weeping quietly inside a wall.

The output is a drawing and a written finding — what evidence was seen, of what kind, and which areas were out of reach and why. What does not come out is proof of absence, and on a house with a finished basement a substantial part of the relevant structure was simply not visible.

## Why does a job here come back?

Three reasons, and the treatment is rarely one of them.

**The moisture fault was never corrected.** The activity was addressed and the bed is still six inches above the top of the foundation, the leader still discharges at the same corner and the irrigation still hits the siding. That building remains the most attractive target on the street, decay fungi are still working, and the conditions now also suit carpenter ants — which is the failure that presents as a different problem two years later.

**Something buried stayed buried.** Formwork under a stoop, a stump beside the foundation, sleepers holding a bed, fence posts along a boundary. Those are colony-supporting resources within feet of the house.

**The perimeter got disturbed.** A treated zone works while it is continuous, and on this housing type it is routinely interrupted: an irrigation trench, a new fence line, a French drain retrofitted the following summer, a bed dug over and replanted, a driveway relaid. Nobody connects a landscaping job with a pest treatment, so nobody mentions it.
