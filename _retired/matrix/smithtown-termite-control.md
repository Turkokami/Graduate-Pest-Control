## How far is the house from the river?

That is the first question on a termite survey here, and it predicts more than the age of the building does.

An eastern subterranean colony lives in soil and needs continuing contact with soil moisture. Its workers are thin-skinned and dry out quickly in open air, which is why everything they build — tubes, galleries, travel routes — exists to keep humidity around them. Anything that keeps the ground damp through the summer therefore expands the amount of a property a colony can work, and anything that dries it contracts it.

Smithtown gives us both conditions inside one hamlet, separated by a few hundred feet of elevation and distance. The river corridor carries a high water table, a short vertical gap between the underside of a floor structure and saturated soil, and made ground alongside a channel that has been dammed twice and dredged, filled and built against for three centuries. Crawlspaces in that band do not dry between rains the way crawlspaces on the higher ground do. Framing sits at a moisture content that decay fungi like, and framing softened by decay is easier for anything else to work.

The higher ground inverts the picture. Free-draining sand and gravel, no standing water, a crawl that reaches a sensible moisture content by June, and a colony's reach limited to whatever the property is doing to keep one strip of soil wet. On those streets termite pressure is a landscaping and drainage question, not a geology one, and it is correctable.

The practical instruction is that a survey establishes the property's position first and lets that set the expectations. Two houses of identical age and construction, one on the corridor and one half a mile up the slope, are not the same risk and should not be sold the same scope. ## What did the storm actually leave behind?

Timber at a raised moisture content inside closed assemblies, and a set of properties that have proved they cannot move water.

Over nine inches of rain fell on 18 and 19 August 2024, enough to breach a dam that had stood since 1798. For termite work the dam is beside the point; the volume is not. Nine inches across a catchment in about a day saturates soil to a depth and for a duration that ordinary rain does not, and saturated soil around a foundation stays workable for foraging long after the surface has dried.

The building-side consequence runs on a longer clock. Water that entered through a window well, a failed area drain, an overwhelmed leader or a wall junction did not stay where it landed. It ran into a stud bay, along a sill, into a rim, under a slab edge or into a crawl, and then it dried at whatever rate a closed assembly allows, which is slowly. Wood does not care how the water arrived. It responds to the moisture content it reaches and how long it holds it, and a sill plate or rim that spent weeks wet is a materially more attractive target the following spring than it was the previous one.

This is why the storm is a diagnostic asset rather than only a misfortune. The hamlet now has an unusually clear record of which buildings could not cope. On a survey, the question worth asking the owner is not whether it rained on the house — it rained on everything — but where the water went afterwards, what got wet, and whether anything downstream of that point has been opened up and looked at since. On a great many properties the honest answer is that nobody has looked.

Cornell Cooperative Extension names leaking pipes and dripping faucets among the conditions that sustain the soil moisture a colony needs. A storm is the same input delivered in one instalment, and the properties that took it are the ones where the ordinary sources will now matter more.

## What is the treated zone going to run into?

A trench, on the streets where the sewer is coming, and the usual suburban interruptions everywhere else.

A continuous treated zone in soil works because it is continuous. Its whole value lies in the absence of gaps between the colony and the structure, which is why an untreated interruption, a later excavation or a disturbance of treated soil compromises it in a way that is invisible from the surface and stays invisible for years.

Smithtown has a scheduled, publicly documented programme of exactly that kind of disturbance. The Sewer District No. 6 extension runs connecting lines inside existing road rights-of-way across downtown and the Village of the Branch, and connecting a premises means bringing a lateral across its frontage. Any treated ground on that frontage goes with the trench.

The sequencing point is straightforward and rarely made. Where a property is programmed for connection, soil treatment on the affected elevation is worth deferring until after the lateral is in and the ground is reinstated. Where treatment has already been carried out and an excavation follows, the treated zone needs re-establishing along the disturbed section, and somebody has to know to ask for that — a contractor digging a service trench has no reason to think about it and no obligation to report it.

Away from downtown the interruptions are the ordinary ones and they are numerous on 1960s suburban lots. Patios, walks, garage aprons, stoops and pool decks laid against the foundation break the soil continuity at the wall, and reaching what is under them means working through them rather than around them. Decks, sunrooms, screened porches and rear additions each bring their own footing and their own junction. Buried services, irrigation runs and on unsewered properties the wastewater structures are all routes through the treated ground. And where a border has been built up over six decades, the soil against the wall now sits well above the original grade line, which changes where the zone has to be for it to mean anything.

The honest position is that a recorded, continuous zone plus corrected drainage and clearance is durable on this ground, and a zone crossed by three slabs, a deck and a service trench with no note of where the gaps sit is not.

## Where does a 1960s Smithtown house lose its clearance?

At the bed, at the patio, at the deck post and at the step — usually all four, and none of them the result of a decision.

Cornell Cooperative Extension's figures are specific and they map directly onto this stock. A minimum of three inches between wood siding or skirting and soil. Eight inches of clean concrete where skirting meets the ground. An inch of free air space on both sides and at the end of a wood girder. Wooden debris, including buried stumps, removed from the property. Downspouts directed farther from the house.

About 2,866 houses here were built in the 1960s and none of them was landscaped once. They have been landscaped continuously for sixty years, and each round adds a little.

**The bed against the wall.** Mulched every spring, edged at some point with timber, planted with shrubs that have doubled in width. Three inches disappears in a decade and nobody notices, because the change per year is smaller than the eye can measure.

**The patio and the walk.** Laid tight to the house, settled toward it, and now returning water to the wall as well as bridging the soil to the skirting.

**The deck.** Two separate problems in one structure. Posts set directly into the ground are wood in soil by design. The ledger bolted to the rim is a wet interface on the wall, and the framing behind it is what carpenter ants exploit as well, which is why the same repair serves two services — see [ant control](/pest-control/ant-control/) and the [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) profile.

**The porch and the step.** Concrete steps poured against a house and later re-faced, and porch structures with a void beneath, both hide a soil-to-timber contact under something that looks solid.

**Landscape timber.** Raised beds and edging built from timber in the seventies and eighties survive on a great many of these lots within a few feet of the wall.

Removing buried and stacked timber and cutting the beds back is the least impressive part of this work and among the most effective, and none of it needs a contractor.

## What does the pre-1940 layer need instead?

Masonry work, and a different set of expectations about what can be seen.

Around 476 units in the census area — 5.8 per cent — date from 1939 or earlier, which is a small share and a real one in a place first settled in 1665 and incorporated on 7 March 1788. Those buildings sit in and around the old centre, along the older frontage and in the waterside villages, and they present a foundation type the post-war stock does not.

A rubble or fieldstone wall is not a defined surface with discrete penetrations. It is a mass of stone and mortar with joints that have opened over a century of freeze-thaw, and it offers a colony continuous concealed routes rather than the specific crack a poured wall offers. A hand-cut sill bearing directly on that stone has no capillary break under it. Additions have been made across generations with no drawings, and a nineteenth-century structure frequently sits on more than one foundation type because it grew.

Three practical differences follow. Evidence is harder to read, because a tube travelling inside an open joint may never appear on the visible face. The corrective work begins as repointing rather than as sealing, using mortar and hydraulic cement appropriate to the wall rather than a modern mix that will simply crack the stone alongside it. And a report on such a building has to be explicit about what could not be seen, because on this construction that list is long and it is the honest part of the document.

The same buildings are worth reading alongside what survives around them. The Caleb Smith House of 1753, built by Caleb Smith and his father Daniel Smith II and later the Wyandanch Club, is a reminder of how much standing timber this ground carried before the subdivisions arrived, and old field lines here still show up as rear property boundaries with a stump line under them.

## What can be done about a crawlspace on the corridor?

Less than on the higher ground, and the honest scope says which of the two you have.

A crawl on free-draining outwash half a mile from the river is a solvable problem. Clear the ground, lay a proper vapour barrier and seal its laps, get the vents working, take the leaders away from the wall, correct the grade so surface water leaves rather than arrives, and insulate the lines that sweat. The space reaches a sensible moisture content, the framing dries, and the conditions that drew the insect stop existing.

A crawl sitting a short distance above a high water table does not respond the same way, because the water is not arriving from the roof or the surface. It is arriving from below, continuously, as vapour off saturated soil. A vapour barrier is still the highest-value item on such a property — it is what separates the framing from the ground — but the surrounding decisions get harder. Ventilation that works well in July can import humid air and condense it on cool framing; ventilation that is closed off without any other change traps what the soil gives up. What actually holds on those properties is a sealed ground plane, controlled air, and framing kept out of contact with anything damp, and that is building work rather than pest work.

The distinction matters commercially because it decides what should be promised. On the higher ground, moisture correction genuinely removes the condition. On the corridor it reduces it, and the treatment and the monitoring carry more of the weight because the ground will never be dry. Selling the first outcome to a property that can only achieve the second is how a client ends up disappointed by work that was competently done.

Two further items belong on a corridor crawl. Timber stored on the soil floor, which is common and which is a colony site inside the building's footprint. And the pier and girder bearings, where Cornell's inch of free air space on both sides and at the end of a girder is very often absent because a pier was rebuilt at some point and the timber was set tight.

## Where do you actually find a tube here?

Inside, on the wall the owner has stacked things against — unless the basement was finished, in which case somewhere worse.

On the 1960s stock the productive place is the interior face of the poured foundation wall in an unfinished basement: the top of the wall, the corners, behind the plant, and around every pipe sleeve. A tube on a poured wall is legible in a way it never is on rubble, and it is one of the few genuine advantages of post-war construction. Getting there means moving stored goods away from the perimeter, which is the single most useful thing an owner can do before a survey.

The tube itself tells you which kind of route you are looking at. A working tube runs from soil to wood and carries traffic. An exploratory tube leaves the soil and ends in mid-air having found nothing, which says there is foraging pressure in that area even though that route failed. A drop tube hangs down from infested wood toward the ground, built from the timber end, and says the wood above is already occupied. The test for whether any of them is live is mechanical rather than interpretive: break out a short section, leave the rest, come back in several days, and see whether it has been repaired.

Warmth concentrates the traffic. Cornell notes that termites favour heat from furnaces, chimneys and hot water pipes, particularly in winter, so the plant corner is the first interior wall to read and not the last — awkwardly, since it is also the corner most obscured by equipment, shelving and pipework. Activity found in a cold perimeter in January carries far more weight than the same finding in July.

The counterpart is the finished basement, and a great many Smithtown basements were finished in the decades after the houses were built. Framing, insulation and wallboard against the foundation cover the most legible surface in the building. On those houses the exterior carries the whole inspection, the mechanical room and any access panel become important, and the report has to name the elevations that were never seen rather than describing the building as clear.

## What does a spring flight tell you here?

Where the colony is relative to your building, which is the only part of the event that carries information.

Eastern subterranean termites swarm March through June. They fly in daylight, often on a warm day after rain, and on a street where every house shares an age, a soil and a landscaping history several neighbours can see a flight within the same hour. That coincidence gets misread in both directions every year: some conclude the street has an outbreak, others conclude that because everybody saw it nothing is specific to them.

The correction is that a swarm is a mature colony reproducing, and maturity takes several years of growth. The flight is never the beginning of the problem. What varies is the location. Swarmers emerging outdoors — over a lawn, from a stump, from landscape timber, from soil away from the building — establish that a colony is on the property. Swarmers emerging indoors, from a crack in a slab, along a baseboard, around a basement window frame or at the base of a post, establish that a colony is in contact with the structure, because those insects came out of the building.

Because the flights are brief and frequently happen while nobody is home, most are never seen directly. What survives is the wing scatter. Cornell describes discarded wings about a quarter of an inch long, and they collect at windows, in sill tracks, in cellar window wells, in a web in a corner and on the floor beneath a window, where they can sit for weeks. Late winter through spring is the right time to look at those places deliberately rather than to wait for something more dramatic.

If you find them, keep some. Tape half a dozen to a card or seal them in a bag, photograph them where they lie with something for scale, and note the room and the elevation. That specimen is the most useful object you can hand an inspection and it is usually gone by the time anyone arrives.

The confusion to head off is with carpenter ant alates, which fly around here in the same weeks and are a different job. A termite swarmer has a broad waist with no pinch, straight beaded antennae and four wings of roughly equal length. A winged ant has a sharply pinched waist, elbowed antennae and forewings noticeably longer than the pair behind.

## What should the report and the re-check contain?

Where the water went, what the clearances actually measure, and what nobody could see.

A report worth having on this stock reads as located findings. The property's position relative to the river corridor and what that implies. The measured grade against each elevation, tested against the three-inch and eight-inch figures rather than described as acceptable. Where each leader, condensate line and pump discharges, and what the ground does there. Every slab, deck and addition that interrupts the perimeter, with the method used at each one. Which elevations of the interior foundation face could actually be inspected, and which were behind a finished surface. Any wood-to-soil contact found, photographed. Any buried or stacked timber on the lot. What the August 2024 event did to this building, if the owner knows. And whether a sewer connection is programmed for the frontage.

The re-check a year later asks four short questions and they are always the same four. Has the mulch gone back up against the siding? Are the leaders still discharging where they were put? Has anything new been built against the house? And has the interior evidence changed anywhere the wall can still be seen?
