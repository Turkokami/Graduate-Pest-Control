## What does a permanent impoundment do to the buildings around it?

It removes the one variable that termite work normally controls.

In most markets a termite job begins by asking where the moisture is coming from and how to stop it. A downspout is redirected. A patio is repitched. A border is cut back. Soil moisture drops, the conditions become marginal, and the treatment has something to hold.

The Roslyn valley floor does not offer that. The five-acre mill pond south of the grist mill survives as physical evidence of the village's industrial past, and the historic district wraps around it along with Roslyn Park. Standing water held permanently at the bottom of a steep-sided valley, ringed by dense old fabric, fixes the water table for everything near the valley floor and keeps it there all year. Below the pond, the head of Hempstead Harbor takes over — an inner harbour generally less than six feet deep at low water, with intertidal mudflats and salt marsh, and a tidal range of approximately seven feet.

Nothing a property owner does changes that. So the emphasis shifts, and it shifts in a direction worth being explicit about.

**Moisture management becomes local rather than general.** You cannot lower the water table, but you can stop the building's own roof water arriving at the base of a wall, keep a terrace from holding a puddle against masonry, and get a leader line functioning again.

**Clearance becomes the primary control.** Cornell Cooperative Extension asks for eight inches of clean concrete between skirting and soil and a minimum of three inches between wood siding or skirting and soil, and describes any wood close to or in contact with soil as susceptible. On saturated ground that clearance is the difference between a route and no route, and it is achievable where drying the soil is not.

**Timber condition becomes the thing to monitor.** Wood that stays wet deteriorates, and deteriorated wood at the base of a building is both easier for a colony to work and less able to hold a repair.

**Monitoring becomes ongoing rather than a one-off verdict.** On ground that is permanently favourable, a clean inspection is a statement about a date, not a status.

That is not a counsel of despair. It is the correct framing for a village where the conditions are fixed and the buildings are worth keeping.

## Why does a building on this ground not stay where it was put?

Because saturated ground under three centuries of masonry does not hold still, and settlement opens exactly the junction this insect uses.

The Roslyn Grist Mill is the clearest illustration in the village. Built between 1715 and 1741 and described as one of the few surviving Dutch colonial commercial frame buildings in the United States, it measures fifty feet six inches by twenty-five feet and retains original timber framing using transverse post-and-beam bents connected by sills and wall plates. Tides and successive renovations have in the past taken its ground floor some twelve feet below the level of the street. It milled until 1916, then served as a tea house and museum until 1975. Foundation work was completed in 2024, and Nassau County awarded $2 million for the final restoration phase in 2025.

That is a landmark rather than a typical property, but the mechanism is general. On the valley floor, buildings settle differentially, and the junction between masonry and the timber bearing on it opens.

For termite work the consequences are specific.

**The sill line widens.** A hand-cut sill sitting on rubble or early block was never a sealed interface. Settlement makes the gap between them larger and more continuous, and that is where a route arrives at timber.

**Grade rises around a settling building.** As a structure drops relative to the ground, the effective clearance between the siding and the soil falls, without anybody adding a single inch of mulch. On a building that has settled several inches over decades, a skirt that once had its three inches has lost them.

**Repairs are made to the settled position.** Steps, terraces, thresholds and paving get rebuilt to meet the building where it now is, which locks the new grade in place.

**Old repairs concentrate the damp.** A century of patched pointing, in mortars of varying hardness, produces a wall that sheds water unevenly and holds it in some joints and not others.

The survey implication is that on this ground the age of a repair matters as much as the age of the building. A well-executed nineteenth-century foundation with three later interventions in it is a more complicated object than either an untouched one or a rebuilt one.

## Where does a clapboard house on rubble hide an active route?

Behind the cladding, inside the mortar joints, and under whatever is planted against the wall.

The district is predominantly wood frame with clapboard siding, running from the circa 1680 Van Nostrand-Starkins house through Colonial Revival work of the early twentieth century, and the Roslyn Village Historic District takes in most of the remaining properties in the triangle formed by Main Street, East Broadway and Old Northern Boulevard. Village-wide, about 261 units predate 1940 and 173 date from the 1940s.

That construction hides evidence in four places.

**Inside the mortar joints.** On a poured concrete wall a mud tube is a visible object on a flat surface. On rubble or early block it travels inside the joints, and it can run a long way before it emerges. Cornell describes tubes varying in diameter from the size of a wheat straw to wider than a thumb; on this masonry the wheat-straw end of that range is easy to miss entirely. The [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile covers how to distinguish a live route from an abandoned one.

**Behind the clapboard.** Lapped cladding with a great many horizontal joints, no sheathing membrane in the modern sense and frequently no insulation. A route that gets behind the bottom course has an unobserved vertical path.

**Under the planting.** The masonry-to-timber junction on a village frame house is behind a bed, below the level anyone looks. Moving soil to get a hand on it is frequently the most productive part of an inspection, and it is where the clearance question is answered.

**Inside the cellar, where there is one.** These cellars were dug for storage and coal. Everything else in them arrived later. The productive interior places are the top of the wall where the sill bears, the corners, the joist pockets, behind the heating plant — Cornell notes termites favour heat from furnaces, chimneys and hot water pipes, particularly in winter — and around every pipe sleeve.

The honest limitation is that some of those surfaces cannot be seen at all on a given building. A report should say which ones were not, rather than presenting a partial inspection as a complete one.

## What can you change on a listed elevation, and what needs a certificate?

Most of the repair, and the answer is written down.

The Historic District Board issues certificates of appropriateness. Any construction, alteration, change, move or demolition within the Historic/Scenic Overlay District — delineated on the village zoning map dated 13 February 1997 — needs board approval before a permit issues. The board weighs general design, character and appropriateness, scale relative to the property and those around it, texture, materials and colour, the proportion and arrangement of openings, roof shape, and historical and architectural value. Individual properties outside the district can also be designated as historic sites.

The exemption is the sentence that governs this work: ordinary maintenance or repair, with like materials of similar quality and colour, does not require approval.

Sorting the typical termite repair list against that line:

**Exempt.** Repointing an open joint in matching mortar. Replacing a decayed section of clapboard with the same profile. Replacing a rotted skirt board in like material. Rebedding a flashing. Cutting back a planting bed. Extending an existing downspout. Clearing a leader line. Re-laying a small area of path to restore fall.

**Needs a certificate.** Cutting a new foundation vent where none existed. Replacing timber cladding with a different material. Rebuilding a terrace or retaining wall to a different height or profile. Altering a window or door opening. Adding a visible metal skirt or barrier on a street elevation. Changing a roof profile where roof work is part of the repair.

The distinction matters most in the middle of a job. Termite repair frequently reveals more decay than the survey predicted — a sill section that has to be replaced, a porch structure that has to come apart. Where that repair can be executed in like materials, work continues. Where the honest fix changes the appearance, it stops until a certificate issues.

Two practical instructions follow. Establish which category a proposed repair falls into before it is priced, not after. And specify the repair in like materials wherever it is technically sound to do so, because that is both what the village wants and what an old building actually needs — a hard modern mortar in a soft old wall causes damage that outlasts the insect.

It is also worth being clear which Roslyn a building is in. The incorporated village is only 0.6 square miles and it incorporated on 11 January 1932. Roslyn Estates, Roslyn Harbor and Roslyn Heights are separately incorporated villages with their own arrangements, and a good deal of surrounding unincorporated ground carries the Roslyn postal name without being in the village at all.

## What does 253 feet of fall decide?

Which corner of the building has the problem — and on this relief it is almost always one corner rather than four.

The highest point in the village, near Piper Court, sits at roughly 253 feet; the lowest is Hempstead Harbor at sea level, and the whole village is 0.6 square miles. That is not a gentle gradient.

On most Roslyn properties water does not sit evenly around a building. It arrives at one face, from uphill, with volume and speed behind it, and it keeps arriving there for as long as the grading stays as it is. Surveys here produce a lopsided result far more often than not.

The pattern repeats:

**The uphill face carries the moisture and the highest grade.** Where a slope meets a building, the usual historical response has been to build the ground up against it — a terrace, a wall, steps, a raised bed — because that is how you make a level garden on a hillside. Every one of those is a structure holding wet soil against masonry, and several of them are two hundred years old.

**The downhill corner collects.** Washed soil, leaf litter and mulch accumulate at the low corner, raising the effective grade a little each year.

**The side elevations read normal.** Which is why a perimeter-average inspection report on a Roslyn property is close to useless. The result has to be given elevation by elevation, with the clearance measured on each face.

**The steps and terrace junctions are the interfaces.** The joint between a masonry terrace and the wall it abuts is both a water path and a route, and it is frequently the single most productive item on a hillside village lot.

The corrective work is landscape work as much as pest work, and on a listed elevation it needs the exemption question asked before it starts.

## What do the 1950s apartment blocks need instead?

A different survey, a different authority and a different set of problems from the frame houses two streets away.

The village is not what a visitor walking Main Street would guess. Roslyn holds roughly 1,423 housing units with a median construction year of 1955, and the largest single block is the 585 units — 41.1 per cent — built during the 1950s. Some 238 units, 16.7 per cent, sit in buildings of 50 or more, 162 in buildings of 10 to 19, and 165 in three- and four-unit buildings.

That mid-century multifamily component sits inside the same ravine, on the same wet ground, and it presents its own version of the problem.

**The perimeter is long and it is landscaped.** A large low building has a great deal of foundation line, and it is usually surrounded by mature planting, beds and paving that have been maintained continuously for seventy years. Grade against the wall is the same question as on a house, asked over a much greater length.

**Planters and podium landscaping are soil against structure by design.** Where a raised planter abuts a building, there is wet soil at or above floor level with structure behind it.

**The evidence appears at the wrong scale.** A swarm at one ground-floor window of a fifty-unit building is a single observation about a very large object. It says a route exists somewhere along a perimeter that may run several hundred feet.

**Access is institutional.** Cellars, plant rooms, service corridors and the grounds are managed rather than owned by the person reporting. A survey needs the managing agent or board to commission it, and it needs a walk of the whole perimeter rather than an appointment in a flat. That framework sits at [co-op and condo pest control](/commercial/coop-condo-pest-control/) and [property management pest control](/commercial/property-management-pest-control/).

**Post-war construction reads more clearly than pre-war.** A poured foundation is a legible surface, and where the interior face is visible in a service area a tube is a visible object rather than something hidden in a joint. That is the one advantage this half of the stock has.

## Where does the March-to-June flight get seen in a village this compact?

Indoors, and frequently by somebody who does not own the building.

Eastern subterranean termites swarm March through June. In Roslyn the flight is reported from three quite different situations.

**A frame house on the slopes**, where an owner sees swarmers at a window or finds wings on a sill. That is the straightforward case and it produces a survey.

**A flat above a Main Street trade premises**, where a tenant sees them and the building is held by somebody else. About 514 of the village's units are renter-occupied and only 44.5 per cent of the stock is detached houses. The observation has to travel upward before it becomes useful, because the survey needs the cellar and the grounds.

**A cellar, an areaway or a rear service yard**, where nobody looks. This is the common late report: by the time somebody connects the wings on a stair landing to something seen in a yard weeks earlier, the flight is over.

Three things are worth doing at the moment of a sighting, none requiring expertise. Keep a sample of the wings — Cornell describes them as about a quarter of an inch long, gathered at windows and light fittings. Note the joint, crack or frame they came out of rather than only where they collected. And send it to whoever holds the building the same day.

Swarmers outdoors near a property indicate a colony in the vicinity. Swarmers emerging indoors indicate a colony with a route into the structure, and in a village this dense the difference between those two findings is worth establishing carefully rather than assumed either way.

## What can a treated zone achieve at the valley floor?

Something real, provided the interruptions are named and the ground is understood.

A village lot in a historic core is one of the harder perimeters in this region to treat continuously, and the reasons are physical rather than technical.

**Terraces, steps and retaining walls** cross the line and hold soil against the wall above the treated depth.

**Areaways and cellar entrances** are voids at or below grade with their own drainage.

**Attached and adjoining buildings** mean the perimeter may not be continuous property at all — on Main Street a foundation carries on into somebody else's cellar, and a party wall belongs to two people.

**A high water table limits what the soil can hold**, so the zone has to be designed for the conditions rather than applied to a standard specification and reported as complete.

**Made ground and centuries of fill** mean the material at one corner is not the material at another, and continuity cannot be inferred from a single point.

The workable position is a scope that lists each interruption, states how it was addressed, and names the ones that could not be — combined with the corrections that are always available: clearance restored where it is achievable, roof water taken away from the base of the wall, leader lines opened, wooden debris and buried timber removed from the lot, and any cellar or crawlspace ventilation kept working rather than blocked.

## What has to be in the report before any repair is priced?

The elevations, the access, the interruptions and the consent question — before anyone quotes carpentry.

A report worth having on a Roslyn building sets out: the grade height measured against Cornell's clearance figures on each elevation; where each downspout and leader terminates and what the ground does there; every wood-to-soil contact found, with a location precise enough for a stranger to find; which parts of the foundation wall — inside and out — were actually visible and which were obscured by planting, finishes or stored material; the condition of terraces, steps and retaining walls where they meet the building; any buried or stacked timber on the lot; and, for a shared building, how many tenancies it holds and who holds the freehold.

Then the village-specific item, which no report in a neighbouring market carries: which of the proposed repairs are ordinary maintenance in like materials and which will require a certificate of appropriateness. Getting that wrong costs weeks in the middle of a job, and the answer is available before the job starts.

The re-check a year on asks whether the landscaping has put the grade back, whether anything new has been built against the building, whether a terrace or step has moved over the winter, and whether the wet elevation has changed at all — which on this relief is the only one that was ever likely to.
