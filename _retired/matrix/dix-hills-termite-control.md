## Why is a 1970 house not a safer house?

Because a subterranean colony is not reading the build date. It is reading the soil against the wood.

By the standards of the Town of Huntington this hamlet was built late. The median year across its roughly 8,067 units is 1970: about 2,948 went up during the 1960s, 1,821 during the 1970s and 1,050 during the 1980s, while barely 133 predate 1940. That reads like a low-risk profile and it is not one, for a simple reason. The risk factor on this ground is not the age of the timber. It is how close that timber sits to permanently damp soil, and the distance between the two has been shrinking every year since the house was finished.

Cornell Cooperative Extension puts numbers on the distances. Soil to wood siding or skirting: three inches at the very least. Concrete between skirting and ground, eight inches of it, clean. An inch of free air either side of a wood girder and at the end where it bears. Wooden debris, buried stumps included, off the property. Downspouts directed further out from the house.

Now set fifty-odd years of ordinary suburban improvement against that list. Mulch topped up every spring. A landscape-timber border installed around 1984. Shrubs planted small, now four feet across, with soil mounded to their stems. A patio extended twice. A deck built, replaced, and rebuilt a little wider. A pool put in, with a surround laid at the height that suited the back doors rather than the height the house was built to. Irrigation added. None of it was a mistake and none of it was a decision about insects, and the cumulative effect on a large share of this stock is a building whose lowest timber now sits closer to wet ground than the guidance allows.

That is the whole of the risk here, and it is also the whole of the remedy. Nothing about 1970 construction is inherently vulnerable. The landscaping is what changed.

## What does glacial ground do once you cut into it?

It stops draining predictably, and on lots this size a great deal has been cut into it.

The high ground is glacial. Long Island's northern spine is the Harbor Hill moraine, and the island's summit stands on it — Jayne's Hill, in West Hills County Park, recorded at 400.9 feet by Suffolk County and at 387 by the Geographic Names Information System. Coarse glacial material takes water down quickly. That is why 15.9 square miles of census area hold no harbour, no creek, no river and no pond: the water goes down rather than across, and nothing is left on the surface to form one.

Undisturbed, that is close to ideal ground. Disturbed, it is unpredictable, and this stock has been disturbed comprehensively.

**Pool excavations.** A large hole, refilled with material that settles at a different rate from what surrounds it, carrying a surround and an equipment pad on top.

**Foundation and basement cuts,** backfilled against the wall with whatever came out of the hole plus whatever the site produced.

**Driveway regrading and retaining walls,** which redirect where surface water arrives.

**Drywells and buried drainage,** put in at various dates by various people and recorded nowhere an owner can look up.

**Utility trenching** for water, gas, irrigation, pool circulation and generator lines, each one a backfilled channel running toward a building.

All of those change where water goes on a specific parcel. So the useful diagnostic on a large lot is not the soil, which is much the same across the hamlet, but the behaviour of it: which elevation stays dark longest after rain, where the lawn is soft in August, and where a trench line shows as a strip of different growth. The ground here is uniform. What has been done to it is not.

## Where does a pool put soil against wood?

In four places, and three of them disappear once the coping is on.

Pools are common on this stock and they are the largest single alteration most of these properties have had. From a wood-destroying insect point of view they matter more than any other piece of landscaping, and the reasons are structural rather than aquatic.

**The surround raised above original grade.** A pool terrace is normally set at a level that works with the back doors and the lawn, and that level is very often higher than the grade the house was built to. Where such a terrace runs up to the building, the clearance behind it has gone and there is now hard surface conducting water toward the wall rather than away from it.

**Timber in the assembly.** Decking, edging, a raised deck section, a bench detail, a privacy screen, a pergola, and the posts beneath any of them. Around a pool, timber and permanently irrigated soil meet by design.

**The equipment enclosure.** A pad, a screen wall or a small structure, usually tight to a boundary or a house wall, wet from backwash and condensation, standing on ground that stays damp all season.

**The backfill and the settlement.** Ground around a pool consolidates for years afterwards. A surround that was level in 2004 has generally dropped somewhere by now, and a slab that has dropped throws water to its lowest point, which is frequently a house wall or a step.

The corrective work is not exotic. Restore clearance where the terrace meets the building, get timber out of soil contact where it can be got out, repitch where a slab has dropped, and move irrigation heads off the walls. It is worth doing before a treatment rather than after, because ground that is about to be dug up for a repair will need its treated zone re-established anyway.

## What is buried in three centuries of farmed ground?

Timber, at exactly the depth a colony can use, and none of it shows up on an inspection.

The land history here is long and agricultural. In 1701 Timothy Carll took title to more than 6,000 acres and put up a log cabin, and by the middle of the eighteenth century Carll's Straight Path ran between two of the family's farms. In the years from 1784 to 1800 some forty families worked subdivided holdings averaging two to three hundred acres, and Half Hollow Hills was where farmers rested on the haul down to the salt marshes of the Great South Bay. The roads arrived afterwards. The old east-west trail was made Jericho Turnpike in 1817. Vanderbilt's Motor Parkway opened on 10 October 1908, the first paved limited-access road in the United States. The Northern State got only as far as Commack Road by 1950, and Expressway construction did not reach Melville until 1962. The subdivisions followed each of them in turn.

Three centuries of that leaves a particular inheritance in the ground.

**Hedgerow and field-boundary timber.** Old field edges tended to become rear property lines, which puts a line of stumps along the back fences of an entire row of houses.

**Farm structure debris,** where an outbuilding was pushed over and covered for a subdivision rather than carted away.

**Construction burial.** A fast build-out is not a tidy one. Offcuts, form boards, stakes, stumps and packaging get buried on site because burying is quicker than removing, and this hamlet raised thousands of houses across two decades.

**Landscape timbers of the 1970s and 1980s,** used for raised borders and edging, still in the soil on a great many lots and often within a few feet of a wall.

**Trees removed and ground rather than dug,** leaving a stump below grade on land that was wooded to start with.

Cornell's warning applies directly: timber left lying in a yard feeds a colony until that colony is big enough to start on the building. On an acre with a wooded margin that material is plentiful, and it is the part of the risk nobody can see. Clearing stacked and buried wood is the least glamorous item on any scope here and among the more effective, and most of it needs a shovel rather than a contractor.

## Why is the treated zone hardest to keep continuous on the biggest lots?

Because more construction crosses the perimeter here than anywhere else in the town.

A continuous treated zone in the soil depends on reaching the soil, and on this stock the quantity of building standing between you and it is substantial.

**Attached slabs.** Patios, walks, garage aprons, porch pads, stoops and pool terraces laid up to a foundation cut the continuity, and the only route beneath one runs through it. A proposal that skips them without saying so leaves an untreated strip as wide as the terrace.

**Attached structures on their own footings.** A sunroom, a rear extension, a screened porch, a bay projection or a conservatory each adds a further foundation, a seam where it meets the original, and its own line of soil.

**Detached structures.** A pool house, a workshop or a detached garage is a separate building with a separate perimeter, and it usually stands closer to the wood line than the house does.

**Utility runs.** Buried lines, sleeves, irrigation trenching, pool circulation and generator gas all pass through the treated ground, and all of them need the zone re-established after any excavation.

**Deep hardscape.** Fifty years of topping up a border can leave the soil against a wall standing far above the level the house was built to, which moves the depth at which a treated zone has to sit.

**Sheer length.** Everything above repeats along a perimeter measured in the low hundreds of feet, on more than one building.

The honest position is that a defined and recorded zone, alongside corrected drainage and restored clearance, will hold on this ground, and that the record has to name where the zone stops. Where a bait system is used instead of or beside it, the same discipline applies to the servicing: stations installed and then left unmonitored have not been used, and on a large lot with mature planting they are also easy to lose.

## How does the spring flight read on a wooded acre?

Ambiguously, unless you note where it came out of — and on a lot this size that is a real question rather than a formality.

Eastern subterranean termites swarm from March through June, generally in daylight and often on a warm afternoon after rain. It is the single occasion each year when an insect that otherwise stays out of sight presents itself, and it carries one specific piece of information: winged reproductives are only produced by a colony that has had several years to mature. A flight is therefore not the beginning of the problem. It is evidence that something mature has been established nearby for a while.

On a compact lot, near the house and on the property amount to much the same statement. On an acre of wooded ground they do not.

**A flight from a stump, a landscape timber, a fence line or open soil well out from the house** puts a colony somewhere on the parcel. That is worth knowing and worth acting on, because a colony on the property forages outward, but it is not a finding about the structure.

**A flight that starts inside** — at a slab crack, along a skirting, around a window frame, at the foot of a basement post — means the colony has reached the building, since those insects emerged from it. That changes the urgency completely.

**Wings with no insects** is the version most people actually get, because a flight is short and usually happens while the house is empty. What remains is wing litter — several dozen identical translucent wings, a quarter of an inch each, on a sill, in a corner web, in a window well, or on the floor under a window. In a large house with rooms that go unused for days, that scatter can sit for weeks before anybody registers it.

Two habits make this readable rather than alarming. The first is looking in the right places in the right month: basement window wells, sills on the shaded elevation, and the garage, through April and May, before anything is vacuumed. The second is keeping specimens. A few taped to a card or dropped into a sealed bag is the most useful thing anyone can hand over, because separating a termite from a carpenter ant alate — straight bead-like antennae against elbowed ones, a broad waist against a pinched one, four matched wings against two unequal pairs — takes thirty seconds with a specimen and is unanswerable without.

## Where does damage concentrate on a two-storey 1970s house?

At the lowest timber, at the joints between separate pours, and behind whatever was finished later.

**The sill plate and band joist.** The first framing above the foundation, running continuously around a long perimeter. This is the most consistently affected element on the stock and among the least often seen, because beds are planted against it outside and the basement is finished inside.

**The garage slab joint.** A garage floor is generally poured separately from the house foundation, so a joint runs between them. Where the house carries a room above the garage, that joint lies under a timber floor, so anything travelling through it is inside the building without ever showing on an outside wall.

**Deck posts and ledgers.** A post driven into the ground is timber in soil by design. The ledger bolted through the band joist stays wet at its interface, and what sits behind it suits carpenter ants every bit as well as termites, so a single repair settles two problems. That side of it is covered at [ant control](/pest-control/ant-control/), with the [carpenter
      ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) profile alongside.

**Bay and box window projections.** Original features on much of this stock, cantilevered or carried on a small footing, with a floor structure close to grade and a flat roof detail above that has been leaking quietly since the 1990s.

**Joist ends and girder bearings** where framing meets masonry, and the base of any post bearing on a slab at grade.

**Framing behind the finished basement,** which is where honesty matters. Studwork, insulation and board against a foundation conceal the inside face from view, so the most readable evidence in the building has been covered over. On those houses the survey works harder at the plant room, the access panels and the exterior, and the report names the elevations nobody saw rather than implying a clean bill.

The damage signature is worth knowing. These insects feed with the grain, taking the softer spring wood first, so a cut section reads as layered. The galleries are dirty and enclosed rather than smooth and swept, and that is the distinction you can see against carpenter ant work. They also hollow a member from within and leave the outer skin standing, so a joist can be mostly gone behind a face that still looks right.

## What does irrigation do that rain does not?

It waters the same square foot on a schedule, for years, whatever the weather is doing.

This earns its own heading on this stock because irrigation is close to universal on large lots here and it is the moisture source least likely to be connected to a finding. Rain is intermittent, wind-driven and spread across a property. An irrigation zone is a fixed set of heads throwing a fixed arc at a fixed hour, four or more mornings a week from April to October, and the arc frequently takes in the bottom two feet of a building.

What that produces is specific and local: one elevation, often one corner, where the cladding never fully dries between cycles, where the mulch stays dark, and where the soil under the bed holds moisture through the driest week of August. That is not weather. It is a permanent damp patch installed by a landscaping contractor and paid for monthly.

The same logic covers the other scheduled water on these properties. A pool backwash discharging to the same spot. A condensate line dripping onto a bed for six months of the year. A dehumidifier pump run out through a rim joist and terminated a foot from the wall. Each is small, continuous and predictable, and continuous and predictable is exactly what a colony works from.

The corrections are ordinary maintenance. Turn the heads away from the building or cap and reposition them. Extend the condensate and pump discharges out beyond the foundation. Take the backwash somewhere else. Lower the bed. Then look again a year later, because on a property with a landscaping contract the heads are adjusted by somebody who was never told.

## What should a report on this stock contain?

Measurements and interruptions, plus a note of everything the survey could not reach.

A report worth having on a property here reads as a list of located items. The height of grade at each elevation, taken with a rule and set beside the three-inch and eight-inch figures rather than called satisfactory. The termination of every leader, and the condition of the ground beneath it. Which slabs, decks, terraces, additions and detached buildings cross the perimeter, and how each was handled. The state of the garage slab joint. Whether anybody actually saw the inside of the foundation wall, and where they could not. Any wood-to-soil contact, photographed. Any buried or stacked timber on the lot, the wood line included. The irrigation zones that reach a building. And whichever elevation holds the wettest ground, because the next finding will be there.

The annual re-check runs to a short list on this stock and it is always the same one. Has the mulch climbed back up? Does the leader still discharge where it was left? Has anything been built tight to a building since — a terrace extension, a deck, a shed, a generator pad, a raised bed? Has anything been dug through the treated ground? And have the irrigation heads been turned?
