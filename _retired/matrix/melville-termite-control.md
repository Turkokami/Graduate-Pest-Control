## Who owns the soil the treatment goes into?

On roughly a quarter of Melville's housing, not the person who found the tube — and that single fact reorders the whole job.

Some 1,710 units here, 22.8 per cent of about 7,489, are attached, with 960 more in buildings of five units or more. In almost every arrangement of that kind the land around the building, the foundation itself and the structure below the finished floor are common elements. The owner's demise begins somewhere around the interior face of the wall. That means an owner who finds discarded wings on a sill can commission an inspection of their own dwelling, and cannot commission a continuous treated zone around the building it sits in, because the ground it would go into is not theirs to authorise work on.

This is not a legal technicality to be worked around. It is the reason so much attached-housing termite work in this hamlet is done badly. What tends to happen is that one owner acts alone, a limited application is made to whatever soil that owner is understood to control, and the result is a treated segment across one frontage in a perimeter that continues past the party wall in both directions. A subterranean colony does not recognise a demise line. It forages under the row, and it will use the untreated ground on either side of a treated strip without altering its behaviour at all.

So the first deliverable on an attached building is a written specification the association can act on: the perimeter described as a whole, the attached slabs and stoops identified, the access constraints named, the sequence set out, and the owner of each item stated. That document is the thing a board can put in front of its members. The same principle governs the sealing work on this stock and is developed at [structural exclusion in Melville](/pest-control/melville/structural-exclusion/).

On the 59 per cent of the stock that is detached — the streets rising toward West Hills, Sweet Hollow, the older frontage along Old Country Road — the ownership question disappears and the technical one gets harder, because those buildings carry the layered alterations of several decades.

## Where does the water come from in a hamlet with none?

From equipment, and in Melville a great deal of that equipment is on a timer.

The census area covers 12.11 square miles with no water area recorded at all. The hamlet sits at about 135 feet on the southern flank of the high ground that carries Jayne's Hill, the island's highest point at 400.9 feet by Suffolk County's measure, a mile north in West Hills County Park. Water leaves this ground downward and southward across the outwash. Nothing here holds it naturally.

Which means every damp soil contact a colony is using was manufactured, and on this stock the manufactured sources have a distinctly Melville accent.

**Irrigation on managed grounds.** A townhouse or condominium property is landscaped as a whole and watered by a controller nobody living there has ever seen. Heads get knocked out of alignment by mowers, by children and by snow, and a misaimed head throws onto a foundation, a veneer face or a stoop several mornings a week for twenty years. It is the most consistent single moisture fault we find on attached housing in this hamlet, and correcting it takes a screwdriver.

**Mulched common beds.** Managed grounds get topped up on a contract every spring. Nobody measures the level, and after fifteen contracts the bed has climbed several inches up the wall.

**Condensate and cooling plant.** Newer construction here is fully air-conditioned, so every unit has a condensate path, and on stacked buildings there is a common condensate line with a pump on it. Both discharge somewhere, and where they discharge is where the ground stays wet through the cooling season.

**Roof drainage on a shared roof.** A long row is served by a long gutter with few outlets. When one outlet blocks, the overflow does not land on one owner's border; it lands on whichever unit is at the low point.

**Hardscape pitched the wrong way.** Patios, walks, stoops and parking-court aprons laid against the building and settled over two decades now return water to the wall.

Each of those is correctable, has an identifiable owner, and needs correcting before an application is worth making. A treated zone under a wall that will be watered on Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday mornings has not addressed the reason the colony chose that elevation.

## What did the corridor build on, and what is still under it?

Farm ground, and farm ground carries wood.

Melville was agricultural within living memory of the street pattern. It was called Sunsquams, then Samuel Ketcham's Valley, then Sweet Hollow, with the name Melville appearing in school records in 1854; a Presbyterian church stood from 1829 at the Old Country Road and Sweet Hollow Road intersection, went dormant between 1930 and 1944, and was moved a mile west in 1977. Canon USA's regional headquarters, a 668,296-square-foot building opened in 2013 at a reported half a billion dollars, was put up on what had been a pumpkin farm.

Ground with that history holds the things Cornell Cooperative Extension asks a homeowner to remove: wooden debris, including buried stumps. Hedgerow and orchard stumps left below grade when a field was cleared. Fence posts along an old boundary that later became a rear lot line. Farm outbuilding sills, footings and buried timber. Then the construction layers added their own — form boards left in a backfill, grade stakes never pulled, offcuts buried because burying was faster than hauling, and on the commercial parcels the timber shoring and cribbing that goes into a large excavation.

None of that is visible on an exterior inspection and all of it sustains a colony inside foraging range of a building. The practical instruction is narrow: on a Melville lot, ask what stood here before the house, look along the old field lines that survive as rear fences, and get every piece of stacked or buried timber off the property. A woodpile against a garage wall is not itself a colony; it is a bridge from one to the building.

## What does a treated zone actually meet on this stock?

Interruptions, and the newer the building the more of them there are.

The soil is helpful. Free-draining outwash sand and gravel is uniform, workable and predictable across a lot, without the boulder-and-clay unpredictability that makes the moraine villages difficult. The foundations are helpful too: poured concrete with discrete, findable penetrations rather than open mortar joints. What is unhelpful is everything built against the buildings.

**Attached slabs.** Patios, walks, stoops, garage aprons, parking-court aprons and the plaza slabs on commercial parcels all break the soil continuity at the wall. Reaching the soil beneath them means working through them, and a proposal that quietly leaves them out has left a gap the width of the slab with no note that it exists.

**Slab-built wings and garages.** On the newer housing here the garage, the rear wing and often the whole ground floor sit on a slab poured separately from the main foundation. Every separate pour makes a joint, and a joint is a concealed route from soil to whatever the slab carries.

**Attached structures with their own footings.** Decks, sunrooms, screened porches, generator pads and condenser pads each bring a footing, a junction with the original wall and their own soil line.

**Service trenches.** On attached housing the trench was dug once for the whole row, so it crosses the perimeter of every unit. Anything excavated afterwards — a fibre run, an irrigation main, a car charger circuit — cuts a treated zone and nobody tells the pest contractor.

**Grade that has risen.** Where a bed has been built up over twenty springs, the soil against the wall sits well above where the original grade line was, which changes where a treated zone has to be established for it to mean anything.

The honest position is that a recorded, continuous treated zone plus corrected drainage and clearance is durable on this ground, and that a treatment applied across a perimeter crossed by three slabs, a deck, a condenser pad and a common trench, with no note of where the gaps sit, is not. The gaps will not announce themselves for years. The discipline itself is set out at [termite control](/pest-control/termite-control/), and the insect at [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/).

## Why is a finished basement the hardest room in Melville?

Because on much of this stock it was finished before anyone had ever looked at the wall behind it.

On a 1958 house the basement was finished later, by an owner, and there is usually a mechanical room, an access panel or an unfinished corner where the foundation is still visible. On a house built in 2004 with a finished lower level as part of the original specification, the framing, insulation and wallboard went up against the poured wall during construction. The interior face of the foundation — the surface where a mud tube is most legible — has never been seen by anybody, including the people who built it.

That matters because it removes the best diagnostic surface in the building. Cornell describes tubes running from the diameter of a wheat straw to wider than a thumb, and on a poured wall in an unfinished cellar they are unmistakable. Behind a finished wall they are invisible, and the first indication is a swarmer emerging through a baseboard, a floor joint or the frame of a basement window.

Three responses follow, and a proposal ought to state which it is taking. Work the surfaces that are still available: the mechanical room, the underside of the stair, behind any access panel, the sump and the perimeter of any penetration. Work the exterior harder, because it is now carrying the whole inspection. And name the elevations that were not seen, in writing, rather than describing the building as clear. A report on a Melville property with a finished lower level that lists no unseen surfaces has not been honest about its own limits.

The warm corner is worth a deliberate look wherever the wall is visible. Cornell notes that termites favour heat from furnaces, chimneys and hot water pipes, particularly in winter, so activity found in a cold perimeter in January carries more weight than the same finding in July, when the whole wall is hospitable.

## What does a corridor building change about the same insect?

The scale, the surfaces and the identity of the person who has to authorise anything.

A campus building on Route 110 has the same relationship with soil that a house does, expressed differently. Slab-on-grade over a large footprint, with expansion joints, cold joints, plumbing and conduit penetrations, and elevator and lift pits below the slab. Landscaped courtyards and planted beds held against the building by a raised kerb. Irrigation across an area measured in acres. Plaza slabs and paving laid up to a curtain wall so the wall's junction with the ground is entirely concealed. Loading docks with a pit, a leveller and a drain in it. And on the older parcels, timber that has been in the ground since the site was cleared.

The evidence is harder to find and much more consequential when missed, because the affected framing on a commercial fit-out is often not framing at all but the timber blocking, nailers, door bucks and casework that sit against the slab and the wall. A pest report on such a building that covers the perimeter landscaping and stops has looked at the least interesting part of the site.

The organisational difference is that no single person can act. Building owner, managing agent, facilities contractor, landscape contractor and tenant each control part of the problem and none controls all of it, and the tenant who reports the swarmers usually controls the least. That is precisely the situation the [commercial programme](/commercial/) is built for, and the reason a written specification with named responsibilities is more useful on a corridor parcel than any amount of field time.

## What does a spring flight mean on a block of identical dwellings?

Less than the neighbours think, and more than the one owner who saw it indoors thinks.

Eastern subterranean termites swarm March through June. On a row of dwellings that share an age, a soil, a landscaping contract and a drainage design, a warm still afternoon in April can produce a flight that several households notice within an hour of each other. The reaction is predictable and unhelpful in both directions: some conclude the block has an outbreak, others conclude that because everybody saw it nothing is specific to them.

The correction is about location rather than quantity. A swarm is a mature colony reproducing, and maturity takes years, so the flight is never the beginning of the problem. Swarmers observed outdoors — over a lawn, around a stump, out of a landscaped bed or a stretch of common ground — establish that a colony is on the property. Swarmers emerging indoors, out of a floor joint, from around a basement window frame, through a baseboard or at the base of a post, establish that a colony has a route into the structure, because those insects came out of the building. On attached housing the second case is the one that has to be escalated to the association, because the structure it refers to is the row.

What to do in the first ten minutes is more useful than what to do in the first week. Do not vacuum everything away. Tape half a dozen specimens to a card or seal them in a bag, and photograph the wings where they lie. Cornell describes discarded wings about a quarter of an inch long, collected at windows and light fittings where the swarmers gathered, and the wing scatter frequently outlasts the insects by weeks. Note which room, which elevation and what time of day. That short record is worth more to an inspection than a description of the numbers involved.

The confusion worth heading off is with carpenter ant alates, which fly around here in the same weeks and are an entirely different job. Termite swarmers have a broad waist with no pinch, straight beaded antennae and four wings of roughly equal length; a winged ant has a pinched waist, elbowed antennae and forewings noticeably longer than the pair behind them.

## What does the pre-corridor housing need that the newer stock does not?

A survey that starts at the ground and assumes six decades of quiet alteration.

About 1,153 Melville units date from the 1950s and 1,103 from the 1960s, with a further 279 standing since before 1940. Those houses sit along Sweet Hollow, on the Old Country Road frontage, out toward Round Swamp Road and up the streets toward West Hills, and they are structurally unrelated to the townhouse rows and the 2000s subdivisions.

**The foundation is low and the grade is not.** Post-war construction did not leave much freeboard between the top of the wall and the ground, and six decades of beds, edging, paving and re-grading have taken most of what there was. This is where the three-inch clearance goes first, and it goes without anybody making a decision.

**The basement is usually unfinished somewhere.** That is an advantage rather than a defect. The inside face of a poured wall is the most legible termite surface in any building, and on this layer of the stock a good deal of it can still be seen with a light and a bit of shifting of stored goods.

**Additions arrived one at a time.** A rear extension in the seventies, a deck in the nineties, a sunroom later, a patio after that. Each has its own footing, its own junction with the original wall and its own soil line, and none of them appears on a drawing anybody kept.

**Landscape timber is still in the ground.** Raised beds and edging built from timber in the seventies and eighties survive on a great many of these lots, frequently within a few feet of the wall, and it is wood in soil by design.

**Porches and steps conceal contacts.** Concrete steps poured against a house and later re-faced, and porch structures with a void beneath, both hide a soil-to-timber contact under something that reads as solid.

None of that makes the older layer worse. It makes it legible in ways the newer layer is not, and the corrections are correspondingly cheaper: cut the bed back, extend the leader, lift the timber out, open the porch void. On a 2004 townhouse the equivalent findings are behind a finished wall and under a slab.

## What should a Melville report actually say?

Where the water is, who owns each item, and what was not seen.

A useful report on this stock reads as a set of located findings rather than a treatment description. The measured grade against each elevation, tested against the three-inch and eight-inch clearances rather than described as acceptable. Every irrigation zone that reaches the building and what was done about it. The discharge point of every leader, condensate line and pump. Which slabs, decks, pads and additions cross the perimeter and how each was addressed. Whether the interior face of the foundation was visible and on which elevations it was not. Any wood-to-soil contact found, photographed. Buried or stacked timber on the lot. And on attached housing, the row plan: which units, which common elements, which board decision is required for each item.

The re-check a year later asks a short set of questions, and on this stock they are not the questions a detached-house checklist would ask. Has the grounds contract re-mulched the beds up the wall? Has an irrigation head moved? Has anything new been built or bolted against the building — a pad, a deck, a bike store, a package locker? And has anyone trenched across the frontage for a service?
