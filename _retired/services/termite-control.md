## What kind of termite actually attacks Long Island houses?

The eastern subterranean termite, *Reticulitermes flavipes*. It is the species of consequence across Long Island and the wider Northeast, and understanding what it is explains almost every decision in how the work is done.

It is a social insect living in a colony based in soil. The colony contains workers — pale, soft-bodied, blind insects that do all the feeding and construction — along with soldiers with darkened heads and enlarged mandibles, and reproductives. It digests cellulose with the help of microorganisms in its gut, which is why it can live on wood at all. Colonies grow slowly and mature colonies can be very large, foraging over an area considerably wider than the footprint of a single house.

Two characteristics dominate everything else.

The first is that the workers are extremely vulnerable to drying out. They have thin cuticles and they die quickly in dry, open air. Everything they build is in service of maintaining humidity: they travel through soil, through the interior of wood, or through enclosed mud tubes, and they will not simply walk across an exposed foundation wall. This is why the visible evidence takes the specific forms it does.

The second is that the colony's centre of gravity is the soil, not the building. The insects in your sill plate are foragers from a colony that lives in the ground and needs continuing contact with soil moisture. That is why treatment strategy is directed at the soil and at the foraging population rather than only at the damaged timber, and it is why removing and replacing a damaged board resolves nothing at all.

Drywood termites, which nest entirely within the wood they are eating and need no soil contact, are a southern and coastal-southwestern problem rather than a New York one. If you have termites in Suffolk or Nassau County, assume subterranean until proven otherwise.

## How do you tell a termite swarmer from a flying ant?

This is the identification homeowners most often need, because the swarm is usually the first thing anyone sees, and mistaking one for the other costs a season.

Look at three things.

**The waist.** A termite has a broad, straight-sided body with no constriction between thorax and abdomen. An ant has a sharply pinched waist, so much so that it reads as two separate segments joined by a thread. This is the fastest field check and the most reliable.

**The antennae.** Termite antennae are straight and made up of small bead-like segments. Ant antennae are elbowed: a long first segment, then a distinct bend.

**The wings.** A termite swarmer has four wings of roughly equal length and shape, considerably longer than its body, held flat over the back. A winged ant's forewings are noticeably larger than its hindwings.

There is a fourth clue that is diagnostic on its own. Termite swarmers shed their wings very readily, often within minutes of landing, so what you frequently find is not the insects but a scatter of small, uniform, translucent wings on a windowsill, a sill track, a web in a corner, or the floor beneath a window. Ants do not leave piles of shed wings in that way.

Colour helps less than people think. Eastern subterranean swarmers are dark — brown to nearly black — with pale wings, and plenty of ants are dark too. Rely on the waist and the antennae.

If you have found them, keep a few. Tape a couple to a card, drop them into a sealed bag or a small jar, or take a close, well-lit photograph with something for scale. That specimen is the most useful thing you can hand an inspector, and it is often gone by the time anyone arrives. The comparison with carpenter ants matters too, because those are also a spring event around here and they are a wholly different job — see [ant control](/pest-control/ant-control/) for that side of it.

## Why does the spring swarm matter more than anything else you will see?

Because it is the one event in the year when an insect that lives entirely out of sight puts itself in front of you, and because of what it implies about the colony's age.

A colony produces winged reproductive swarmers only once it has reached a certain maturity, which takes several years of growth. So a swarm is not the beginning of your termite problem. It is evidence that a mature colony has been established near or in your structure for some time. That is the single most important thing to understand about it, and it is why "we saw a few and then they went away" is not a reassuring report.

On Long Island the swarm typically occurs in spring, and eastern subterranean termites swarm in daylight, frequently on a warm day after rain. The swarmers emerge, fly weakly, drop their wings, pair off and attempt to found new colonies. The overwhelming majority die. That is fine from the colony's perspective and it is why the numbers are large.

The location of the swarm is diagnostic in a way that is easy to miss. Swarmers emerging **outdoors** in the yard, from a stump, from landscape timbers or from soil away from the house, tell you there is a colony on the property. Swarmers emerging **indoors** — from a crack in a slab, along a baseboard, from a window frame, from the base of a post in a cellar — tell you the colony is in contact with the structure itself, because those insects came out of your building. That distinction changes the urgency considerably.

Because swarms are brief, seasonal and frequently occur while people are at work, most of them are never observed directly. What survives is the wing scatter, which is why late winter through spring is the right time to look at windowsills, cellar window wells and the corners of a finished basement.

## What is a mud tube telling you?

That there is a live route between soil and wood, and roughly where it goes.

Because workers dry out in open air, they build shelter tubes from soil particles, faecal material and saliva to bridge any exposed surface they must cross. The result is a pencil-width to inch-wide ridge of dried mud running up a foundation wall, along a pier, up the face of a block wall, over a slab edge, along a joist, or down from framing to the ground. They are the most characteristic sign of subterranean termite activity and the one most likely to be visible without dismantling anything.

Different tubes tell you different things.

**Working tubes** run from soil to wood and carry traffic. These are the ones that matter most.

**Exploratory tubes** extend from soil and end in mid-air without reaching anything. They represent the colony searching, and they indicate foraging pressure in that area even though that particular route came to nothing.

**Drop tubes** hang from infested wood down toward the soil, built from the wood end rather than the ground end. They tell you the wood above is already occupied.

**Shelter or transition sheets** are flattened, spreading deposits where termites bridge a wider surface rather than a narrow one.

There is a straightforward field test for whether a tube is live. Break out a short section, leave the surrounding tube intact, and come back in several days. If it has been repaired, the route is in current use. If it has not, either the route is abandoned or the foraging pattern has shifted — which is a reason to look elsewhere, not a reason to relax.

Tubes are most often found where they are hardest to see: on the interior face of a foundation wall behind stored goods, in a crawlspace, behind insulation, behind a finished basement wall, or on the inside of a hollow block wall where nothing is visible at all. That last case is common and it is one of several reasons a visual perimeter walk is not a termite inspection.

## Why is soil-to-wood contact the thing that matters?

Because it removes the only barrier the insect actually has to overcome.

A subterranean termite in soil next to a house has to reach cellulose without crossing dry, exposed air. If untreated wood is in direct contact with the ground, it does not have to build anything or expose itself at all; it simply moves from soil into timber. Every point of soil-to-wood contact is therefore a free entry, and finding those points is the backbone of an inspection.

The recurring ones on Long Island housing stock:

**Grade too high against the structure.** Soil, mulch or a raised planting bed built up over the years until it covers the top of the foundation and touches siding or the sill. This is probably the single most common finding, and it is created gradually by landscaping rather than by construction.

**Porch and stoop construction.** Concrete steps and porches poured against a house frequently contain fill and voids, and framing behind or beneath them is often in contact with soil that no one can see.

**Wooden posts set in the ground.** Deck posts, fence posts abutting the house, carport supports and porch columns bearing directly on soil or on a footing at grade.

**Attached structures.** Decks, sunrooms and additions where a ledger board sits low, where framing meets an existing wall without proper flashing, and where the transition is buried behind trim.

**Buried construction debris and form boards.** Timber left in backfill against a foundation, and grade stakes and forms never pulled after the pour. These maintain a colony immediately adjacent to the structure.

**Slab construction.** Expansion joints, plumbing and conduit penetrations, cold joints and cracks in a slab all provide concealed routes from soil into the framing above, with nothing visible on the exterior at all.

**Crawlspaces.** Low clearance, wood stored on the ground, a missing or damaged vapour barrier, and framing close to a damp soil floor.

Moisture is the amplifier running through all of it. Termites are drawn to consistently damp conditions, wet wood is easier to attack and decays alongside the termite damage, and a damp crawlspace or a chronically wet foundation corner is where activity concentrates. That is why gutters, downspout discharge, grading, condensate lines and slow plumbing leaks are termite findings and appear in our reports.

## Where does the damage concentrate?

At the lowest wood in the structure, closest to the soil, and preferentially where it is damp.

**Sill plates.** The horizontal member sitting directly on top of the foundation. It is the first framing a termite reaches after leaving the soil and it is the most consistently affected element on Long Island houses. It is also structural, which is why damage there matters disproportionately.

**Band and rim joists.** The perimeter joist closing the floor system at the sill line. Damage here often travels a considerable distance along the perimeter before anything is visible inside.

**Joist ends and girders.** Particularly where a joist end is pocketed into a masonry wall, which is a damp, enclosed, unventilated location that suits them very well.

**Posts, columns and their bases**, especially where a post bears on a slab or a footing at grade level.

**Framing around plumbing penetrations**, where moisture and access coincide.

**Door and window framing at grade level**, and the framing behind porch and stoop construction.

**Subflooring and interior trim** in advanced cases, along with, occasionally, paper-faced materials and stored cardboard and books in a cellar — the paper is cellulose too.

The signature of the damage is characteristic. Subterranean termites eat along the grain, following the softer spring wood and leaving the harder growth rings behind, so a cut section looks layered or honeycombed. The galleries are typically lined and spotted with soil and faecal material, which is a key distinction from carpenter ants: ant galleries are clean, smooth-walled and free of mud, and ants push debris out as coarse frass. Termite galleries are dirty and closed.

Crucially, they work from the inside out and leave a thin surface layer intact. A joist can be substantially hollow with an apparently sound face. Paint over affected wood may blister, dimple or show faint wandering lines. Trim can look perfect and give way under a screwdriver.

## How is a termite inspection actually done?

Slowly, from the ground up, with hands on the structure rather than eyes on it.

**The exterior perimeter.** The full foundation line, looking for tubes, checking where grade sits relative to the top of the foundation and the siding, examining porch and stoop junctions, deck posts and ledgers, hose bibs and condensate discharge, downspout outlets, and any wood in ground contact. Also expansion joints and cracks at slab edges.

**The interior of the foundation.** In a cellar, the entire interior face of the wall, the sill and band joist all the way round, the top of piers and columns, joist pockets and any point where framing meets masonry. Stored goods have to be moved away from the walls for this to mean anything. Finished basement walls conceal exactly the surfaces that need looking at, and there are limits to what can be seen without opening them — we say so rather than implying otherwise.

**Crawlspaces.** Entered rather than looked into. Piers, the underside of the floor system, the vapour barrier, the soil surface, and any wood on the ground.

**Sounding and probing.** Tapping framing with the handle of a tool and listening for the dull, hollow note that indicates loss of section, then probing suspect areas with an awl or screwdriver. Damage that is invisible is frequently obvious to a probe, and this is the part of the inspection that separates a real one from a walk-through.

**Moisture assessment.** Where water is sitting, where it is coming from, and how the drainage behaves. A moisture meter on suspect framing is useful; so is looking at the gutters.

**Attics, upper storeys and unexpected places** where circumstances warrant it, because tubes and damage occasionally turn up well above grade where a persistent moisture source has drawn activity upward.

An inspection produces a diagram and a written finding, because the location of activity relative to structure is the whole point. What it cannot produce is proof of absence — no visual inspection can see inside a closed wall or beneath a slab, and any report that implies otherwise is overstating what the method can do.

## What treatment approaches are used?

Two principal ones, used separately or together, and both directed at the soil and the foraging population rather than at the individual insects you happened to see.

**A continuous treated zone in the soil.** A liquid termiticide is applied to the soil around and, where required, beneath the structure to create a treated barrier between the colony and the building. In practice this means trenching along the foundation, rodding where trenching alone cannot reach, and drilling and treating through slabs, porches, patios and attached concrete where the soil beneath them cannot otherwise be reached. Its strength is that it addresses the whole perimeter at once and acts quickly. Its weakness is that its effectiveness depends entirely on the continuity of the treated zone — an untreated gap, an obstruction, a later excavation or a disturbance of the treated soil can compromise it. Application is governed by the product label and by New York State regulation, and it is not work for anyone without the appropriate licensing.

**Baiting systems.** In-ground stations are installed around the structure at intervals, containing a material — commonly an insect growth regulator — that foraging workers consume and transfer through the colony. The strength is that it exploits the colony's own behaviour to affect the population at its source, uses very little material, and works on structures where a continuous soil treatment is impractical. The weakness is that it takes time: workers must find the stations, feed and transfer, and stations must be serviced on a schedule for the system to mean anything. A bait system that is installed and then never monitored has not been used.

**Direct treatment of infested wood or voids** may accompany either approach where there is an accessible active gallery or an above-ground infestation.

**Physical correction** sits alongside both: removing wood-to-soil contact, lowering grade away from the foundation, removing buried debris, replacing structurally compromised members, and correcting the moisture path. This last category is where our approach differs from a company selling only an application, and it is why a proposal from us often lists carpentry and drainage items next to the treatment items.

We do not name products, and we do not publish prices for this work because the drilling, the access constraints, the linear footage and the moisture correction differ enormously from house to house. What we will do is describe exactly what a given structure needs and why.

## Why is moisture correction part of the job?

Because the conditions that make a structure attractive to termites are conditions you can change, and the treatment lasts longer when you do.

Chronic damp does several things at once. It softens and decays wood, which fungal decay does independently of termites and which then compounds the structural loss. It maintains the humidity that foraging workers require, letting them persist in places that would otherwise be inhospitable. It concentrates activity — the wettest corner of a crawlspace is very often where the tubes are. And it makes the same framing attractive to other structural pests, so a wet band joist that has termites this year has carpenter ants the year after.

The sources are ordinary. Gutters that overflow or discharge at the foundation. Grade sloping toward the house. A downspout emptying into a splash block that has tipped. Failed flashing at a roof-wall junction or a deck ledger. A crawlspace with no vapour barrier and blocked vents. Condensation on uninsulated lines. A supply or waste leak running slowly for years inside a wall.

Correcting these is not adjacent to termite control; it is part of it. It is also the part with the longest tail of benefit, because it improves the building's resistance to the next colony as well as this one. The same argument in its general form is set out under [structural exclusion](/pest-control/structural-exclusion/), which is the discipline this practice is built on.

## Why is termite damage so often found late?

Because every property of the insect works against early discovery.

The colony is in the soil, out of sight. The workers are blind, pale and stay enclosed — inside soil, inside tubes, inside wood — so you do not encounter them on a counter or a floor the way you encounter ants or cockroaches. They eat from the inside out and leave the surface intact, so damaged framing looks fine. The affected areas are the least visited parts of a house: crawlspaces, the back of a cellar behind stored boxes, the framing behind a finished basement wall, the underside of a porch. And the one clearly visible annual event, the swarm, lasts a very short time and often happens when no one is home.

The result is that a significant proportion of termite damage on Long Island is found during a renovation, a real estate transaction, or the investigation of an apparently unrelated problem — a sagging floor, a sticking door, a soft spot underfoot near an exterior wall.

The practical implications follow directly. Look at windowsills and cellar window wells in spring. Keep grade and mulch down off the siding, and keep firewood, timber and stored lumber away from the foundation. Keep stored goods off the perimeter walls of a cellar so the sill line can actually be seen. Fix the water. And have the structure looked at annually rather than waiting for a reason, because the difference between finding this in year three and finding it in year eight is measured in framing.
