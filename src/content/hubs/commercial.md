## What is actually at stake when a commercial building has a pest problem?

Almost never the insect. What is at stake is an obligation, an inspection, a complaint that is now in writing, or an exposure that someone will eventually be asked to account for.

That is the first thing worth being honest about, because it changes what a good outcome looks like. A homeowner who stops seeing mice considers the matter closed. A board that stops receiving complaints has solved nothing if it cannot demonstrate what it did, when, and on whose advice. The two jobs use much of the same fieldcraft and produce completely different deliverables.

The stakes usually arrive in one of four shapes. There is a statutory duty — most obviously in a New York City multiple dwelling, where the responsibility to address infestation sits with ownership rather than with the resident who noticed it. There is an audit, which may be a health department inspection with a scoring consequence, or a third-party scheme where a documented pest programme is a scored element and a gap is a finding whether or not anything is actually crawling. There is a complaint chain, which begins as a phone call to a super and becomes, over some months, a letter from an attorney or a filed complaint. And there is straightforward liability, which in practice concentrates around bed bugs, rodent contamination of food, and anything that causes an injury.

None of those are resolved by a technician arriving, treating and leaving. They are resolved by changing the building's condition and being able to show that you did. That is what a programme is for, and it is why the reporting on a commercial account is a deliverable rather than an afterthought — someone other than the person paying the invoice is going to read it, possibly under adversarial circumstances, possibly two years later when nobody remembers the detail.

The upside of taking that view is that it aligns the compliance objective with the actual pest objective. The building conditions an inspector marks you down for — an unsealed penetration, a failed door sweep, standing water in a refuse area, a gap at a loading dock — are the same conditions sustaining the population. Fix them and both problems recede together. Treat around them and you are managing two problems forever.

## Why does a multi-unit building need building-wide work instead of unit-by-unit treatment?

Because in a shared structure the population is not in a unit. It is in the building, and the units are simply where it becomes visible.

Consider how a pre-war apartment building is actually put together. Plumbing chases run vertically from cellar to roof, and unless they were firestopped and sealed at every floor — which in older stock they frequently were not — they are continuous shafts connecting every kitchen and bathroom in the line. Electrical conduit, gas lines, steam risers and later low-voltage cable runs do the same thing in parallel. Horizontally, the joist bays between floors run wall to wall, and in an attached building the party wall often has open joist pockets connecting to the structure next door. Then there is the shared service infrastructure: compactor chutes and rooms, refuse staging, cellar corridors, sidewalk hatches, and the drain and sewer connection at the bottom of everything.

A German cockroach population living in that network appears in whichever apartment currently offers the best combination of water and harbourage. Treat that apartment and two things happen. The count there falls, and a portion of the population moves along the chase into adjacent units, which are usually the ones directly above and below. Six weeks later there are two new complaints and the file now shows that the problem is spreading despite service — a record that is actively unhelpful if anyone ever reads it critically.

Bed bugs behave similarly but on a longer clock, moving through wall voids, conduit and along baseboards, which is why assessing only the complaining apartment produces such poor results. Mice use the same vertical routes and add the exterior envelope. Rats characteristically come from below rather than from the street, through a deteriorated house trap or a broken cellar drain line, so a building convinced it has a street-level rat problem often has an interior break as the real source.

The alternative is to treat the network as the client. That means assessing a contiguous block of units around every report rather than the reporting unit alone, sealing the chases and pipe penetrations floor by floor, closing joist pockets at party walls, correcting the refuse handling, and fixing the drainage below. It is more work up front and materially cheaper over three years, because it ends rather than relocates. The physical detail of that work is covered under [structural exclusion](/pest-control/structural-exclusion/); the pest-specific approaches sit under [cockroach control](/pest-control/cockroach-control/), [bed bug treatment](/pest-control/bed-bug-treatment/) and [rodent control](/pest-control/rodent-control/).

## What does the NYC Housing Maintenance Code actually require of an owner?

It places the duty on ownership rather than on occupancy, and that single point reframes most of the conversations we have with boards.

The [NYC Housing Maintenance Code](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/pdf/HousingMaintenanceCode.pdf), at Title 27, Chapter 2 of the Administrative Code, sets the habitability standards for multiple dwellings in the city and includes the obligation to keep the premises free from pests and to take reasonable measures to eradicate an infestation when one exists. It is worth reading the text rather than a summary of it, which is why the document is linked here and in the sources below. We are a pest contractor, not your counsel, and a building's specific position should be confirmed with its attorney.

What follows practically is straightforward. A resident reporting an infestation is reporting a condition the owner is responsible for addressing, and "the tenant should call an exterminator" is not a position that survives contact with the code. Access matters too: a treatment programme that fails because units would not admit the technician still leaves the obligation in place, so access notices, refusals and re-attempts need to be recorded as carefully as the treatments themselves. In our experience that record is the single most common gap in a building's file, and it is the one that hurts most when a matter is disputed, because a building that tried repeatedly and was refused entry is in a very different position from a building that cannot show it tried at all.

The other consequence is scope. If the duty is the owner's and the pathways are shared, then the reasonable measure is by definition building-wide. A programme that only ever visits units which complain is, on its face, responsive rather than preventive, and it will read that way to anyone assessing it later.

## What does Local Law 55 add to that?

It adds a proactive inspection duty and it ties pests explicitly to indoor air quality, which changes both the frequency and the paperwork.

[Local Law 55 of 2018](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/buildings/local_laws/ll55of2018.pdf), the Asthma Free Housing Act, requires owners of multiple dwellings to inspect dwelling units for indoor allergen hazards — a category that includes pests along with mould and the conditions that produce them — and to remediate what is found. The framing is significant. Under the older model, pest work was reactive: a complaint arrived and a response followed. Under this one, the inspection itself is the obligation, and it recurs whether or not anyone has complained.

Two operational consequences follow. The first is that the inspection has to be scheduled, evidenced and recorded across the whole building, on a cycle, with the units that could not be accessed logged rather than quietly skipped. The second is that remediation has to address the underlying condition, not merely the visible pest, because a law framed around allergen hazards is inherently concerned with the moisture, the harbourage and the entry points rather than with a body count.

That is a fairly close match to how we would run a building anyway. The law asks for what a competent programme produces: a cyclical inspection, a written finding, a corrective action, and evidence that the condition was actually changed. Where buildings run into difficulty is not usually the field work but the file — the inspection happened, the work happened, and nothing on paper links the two in a way a third party can follow.

Again, read the law itself and take advice on your building's obligations. What we can do is make sure the pest side of the record is complete and legible when someone asks for it.

## What do auditors and inspectors actually look for?

Conditions and paperwork, roughly in that order, and much less often the pest itself.

A health inspector in a food environment is looking at evidence and at structure. Droppings and their distribution, gnawing on packaging and on the structure, live or dead insects in the wrong places, and then the conditions that permit them: gaps at doors and loading bays, failed sweeps, unsealed penetrations around plumbing and conduit, standing water, unmanaged grease, refuse handling, and food stored in a way that creates harbourage. Findings about conditions carry further than a single sighting, because a sighting is an event and a condition is a system.

A third-party scheme auditor works from the file first. They will ask for the programme documentation, the service history, the trend data, the corrective-action log and the qualifications of the contractor, and then walk the site to test whether the file describes reality. The specific failure that costs buildings points is the mismatch: a device map showing thirty-two stations when the site has twenty-eight, a corrective item marked closed that is visibly still open, a monitoring log with no activity recorded on a line where there is obvious activity. An auditor who finds one mismatch starts checking everything.

Both types share a preference that is worth stating explicitly. They would rather see a documented problem being actively driven down than a spotless record with nothing in it. A log showing an elevated count in a receiving area, a corrective action assigned to maintenance, a date, and three subsequent readings trending toward zero is strong evidence of a functioning programme. A log showing no activity anywhere for eighteen months invites the question of whether anyone is really looking — and in a building of any size, that scepticism is usually well founded.

The practical implication for how we write things up is that we record what we find, including the awkward findings, because a sanitised record is worth nothing precisely when you need it.

## What does documentation that survives an audit look like?

It has five parts, and the gap is almost always in the fourth.

**The service log.** Date, who attended, what areas were covered, what was found and what was done. Specific enough to be useful: "monitors checked, third-floor trash room, two German cockroach captures in station 14, none elsewhere on the floor" rather than "serviced building."

**The product record.** What was applied, where, at what rate, on what date, under what label. This is a legal record as much as an operational one, and in a food facility it is examined closely.

**The device map.** A current diagram of every monitor and station with its identifier, matched to the physical site. Maps go stale quickly whenever a building renovates, and a stale map is an audit finding in its own right.

**The corrective-action log.** This is the part buildings most often lack, and it is the part that determines whether the file demonstrates anything. Every structural or sanitation finding needs a named owner, a target date and a closure record with evidence. The critical detail is that many of these items do not belong to the pest contractor. Sealing a chase is a carpentry job. Regrading a loading dock apron is a paving job. Replacing a compactor room door sweep is building maintenance. Without an assignment and a date, those items simply sit, and the field notes accumulate as a record of an unaddressed condition rather than of a programme.

**The trend.** Counts over time by area, so that the direction of travel is visible without anyone reading twelve months of notes. This is what a board actually needs to make a funding decision and what an auditor uses to judge whether the programme is working.

Reporting is written to the audience that has to act on it. A managing agent needs the operational detail and the list of what is still open. A board needs the trend, the risk position and a clear statement of what it is being asked to approve and why. Producing a single document and hoping it serves both is a reliable way to have a necessary repair sit unfunded through an entire budget cycle.

## How is a programme actually structured?

In four phases, and the first is longer than clients expect.

**Survey.** A full building assessment before any recurring schedule is set. Every common area, the cellar and mechanical spaces, refuse handling end to end, the loading and receiving areas, the exterior perimeter and drainage, and a meaningful sample of units or spaces rather than only the ones that have complained. The output is a baseline: what is present, where, at what intensity, by what route, and what conditions are sustaining it. Setting a service frequency before this exists is guesswork.

**Reduction.** Bringing the existing population down using whatever is appropriate to the species and the environment, with placement and product choice constrained by what the space is used for. This phase is intensive and finite. It is not the programme; it is the thing that has to happen before the programme can be judged.

**Correction.** The structural and sanitation work identified in the survey — sealing, door and dock hardware, drainage, refuse process, storage practice. Correction is where a programme either becomes durable or does not, and it is the phase most likely to stall, because it costs money in a different budget line and involves trades other than the pest contractor. Our part is to specify it precisely enough that it can be scoped and priced by whoever does it, then to verify it once done.

**Monitoring and verification.** A recurring cycle sized to the risk of the building rather than to a standard contract: monitors read and logged, conditions re-checked, the corrective log worked through, and reporting produced on a schedule the client's own governance can use. Frequency is higher in a food facility than in a residential building, and higher in the first year than the third if the correction phase was done properly. If it was not, the frequency never comes down, and that is the tell.

## Which kinds of operation is this work aimed at, and how do the stakes differ?

Five, and they share a method while differing sharply in what failure costs.

**Restaurants and food service** operate against health department inspection with a scoring consequence that is public and immediate. The pressure points are receiving, refuse, drains and the gaps around them, and the timeline is unforgiving because the consequence of a finding lands the same week.

**Food manufacturing and processing** operate against preventive-controls requirements and third-party audit schemes, where a documented pest programme is itself a scored element. The stake is not usually a closure but a customer: a failed audit can put a supply relationship at risk regardless of whether product was ever affected.

**Co-op and condo boards** face a statutory duty under the codes described above, plus a governance problem — a board is spending shareholders' money on a building-wide measure that most shareholders have not personally experienced a need for. That makes the trend reporting and the corrective log unusually important, because they are what a board actually presents to justify the spend.

**Property management** faces the same duties multiplied across a portfolio, with the added difficulty of consistency: several buildings, several supers, several ages of construction, and a need for the reporting to be comparable so that the portfolio can be triaged rather than handled building by building as complaints arrive.

**Hotels** face a liability and reputation problem concentrated almost entirely in one pest. A single confirmed bed bug report can outlive every other aspect of a property's performance, and brand-standard audits reflect that. The work is disproportionately about rapid, discreet assessment of rooms adjacent to a report and about a documented response that begins the same day.

## How does a complaint chain get handled before it becomes a legal matter?

By responding to the second report differently from the first, because the escalation almost always happens in the gap between them.

The pattern is remarkably consistent. A resident or a guest reports something to a super or a front desk. Someone attends, treats, and the immediate issue subsides. Weeks later the same person reports again, and this time the tone changes, because from their side the first response evidently failed. A third report is usually in writing. A fourth involves someone other than the building. At no point in that sequence does anyone necessarily do anything incompetent — but the record being created reads as a building that was told repeatedly and did not resolve it.

What breaks the pattern is treating the second report as a diagnostic event rather than a repeat service call. A second report from the same location means the first diagnosis was incomplete, and the correct response is to widen the assessment — the units or rooms adjacent, above and below, the chase serving them, the common space behind the wall — rather than to repeat the same treatment with more product. That single change resolves a large share of chains before they lengthen.

Three practices support it. Log every contact, including the ones that come in verbally to staff, because an undocumented first report is what later makes a building look unresponsive. Record access attempts and refusals with dates, since a unit that will not admit a technician is a material fact and the building needs to be able to show it tried. And close the loop with the person who reported: telling a resident what was found, what is being done and when the follow-up is scheduled removes most of the motivation to escalate, and costs nothing.

For boards and agents, the useful reframing is that complaint management and pest management are the same activity documented two ways. A complaint that is answered with a finding, an action and a date rarely becomes anything else.

## What tends to turn up in the first survey?

A short list, with tedious consistency, across buildings of every age and class.

Refuse handling is the most common single finding, and it is rarely about volume. It is about the interval between a bag leaving a unit and the material leaving the property, the condition of the compactor room floor and its drain, whether containers have lids that close, and whether the staging area is washed down. A building can be immaculate on every floor and be feeding a substantial population in one room in the cellar.

Doors are the second. Exterior doors, dock doors, cellar doors and compactor room doors with sweeps that have worn, lifted or been removed. A door that does not seal at the bottom corner is a rodent entrance and reads as a finding to any inspector who bends down.

Penetrations are the third and the most numerous. Every trade that has ever worked in the building cut a hole and very few of them sealed it afterwards. Renovation is the strongest predictor of a new problem in a previously stable building, and the file usually shows the correlation clearly once you look for it.

Water is the fourth. Condensation on uninsulated lines, slow leaks under sinks and behind fixtures, drains that hold, and standing water in refuse and mechanical areas. A cockroach population with a reliable water source is very difficult to starve out however good the housekeeping is.

Below-grade drainage is the fifth and the least visible. Deteriorated house traps, cracked or disconnected cellar lines, and unsealed floor drains connect the building directly to the sewer. When a rat problem persists through competent exterior work, this is where it usually is.
