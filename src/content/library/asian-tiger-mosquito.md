## What does an Asian tiger mosquito look like, and how do you know it is the one biting you?

Small, black, sharply marked with white — and biting you at three in the afternoon, which is the part that gives it away.

The identification is unusually easy for a mosquito. The body is black and the markings are crisp silvery-white rather than the muted browns and grays of most local species. The diagnostic feature is a single clean white stripe running down the center of the top of the thorax, from just behind the head backwards, like a racing stripe. The legs are boldly banded in black and white, and the last segment of the hind leg is white. It is noticeably smaller than the mosquitoes most people picture.

**The behavior is as diagnostic as the appearance.** This species bites during the day. It is most active in the morning and again through the afternoon into early evening, which contradicts the assumption that mosquitoes are a dusk problem and is the reason people describe being bitten while weeding, mowing, gardening or sitting on a shaded deck at two in the afternoon. It flies low, works around ankles and lower legs, approaches quietly, and is persistent — swatted at, it circles and comes back. It prefers shade, so the pressure is worst in the shrubbery, under the deck, beside the hedge and along the north side of the house rather than out in the open lawn.

**Against the other mosquitoes you meet here.** The dusk-and-dawn biters that come in from ditches, wet meadows and salt marsh are generally larger, less strongly marked, and arrive in waves after a wet stretch or a high tide. The species associated with catch basins and organically rich standing water are duller and less inclined to bite in daylight. If you are being bitten at midday in your own shrub border by something small and striped, you are almost certainly dealing with this one — and that is good news operationally, because it means the production site is very likely on the property you are standing on.

**Against non-biting look-alikes.** Crane flies are much larger, long-legged and harmless. Midges swarm in columns at dusk and do not bite. Neither has the central white stripe.

## Why does this species stay on your own property?

Because it breeds in containers rather than in wetland, and because it does not travel far from where it emerged.

Every mosquito has four life stages, and the first three are aquatic and effectively immobile — the egg, the larva and the pupa are stuck in whatever water they are in. Only the adult flies. The Asian tiger mosquito completes those first three stages in remarkably small volumes of water held in artificial containers and small natural cavities, and it does not need a pond, a ditch or a marsh at any point.

That produces two properties that change everything about how the problem is handled.

**The production site is a physical object you can go and look at.** Not a landscape feature, not a watershed, not somebody else's wetland — a saucer, a gutter, a bucket, a tarp fold, a drain extension. There is a bottleneck in the life cycle and it is sitting in your side yard.

**The adults stay close.** This species is a weak, low, short-range flier that tends to remain near where it emerged rather than dispersing across a neighborhood. That is the single most encouraging fact on this page, because it means the mosquitoes biting you at your own back door were very probably produced within a short distance of it — and removing that water has a direct, observable effect. It is also why this species rewards property-level work in a way that marsh and floodwater species simply do not.

**The corollary is that a neighbor matters.** Short range cuts both ways. An unmaintained pool, a boat under a tarp or a rain barrel with no screen on the adjoining lot is close enough to supply your yard, and no amount of work on your side of the fence changes it. We survey what we can access and tell you honestly when the source appears to be off the property, because setting that expectation in the first conversation is better than discovering it in August.

## How do the eggs survive a dry container and a Long Island winter?

By being laid above the water rather than on it, and by being built to dry out — which is why tipping a container is only half the job.

A female of this species does not lay on the water surface. She lays on the damp inner wall of a container, just above the current waterline, where the eggs stick fast. Those eggs are resistant to drying. They can sit on a dry container wall for a long period and remain viable, and they hatch when the water rises and re-wets them.

Three consequences follow, and each of them explains something people find baffling.

**Tipping is good; scrubbing is better.** Emptying a plant saucer removes the larvae in it and leaves the eggs cemented to the sides. The next rain refills it and the site restarts. Wiping or scrubbing the inside wall is what actually clears it, and it takes seconds.

**A dry container is not necessarily a clean one.** A bucket that has been bone dry behind the garage for six weeks may be loaded with eggs waiting for rain. This is why an inventory of every water-holding object matters even in a dry spell, and why the best time to deal with containers is before they fill rather than after.

**The population overwinters as eggs.** The adults die with the cold, but the eggs laid on container walls in autumn survive the winter and hatch in the spring when the containers refill and the temperature rises. So the containers you leave outside over winter are next year's starting population, and emptying, scrubbing and storing them upside down in the autumn is one of the highest-leverage jobs on the whole subject. It is a small piece of work with a disproportionate effect on the following June.

**And it explains the rain lag.** Households notice that biting gets sharply worse roughly a week to ten days after a heavy rain, not during it. That is not weather washing insects in. It is a synchronised hatch: rain re-wets eggs across every container on the street at once, and the whole cohort develops on the same timetable and emerges together. The lag is the development time from egg to flying adult, which is short in warm weather and longer when it is cool.

## What is the season here, and what drives the surges?

A build through the spring, a hot compounding summer, a late peak, and a hard stop at sustained cold.

**Spring.** Overwintering eggs hatch as containers refill and temperatures rise. Numbers are low and nobody notices, which is unfortunate, because this is the cheapest and most effective moment of the year to act. Every container removed, drained or scrubbed now is a site that produces nothing for the entire season.

**Early summer.** The first generations complete and adults appear. Development accelerates with heat, and generations begin to stack rather than succeed one another cleanly. This is when the first complaints usually arrive.

**High summer.** Peak development speed. Warm wet weather is the driver: a hot, humid stretch punctuated by rain produces the largest surges, and dry hot spells actually suppress the problem by drying containers out — though not by clearing them, because the eggs are still on the walls waiting.

**Late summer and early autumn.** Populations remain high considerably later than people expect, and a warm September holds pressure that a cool one does not. This is also when females are laying the eggs that will overwinter.

**Autumn and winter.** Sustained cold ends adult activity. Eggs persist. The season is over above ground and the work moves to containers, gutters and drainage.

Two other drivers matter locally. **Shade and humidity** determine where adults rest through the day, which is why the pressure is worst around dense low plantings, ivy, pachysandra beds, hedge bottoms, the north side of a house and the space under a deck. And **irrigation** extends the season and the number of viable sites, because a system running on a timer keeps saucers, low spots and corrugated pipe wet through dry weeks that would otherwise have broken the cycle.

## What is the risk, stated honestly?

A biting nuisance that makes a property unusable, and a public-health question that belongs to public-health agencies rather than to us.

**The nuisance is the part we can speak to directly, and it is not trivial.** A property producing this species is a property where the yard cannot be used in daylight, where children come in covered in bites after twenty minutes, where gardening becomes unpleasant, and where outdoor dining is off the table. Because the biting happens in the middle of the day rather than at dusk, the usual advice about avoiding peak hours does not apply. On a small city rear yard or a terrace, that can mean losing the only outdoor space the household has.

**The public-health dimension.** This is a species that health agencies monitor because of its vector potential, and it is one of the reasons county health departments run mosquito surveillance programs across this region. That is the correct source for current local information, and it is where questions about disease risk should go. We do not make claims about disease risk at a particular address, we do not quote statistics, and we would treat any contractor who does with considerable skepticism. What we can do is find and remove the water on your property, which is a contribution to the same objective made in the only way a private contractor honestly can.

**A practical note on bites.** Reactions to mosquito bites vary widely between individuals, and anything beyond an ordinary local reaction is a matter for a physician. That is not a subject a pest control company should be advising on.

## How do you know the mosquitoes are being produced on your own property?

Because of when you are bitten, where you are bitten and what happens after rain — and those three together are a fairly reliable diagnosis before anyone walks the site.

**Daytime biting.** If the problem is confined to dusk, you are more likely dealing with the marsh and floodwater species and the source may be well off the property. Persistent midday and afternoon biting points at container breeding close by.

**Low bites, in the shade, near the house.** Ankles and calves rather than face and arms. Worst in the shrub border, under the deck, along the fence line and in the shaded side yard rather than in the middle of an open lawn.

**A tight geography.** Bad in one part of the yard and noticeably better twenty yards away. Short-range fliers produce that pattern; long-range species do not.

**The post-rain surge.** A pronounced worsening roughly a week to ten days after a downpour is a container signature.

**Larvae themselves.** The definitive evidence, and anyone can check for it. Look into any standing water on the property — a saucer, a bucket, a bird bath, a wheelbarrow, a tarp fold — and watch for a few seconds. Larvae hang at the surface and jerk downward in a wriggling motion when disturbed, which is why they are called wrigglers. Pupae are comma-shaped and tumble. Finding either confirms production at that exact spot, and it turns an abstract complaint into a list of locations.

**No larvae, still bitten.** Then the site has not been found yet. Gutters and buried corrugated pipe are the two we find most often, precisely because you cannot see into either from the ground.

## Where is the water on a New York or Long Island property?

Rarely where people look, and the productive sites are the ones that do not look like water.

**On the building itself.** Gutters holding a shallow pond at a sag or behind a blockage — the most consistently missed site there is, invisible from the ground and only assessable from a ladder. Corrugated downspout extensions, where every rib holds a reservoir when the run is level or has settled backward. Buried corrugated drain pipe from a downspout to a pop-up emitter, which is the same problem underground and entirely out of sight. Flat and low-slope roofs that pond, roof drains and scuppers that silt up, and porch and bay window roofs with poor fall. Air-conditioning condensate at a pad or in a window unit's pan. Window wells. The well at the bottom of a cellar hatch or a bulkhead stair. Fire escape landings and drip pans in the city. Terrace and roof-deck planters, self-watering pots and their integrated trays.

**In the yard.** Plant saucers under every pot. Tarps and covers on grills, furniture, pools, boats and woodpiles, which hold water in every wrinkle. Tires lying flat, which never fully dry because the shape shelters the water from sun and wind. Wheelbarrows, buckets and recycling bins standing upright. Children's toys left face-up. Kayaks and canoes stored the wrong way up. Rain barrels without a fitted screen. Bird baths and pet bowls. Corrugated fiberglass panels stored flat. Cinder blocks. Fence post caps. Boat trailers with the plug still in. Ornamental ponds and fountains that are not circulating.

**In the ground and the landscape.** Catch basins and dry wells, which hold water by design and become long-term production sites when they silt up. Tree rot holes and cut stumps, which are the natural habitat this insect evolved in and which are almost impossible to spot from a driveway. Sump pits and the discharge point of a sump line into a low spot. Low areas that pond for more than a few days. Tree pits and planters on a city street.

**In the places nobody walks.** Behind the shed. Down the side of the garage. Under the deck. Beside the compost. Behind the pool equipment. In a brownstone areaway, where a decade of stored objects sits beside a drain that no longer drains. Objects collect in these places and stay there unexamined from one spring to the next.

## How is the source actually removed?

In three tiers, in order of preference, and the first tier is the one that lasts.

**Eliminate the water.** This tier comes first every time, because a site that no longer exists needs no upkeep and no reapplication. Tip, scrub and store containers upside down. Clear the gutters, correct the fall on any sagging run, and go back up after the leaves come down. Swap ribbed downspout extensions for smooth rigid pipe, laid to a fall that actually discharges. Drain, fold and store tarps, or re-pitch them so they shed. Remove tires. Drill drain holes in anything that must stay outside. Fill or open tree rot holes so they drain. Pull the boat plug. Fit a tight screen to the rain barrel. Regrade or install drainage where a low spot holds for more than a few days. Clear roof drains and scuppers. Route condensate to somewhere it can drain away.

**Introduce movement or a predator where the water must stay.** Larvae cannot use moving water, so a pump or an aerator resolves an ornamental feature. A properly circulating and maintained pool is not a mosquito site; an abandoned green one is among the most productive in existence. Fish in a stocked pond deal with larvae efficiently.

**Build the little that genuinely remains out of the property.** Catch basins, a working drainage feature, a dry well, a corner that truly holds. Aquatic use of minimum-risk materials is not permitted in this state, so that water cannot be larvicided by us at all, and every honest answer left is a physical one: get the basin or dry well cleaned until it drains as designed, cut a positive outlet, re-pitch the run, screen the chamber, put a pump on the feature. Against *Aedes albopictus* in particular this tier stays small, because hardly any of its habitat is a water body to begin with. It is objects, and objects can be tipped.

A proper survey ends in a written list, split three ways: gone, fixed, or flagged as a producer nobody working on this job is permitted to correct. A good deal of that list is work for a gutter contractor, a landscaper or the homeowner with a screwdriver, and handing it over is more useful than pretending all of it is ours.

## What gets applied against this species, and what will it not do?

Very little, deliberately, and the reasons are worth understanding because they are the reasons the container list matters so much.

Start with the certification, since it decides what is legal before it decides what is sensible. Public health pest control is a distinct New York certification category, number 8, and it is the one vector work belongs in; Ryan Katz holds it, alongside 7A and 7F, on certified applicator credential C1822141. Even a minimum-risk product, applied commercially or for hire in this state, has to go on by a certified applicator, with daily use records kept for it. The ornamental and turf category, 3A, is not held, so nothing on this page is an offer to treat plantings or lawns.

The material itself is a product exempt from federal registration under FIFRA section 25(b) — plant oils and food-grade ingredients from the list at 40 CFR 152.25(f). New York does not require these to be registered by the state, but three things about them govern how they can honestly be described.

**They are short-lived.** A 25(b) material acts on contact and stops mattering quickly, faster than the conventional synthetic residues it displaces. Against a species that rests low in dense shade and emerges continuously from containers a few yards away, a brief contact effect on the vegetation is a small intervention, not a season.

**They may not be sold as disease protection.** Federal law makes it a term of the exemption itself: an exempt label carries no claim against disease vectors or organisms that threaten human health. Since this particular species is monitored precisely for its vector potential, the boundary needs saying plainly rather than left to inference — nothing applied here is protection against a virus, and the surveillance belongs to county health departments.

**They cannot be put in water in New York.** Which removes the one intervention that would otherwise be aimed squarely at *Aedes albopictus* — treating the container. So the container is tipped and scrubbed instead, which is better anyway, because scrubbing takes the drought-proof eggs off the wall and a larvicide would not.

Put together, the material is the last and smallest line of the job. The eight hundred words above it, about where the water is, are the job. That is the same argument set out at length under [mosquito management](/pest-control/mosquito-management/).

## What does the weekly routine look like, and what building work helps?

Fifteen minutes after every rain, an autumn clear-out, and a handful of building details that keep the insects outside.

**The weekly walk.** After every meaningful rain, go round the property with the specific intention of looking down and behind things. Tip anything holding water and, where you can, wipe the inside. Check saucers, buckets, toys, covers, the wheelbarrow, the recycling bin, the bird bath. Look under the deck, behind the shed and down the side of the garage. This is the highest-value unpaid work available on the subject and it beats anything that can be sold to you.

**Twice a year, the gutters.** Once after leaf fall, which is the clearing that determines next spring, and once in early summer. Check from a ladder rather than from the ground, because a gutter holding water looks identical from below to one that is not.

**In the autumn, the containers.** Empty, scrub and store inverted. Scrubbing removes the overwintering eggs from the walls, which is a small task with an outsized effect the following year.

**Thin the resting habitat.** Dense low ground cover, ivy and shaded shrub beds against the house are not breeding sites but they are where adults spend the day. Opening those up, raising hedge bottoms and improving air movement lowers the local pressure.

**And close the building.** This is where a mosquito job turns into an envelope job. Intact window and door screens with no tears and a proper fit. Screen doors that actually close. A door sweep that meets the threshold. Sealed air-conditioner surrounds, which on older sleeve installations are frequently open around the whole perimeter. Closed gaps around window frames and casings. That work belongs to [structural exclusion](/pest-control/structural-exclusion/), and it is the same list that keeps out most of the rest of the library.

One last connection worth making. The clogged gutter producing mosquitoes on the north side of your house is also keeping the fascia behind it permanently wet, which softens the timber and invites a completely different set of problems. A mosquito survey and a moisture survey tend to produce overlapping lists, and it is generally cheaper to act on both at once.
