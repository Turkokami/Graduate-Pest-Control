## What are you actually looking at?

A compact, glossy, sharply banded wasp — and the giveaway is what it does with its legs.

An eastern yellowjacket is smaller and stockier than a bald-faced hornet, with a short narrow waist and a bold black-and-yellow pattern in crisp, hard-edged bands. The surface is smooth and shiny rather than hairy. In flight it holds its legs tucked in close against the body, which gives it a bullet-like profile. That is the field mark that separates it at a glance from a [paper wasp](/pest-library/paper-wasp/), which flies with long legs dangling beneath it and has a longer, more slender body.

**Against a honey bee**, which is the confusion that matters most. A honey bee is fuzzy and dull, more amber and brown than yellow, with pollen baskets on the hind legs and a much less aggressive disposition around a food source. Honey bees are not a pest and are not treated. If what is in your wall turns out to be honey bees, that is a beekeeping and removal matter, with the additional structural complication that abandoned comb and honey left in a cavity ferments, stains through and attracts a long list of secondary pests. We will tell you that rather than treating them.

**Against a bald-faced hornet**, which is considerably larger, matt black and ivory-white with no yellow anywhere on it, and which builds a visible envelope in the open rather than hiding in a hole.

**Against a hoverfly**, which mimics the pattern convincingly but has one pair of wings rather than two, enormous eyes, no waist to speak of, and a habit of hanging motionless in the air. It cannot sting.

The identification is worth getting right for one reason: yellowjackets are cavity nesters and the other species in this group are not. That single fact turns an insect problem into a building problem, and it changes everything about how the job is approached.

## Where is the colony, and why can you not see it?

Because a yellowjacket builds the same layered paper structure a bald-faced hornet does, and then puts it inside something.

The architecture is a stack of horizontal combs, connected by stalks and wrapped in a papery envelope, exactly as an aerial nest is — but rather than hanging it in the open, the founding queen selects an existing void and expands into it. The colony grows to fill whatever volume the cavity permits, and by late summer a mature nest can be a couple of feet across with thousands of workers in it. None of it is visible. What is visible is a small opening with a steady two-way stream of insects.

**Below ground.** The classic and most common site is an abandoned rodent burrow in a lawn or a planting bed. That connection is worth pausing on, because it is a genuinely useful piece of property diagnosis: a lawn with rodent burrows in it is a lawn with future yellowjacket sites in it, and the two problems share a cause. Other subterranean sites include the void beneath a patio slab or a set of steps, the space behind landscape timbers or a railway-tie retaining wall, the hollow under a shed floor, a gap beneath a walkway, an old stump socket, and the loose fill behind a stone wall.

**In the structure.** The version that becomes a serious problem. A framing bay in an exterior wall. A soffit cavity. An attic corner. The void behind brick veneer. A porch or deck framing box. A hollow porch column. The space above a bay window's roof. A garage wall. A chimney chase. The cavity behind exterior corner boards or J-channel.

**In objects.** A compost bin, a disused grill, a wall-mounted mailbox, an electrical meter enclosure, a stack of stored material, an upturned boat, or the housing of an outdoor unit that has not run since last summer.

The practical consequence is that there is no nest to remove without opening the building, and the entrance the colony is using is the only reliable route to the insects. That is why the work is treatment at the opening rather than nest extraction, and why the opening — not the nest — is the thing that has to be dealt with afterwards.

## Which building details admit a founding queen?

The same details that admit everything else, which is the most useful observation on this page.

In spring, a single overwintered queen goes looking for a cavity. Her specification is short: a small entrance she can defend, a dry protected void behind it large enough to expand into, and no competition. Walk the exterior of an ordinary frame house and count how many details meet that description.

**At the roofline.** A soffit return left open in original construction is the most consistently productive of the lot — a hollow boxed triangle connected straight into the eave. A gable louver with corroded or missing screening behind it. A ridge vent where the mesh has torn or the closure strip has compressed. The channel where a vinyl soffit panel meets the fascia. The gap behind a fascia board that has pulled away. The angle at which a dormer roof runs into the main roof plane.

**On the wall.** Weep holes in brick veneer, which have to stay open for drainage and are therefore permanent candidate entrances that must be screened rather than filled. The gap at the top of a window casing. The joint where siding terminates at a corner board. Behind a shutter. An unsealed sleeve where an air-conditioning line set passes through the wall. A through-wall air conditioner with an unsealed surround. A dryer vent whose flapper no longer closes. A hose bib penetration. An electrical or cable entry that was cored and never sealed. A gap behind a light fixture or an outlet box.

**Low down.** The joint where the frame meets the top of the foundation. A crawlspace vent with failed screening. A bulkhead surround. The gap under a lifted corner of garage door seal.

Set that inventory beside the roofline list on our [wildlife management](/pest-control/wildlife-management/) work and it is the same list. A soffit return that admits a yellowjacket queen in April admits a gray squirrel in March and mice all year. That is not coincidence — it is one building fault with several tenants, and it is why a wasp call on a house with a chronically open roofline turns into a broader conversation about the envelope.

There is also a reason the problem repeats in the same location year after year even though the nest is new each time. New queens overwinter close to where they were produced, frequently inside the structure itself — behind loose siding, in an unheated attic corner, in a louver, behind trim. A property that supported a large colony last season has a higher density of queens hunting for cavities the following spring, and the best cavity within a short distance is the one that already worked.

## What does the colony year look like, and why does August change everything?

Because the colony peaks in size at exactly the moment its diet changes and its temper shortens.

**Spring.** One queen, one small comb, no workers. She forages, builds and raises the first brood alone. The colony is invisible and effectively harmless, and a cavity that will hold thousands of insects in September currently holds one.

**Early summer.** Workers emerge and take over foraging, building and brood care. The queen becomes a dedicated egg layer. Foraging is heavily protein-oriented, because larvae need protein — which is why yellowjackets are on the grilled chicken in July rather than the soda.

**High summer.** Growth compounds. The nest expands into whatever volume the cavity permits: up a stud bay, along a soffit run, out into an attic corner, or downward and outward in a lawn burrow. The defended radius grows with it. This is usually when a wall colony first becomes obvious, because the traffic at the entrance finally gets heavy enough to notice.

**Late summer into early autumn.** The dangerous part of the year, and three things converge to produce it.

First, sheer numbers — the colony is at its annual maximum.

Second, a dietary switch that explains the behavior everyone recognizes. Through the season, larvae in the nest give the adult workers a sugary secretion in exchange for the protein they are fed. As the queen stops producing worker brood and begins producing next year's reproductives, that supply falls away. Workers deprived of it go looking for sugar for themselves, which is why yellowjackets appear at bins, fallen fruit, outdoor tables, soft drink cans and ice cream from mid-August onward, and why they become so persistent about it.

Third, natural forage declines and competition rises, so they range further and press harder.

The result is a very large colony, foraging widely, hungry, and defending a season's investment with a short fuse. Nearly all of the stinging incidents happen in this window.

**Autumn and winter.** The new queens mate and scatter to find somewhere to sit out the cold. The founding queen and every worker dies with sustained cold. The nest is finished permanently.

The practical reading: a colony dealt with in June is a small job. The same colony in September is a much more careful one. Waiting for the frost is not a plan, because the frost does not close the hole.

## What is genuinely at risk?

Four things, and the one people underestimate is the interior of their own house.

**Breakthrough into living space.** A colony in a framing bay is separated from a bedroom by whatever sheathing and finish material happens to be in the way. Heat, disturbance, vibration or a poorly judged intervention can bring workers through a ceiling fixture, a recessed light housing, a switch box, a gap at a top plate or the void behind a baseboard. This is where a nuisance becomes an emergency, and it is the reason a colony in a wall adjoining a bedroom is treated as urgent rather than seasonal.

**Multiple stings.** Yellowjackets do not lose the sting and can deliver repeatedly, and a disturbed cavity colony releases many workers at once. The ground-nest version struck by a mower or a string trimmer is the classic residential incident, and it is the most common cause of a serious sting event on a suburban property. A known venom allergy changes the risk profile entirely and is a matter for a physician; anyone in that category should be well away from the work.

**Stings around the face and throat**, which are dangerous regardless of allergy status. Late-season yellowjackets inside an open drink can are a genuine hazard, particularly for children.

**Damage and secondary problems in the structure.** A large nest against interior finish produces staining and moisture on a ceiling or a wall. A nest sealed into a cavity and left there decomposes, and the brood and dead adults draw a secondary population of beetles, flies and ants into the wall — a different problem arriving a month later. There is also the fall risk, which is not a venom risk at all: a significant share of injuries associated with wasp nests come from someone on a ladder being stung and reacting.

## What are the signs of a colony inside a structure?

Traffic converging on one point, and a handful of secondary indicators that matter when the entrance is not obvious.

**The two-way stream.** The definitive sign. A steady, purposeful flow of insects in and out of a single small location, sustained over minutes rather than sporadic. This is what separates a colony from foragers: scattered individuals around a bin or a fallen apple may be coming from a neighboring property entirely. Watch from a safe distance in the middle of a warm day, when traffic is heaviest, and time how many pass in a minute — a heavy stream indicates a mature colony and changes how the job is approached.

**Sound.** A mature colony in a wall or a soffit is audible on a hot afternoon: a low, continuous hum or rustle from inside the cavity, sometimes with a papery note to it.

**Staining or damp on an interior surface.** A discoloured patch on a ceiling or an upstairs wall, sometimes slightly bulging, above or beside where the traffic is on the outside. This indicates a large nest pressing against the back of the finish, and it moves the job up the priority list.

**Insects at interior windows.** Single wasps appearing inside a room, particularly upstairs and particularly at windows, mean the colony has already found a route through the assembly. That is not a stray from the garden; it is a warning.

**In a lawn.** A bare, roughly circular hole with worn soil around it and a steady stream of traffic. Frequently found by a mower, which is precisely the problem. Marking the location from a distance and keeping everyone off that part of the yard is the right immediate step.

**Heat and orientation.** Colonies favor south- and west-facing walls, because the warmth accelerates brood development. If you are looking for an entrance and cannot find it, start on the sunny side.

## Why must you never seal the entrance while the colony is active?

Because a wall is not a container, and closing the exit does not confine anything — it only changes the direction.

The instinct is entirely understandable. An opening exists, insects are streaming through it, and the obvious response is to shut it. The problem is what the cavity behind it is connected to. A framing bay opens into other framing bays. It opens into the soffit and the attic. Through top plates, electrical boxes, recessed light housings, plumbing penetrations, the gap behind a baseboard and the void around a window frame, it opens into the rooms of the house.

Block the exterior entrance on a colony of a few thousand workers and you have applied pressure to a large population whose single imperative is to get out, in a space whose remaining exits point inward. Yellowjackets chew. They will work at the softest material available, and drywall paper and joint compound are considerably softer than whatever you just fastened over the outside. What follows is workers appearing in a bedroom or through a ceiling fixture, often over several days, and a job that could have been done from a ladder now involves the inside of the house and a family that cannot use an upstairs room.

There is a slower failure mode as well. Even where the colony does not break through, a large nest sealed into a cavity dies there and decomposes, and the resulting accumulation draws its own succession of insects into the wall.

The same caution covers the improvised remedies. Do not run a hose into a cavity nest. Do not use fire or fuel anywhere near a structure. Do not attack a ground nest with a shop vacuum. Do not tape over a hole and hope. And do not attempt any of it in the middle of the day, when a large fraction of the foragers are out and will return to a disturbed nest and an exposed person.

The correct order is the reverse of the instinct: treat, verify, then seal. Nothing gets closed while there is traffic.

## How is a cavity colony treated?

At the opening, at the right hour, verified before anything is closed, and followed by building work.

**Find the actual entrance.** Often it is not where the wasps are being seen. Watching the traffic for a few minutes at the warmest part of the day resolves it quickly, and it distinguishes a defined entrance from scattered foraging.

**Assess the position.** Establish what lies on the far side of that void. Whether it adjoins occupied space, and which room. Who uses the area and when. Whether there is a ventilation path from the void into the interior. Whether anyone in the household has a venom allergy. Whether a ladder can be set safely and where the escape route is.

**Treat at dusk or after dark.** The foraging force is home, the colony is least active, and the person doing the work is not standing under a returning stream of insects. Material is applied at the entrance the workers themselves use, and it works because returning foragers carry it in to a nest nobody can see.

**Verify.** Traffic at the entrance is re-checked before anything is sealed. A colony that looks finished at dusk can still have foragers arriving the following morning, and those animals matter, because the whole sealing step depends on the cavity being genuinely empty.

**Open the assembly only where it is warranted.** A very large colony pressed against interior finish, a nest that has caused staining or damage, an attic nest with an accumulation that should not be left, or a case where the void has to be opened for repair anyway. Otherwise the nest is left in place and the void is closed.

**Then close the entrance and the equivalent details around the rest of the building.** This is the part that decides whether you do this again next year. Soffit returns closed properly with rigid material. Gable louvers rescreened from behind in metal. Ridge vent end caps reseated and torn mesh repaired. Fascia gaps closed. Weep holes screened rather than filled, because they must keep draining. Line-set sleeves and utility penetrations packed and sealed. Trim and siding secured. The method is the same discipline described under [structural exclusion](/pest-control/structural-exclusion/), applied to an insect that happens to sting.

## What stops a queen choosing your building next spring?

Closing the cavities, removing the burrows, and looking at the property in April rather than in August.

**Seal the envelope in the autumn or the winter.** The queens are looking in spring; the work should be finished before then. Every soffit return, louver, ridge end, fascia gap, weep hole and penetration on the list above is a candidate entrance, and closing them removes the specification she is searching for.

**Deal with the rodent burrows.** The most-used subterranean nest site is an abandoned rodent burrow, so a property with an active or historic rodent problem is generating yellowjacket sites as a by-product. Collapsing and backfilling old burrows along foundation lines, under decks and around sheds removes them. This is one of the more satisfying overlaps in the trade, and it is covered from the other direction on the [Norway rat](/pest-library/norway-rat/) page.

**Close the landscape voids.** Fill the gaps behind landscape timbers and retaining walls. Deal with the void under a settled patio slab or a set of steps. Skirt sheds and decks properly. Remove stump sockets.

**Walk the property in late spring, looking for small nests and low traffic.** A founding colony is trivially dealt with; a mature one is not.

**Manage the late-season attraction.** From mid-August, keep bins closed and clean around them, rinse cans and bottles left outside, cover food and drink at outdoor tables, pick up fallen fruit, and keep pet food indoors. This does not affect the colony but it dramatically affects how many wasps you meet.

**And keep a nest location marked and avoided** while you wait for an appointment, rather than working around it. Suspend mowing and trimming in that area, keep pets off it, and do not let anyone try to fix it with a can.
