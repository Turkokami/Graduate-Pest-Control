## Why is a Huntington ant problem usually a water problem?

Because the ant that matters here does not eat wood. It moves into wood that has already been ruined by something else.

Carpenter ants excavate galleries for nest space and push the debris out. They select decaying or water-softened timber because it is far easier to work and because the moisture helps the brood survive — the Northeastern IPM Center states the mechanism plainly. That single fact governs everything that follows. Ant activity in a Huntington house is a readout of the building's moisture history. Kill the ants and leave the water, and the site remains exactly as attractive as it was.

Huntington supplies wet wood generously and in several distinct ways.

The harbor end of town does it through the air. Halesite sits at around twenty-six feet directly on Huntington Harbor, and its crawlspaces ventilate into summer air that is already close to saturation. Framing down there does not fall below the moisture content at which decay fungi keep working, so it softens slowly and permanently — sills, band joists, the underside of a porch, the ends of joists near an exterior wall.

The post-war ring inland does it through detail failures. Attached garages, low-slope porch roofs, additions built onto rear walls, and re-siding done over the original cladding all create joints where water is admitted and cannot leave. That produces wet framing in a specific and locatable place rather than generally damp framing everywhere.

The old village stock does it through age. A cedar-shingle wall over hand-framed timber has no sheathing membrane behind it and generous cavities in the wall. Water that gets behind the shingle course wets timber that then dries slowly, in the dark, out of sight.

The consequence is that our first hour on a Huntington ant job is spent on gutters, grade, flashing and crawlspace condition rather than on ants. The insects are the easy part. The [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) profile covers the biology in full; what follows is what the biology does to buildings in this town.

## What changes when the house sits on the ridge rather than at the water?

The nest moves outdoors and the survey turns upward.

Huntington's higher ground — West Hills, the streets climbing away from the village, the wooded northern edge — is glacial material that drains quickly, on large lots with heavy mature canopy. Framing on those properties genuinely does dry. What replaces the harbor's chronic damp is a different supply of decayed wood: the trees themselves.

An oak with heartwood decay, a dead limb left in the canopy, a stump from a storm-felled tree, a stack of split firewood that has been sitting against a garage since 2019 — these are excellent parent colony sites, and they are everywhere on the ridge. In that situation the colony inside the house is a satellite: workers, older larvae and pupae, moved into a convenient interior void, connected to the parent nest by a trail the ants follow religiously. Satellites do not need damp wood in the way a parent colony does. They need a void, warmth and a route.

That changes the work in three practical ways. First, the survey has to include the trees and the ground within a reasonable radius of the building, not just the building. Second, the branches touching or overhanging the roof matter, because they are the trail: a limb resting on a gutter is a bridge that removes the need for the ants to cross any of the treatments applied at ground level. Third, the timing of activity is a clue — foragers moving after dusk along a fascia or a soffit line, seen with a flashlight, tell you where the trail runs, and following that trail outward is a far better use of an evening than searching interior cupboards.

On a harbor-side property the inverse holds. The parent colony is more often in the structure itself, in the wettest available timber, and the ants indoors are living where they were found. That is why the two ends of the same town produce jobs that look nothing alike.

## Which details on Huntington's post-war houses feed a colony?

Five, and they repeat from street to street because the houses were built the same way in the same decade.

**The porch or stoop roof.** A low-slope roof over an entry, tied into the wall with flashing that was correct in 1958 and has been painted over four times since, wets the framing behind it. The band joist and the wall plate immediately inside are a standing find on capes and splits in Huntington Station and South Huntington.

**The re-sided wall.** When vinyl or aluminum was laid over original cladding — and on this housing stock it very often was — the new course terminates at the top, at the corner boards and around openings, and those terminations are rarely sealed. Behind it there is now a continuous cavity from grade to soffit, ventilated by nothing, admitting wind-driven rain at the top. Wet sheathing in a closed cavity is the ideal carpenter ant site because it is dark, damp and undisturbed.

**The attached garage's shared wall.** The wall between garage and living space usually has an unsealed top plate, a step in the slab, and stored goods against it for its whole length. It is warm on one side, cool on the other, and it is where condensation happens.

**Window and door sills on the weather elevation.** On a harbor-facing wall, rain arrives horizontally. It is driven into the joint between trim and siding, wets the back of the trim, and shows nothing on the painted face until the decay is well advanced. When frass appears beneath a trim board here, the fault is almost never at the frass. It is at a joint above and behind it.

**The crawlspace hatch and the vents.** A crawlspace with a torn or absent vapor barrier and vents that admit harbor air is not being ventilated, it is being humidified. On the lowest properties the correct answer is sometimes a sealed, conditioned crawlspace with a ground barrier rather than more vents — a construction decision made for pest reasons.

None of those is a defect that makes a house unsound. They are the specific reasons this town produces the volume of carpenter ant work it does.

## What about the cedar-shingled houses in the old village?

They have to be read as buildings before anything is applied to them.

The pre-1940 share of Huntington's housing concentrates in and around the historic village — the Old Town Green and Old Town Hall districts, listed on the National Register in 1985, hold colonial and nineteenth-century buildings on Park Avenue, West Main Street and Stewart Avenue. Add the 1816 Whitman farmhouse out at West Hills and you have a class of building framed before vapor barriers, before sheathing membranes and in some cases before dimensional lumber.

Three things follow for ant work.

The wall cavities are generous and continuous. In a house framed that way, and particularly in the balloon-framed houses of the late nineteenth and early twentieth century, a stud bay can run without interruption from the sill to the attic. An ant trail exploiting that runs vertically through the whole house with no need to appear in any room. It is also why an injection into one wall void can be entirely correct and entirely ineffective — the void extends much further than the person holding the equipment assumes.

The sill bears on stone. Hand-hewn timber sitting on a rubble or fieldstone wall with no capillary break wicks moisture out of the masonry continuously. That sill is permanently at an elevated moisture content, which is a decay condition first and an ant condition second.

The alterations are the map. Every enclosed porch, every kitchen extension, every dormer punched through a roof plane meets the older structure at a junction where framing does not align and where a void was left because closing it properly would have meant opening more of the building. Those junctions are where the nests are, and they are found by working out the building's construction sequence rather than by tapping randomly.

Work on this stock also has a preservation dimension. Cutting an access hole in original plaster or removing a section of historic shingle to reach a gallery is a decision the owner has to make with full information, not something to be done on the day because it was convenient.

## How do you find a nest you cannot see?

By reading what the colony leaves behind and by narrowing, rather than by searching.

**Frass first.** Carpenter ants keep their galleries clean and push the debris out through slots they maintain. The result is a small pile of coarse pale sawdust containing insect fragments, bits of insulation and sometimes dead ants — noticeably different from the fine flour-like dust of a wood-boring beetle. Its location is a far better locator than seeing ants, because it falls straight down from the gallery. On a Huntington property the productive places to look are the cellar steps directly beneath a beam, the ground under a porch, along the sill line at the shingle course, and under window trim on the weather elevation.

**Then sound.** Tapping suspect framing with a tool handle and listening for the change to a hollow note works, and on a quiet night an established colony is genuinely audible: a faint dry rustling inside a wall, most obvious after dark when the colony is active and the house is silent.

**Then follow the traffic.** Carpenter ants are mainly nocturnal. A flashlight at ten in the evening, walking the exterior and looking at fascia, soffit, the top of the foundation and any branch touching the building, will show trails that are invisible at midday. Indoors, put a small amount of a sweet or protein-based attractant on a card at a suspected route and watch which direction departing workers take — they go home, and home is what you are looking for.

**And read the water.** Every one of the above narrows to a location. The water then explains it. If the frass is under a porch beam, the flashing above it is the reason. If the trail runs from a crawlspace hatch, the crawlspace is the reason. On this town's building stock the moisture almost always identifies the nest before any wall is opened.

Where none of that resolves it, the honest answer is that a wall has to be opened, and the right thing to do is say so and agree where, rather than treating blind and calling the result a plan.

## Which other ants turn up in Huntington houses, and what do they mean?

Three, and each one means something different about the building.

**Pavement ants** are the small dark ants that come up through cracks in a slab, at an expansion joint, along the edge of a garage floor or between patio flags. On the post-war ring they are the most common non-carpenter complaint, precisely because that stock has so much concrete against it. They nest under the slab or the walk and enter through a joint. The [pavement ant](/pest-library/pavement-ant/) profile has the identification detail; what matters structurally is that they are telling you where the slab has a route through it, which is information worth having for other reasons.

**Odorous house ants** arrive in numbers after summer rain. They nest in shallow, unstable places — under a stone, beneath mulch, in a wall void, under insulation — and when those sites flood the colony moves, often indoors. A trail appearing on a counter within a day of a heavy storm is characteristic. See [odorous house ant](/pest-library/odorous-house-ant/) for identification. The important operational point is that spraying a trail of these can split the colony into more nests than you started with, so it is the wrong first move.

**Carpenter ants** remain the headline for the reasons above. Huntington's combination of harbor humidity, mature canopy and older frame construction makes it a carpenter ant town in a way that a newer, drier, inland market is not.

There is a fourth category worth naming for what it is not: a large winged insect on a windowsill in April is routinely reported as a termite when it is a carpenter ant reproductive, and occasionally the other way round. The pinched waist and the elbowed antennae settle it in seconds, and the [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile sets the two side by side. In a town with this much old frame construction the answer is usually the ant, which is the work described on this page. Where it is genuinely the termite, the position is worth stating without decoration: Graduate does not treat termites and does not issue wood-destroying-insect reports, so that job belongs with a firm that does.

## What does the work actually involve, and in what order?

Locate, treat the colony rather than the trail, correct the water, then check.

**Locate.** Everything above. Until the nest area is identified, any application is a guess with a good chance of suppressing foragers and leaving the colony intact.

**Treat what the ants take home.** Carpenter ant work relies on material that workers carry back to the nest, or on direct treatment of a located gallery through a small access. Contact sprays along a visible trail kill the individuals on that trail and do nothing to the colony, which is why houses treated that way see the ants return within weeks. Baiting is slower to look impressive and considerably more likely to end it.

**Correct the water.** This is the part that decides whether you are done. Extending the leader that discharges at the corner where the frass was found. Re-flashing the porch roof. Replacing the trim that has been wet for six years. Getting a ground vapor barrier and a properly sealed hatch into a Halesite crawlspace. Cutting back the limb that both bridges the roof and keeps a wall in permanent shade. Replacing framing that has lost section. Our proposals on this work routinely contain carpentry and drainage items sitting next to the treatment items, and that is deliberate.

**Then check.** A follow-up in the same season, and a look the following spring when activity resumes. Carpenter ant colonies are slow, and absence in July is weaker evidence than absence the following April.

What we will not do is put a house on an indefinite exterior spray schedule and call it ant control. That produces a property that looks clear between visits and reverts whenever the schedule lapses, because nothing about the building has changed.

## What can you check on your own property before anyone comes?

Four things, none of which costs money, all of which change what a survey finds.

**Stand at the far end of the drive and look at the roofline and the trees.** Is any branch touching the roof, the gutter or a corner of the house? On the wooded ridge that single observation frequently explains the whole problem, because a limb in contact with the building is a trail that bypasses everything at ground level. Note which tree, and note whether it has a dead limb or an old wound in the trunk.

**Walk the perimeter looking at the ground rather than the house.** Where does the grade fall — toward the foundation or away from it? Where does each downspout discharge? A leader emptying onto a splash block that has tipped, two feet from the wall, will keep one corner of the building wet for its whole life, and in Huntington that corner is where the frass eventually appears. Note whether mulch or a raised bed has climbed up against the siding.

**Open the crawlspace hatch, if there is one, and put a hand on a joist in July.** If the underside of the floor feels cool and slightly damp in high summer, the crawlspace is not drying. On a low harbor-side lot that is the single finding that shapes everything else about the job, and it is worth knowing before the conversation starts rather than during it.

**Look under things at the ground floor with a flashlight.** The cellar steps directly beneath a beam, the ground under the porch, the sill line at the bottom of the shingle course, the interior of the garage where the wall meets the slab. You are looking for small heaps of coarse pale sawdust. If you find one, photograph it and leave it where it is — sweeping it up removes the only piece of evidence that says which side of the building the gallery is on, and it will take weeks to reaccumulate.

Two things not to do. Do not spray a trail with something from a hardware shop before the survey; with odorous house ants in particular it can split the colony and multiply the number of nests, and with carpenter ants it removes the traffic that would have led us to the nest. And do not put down a scatter of granular material around the foundation on the assumption that it forms a barrier — it does not stop a satellite colony that is already living in a wall, and it makes the interior evidence harder to interpret.

None of that is a substitute for a survey and none of it is treatment. It is the information that lets the first conversation start in the right place.

## What does the ant year look like in Huntington?

Four phases, and they line up with the harbor's weather rather than with a generic calendar.

**Late March into May.** Activity resumes as the structure warms. Indoor sightings often precede outdoor foraging by weeks, which is a useful diagnostic: ants indoors in March, when nothing is moving outside, indicate a nest inside the heated envelope rather than an invasion from the garden. Winged reproductives may appear in the same window and are routinely mistaken for termite swarmers.

**June through August.** Peak foraging and the period when harbor-side crawlspaces reach their worst humidity of the year. It is also when frass accumulates visibly, which makes high summer a good time to locate a nest even though it is a poor time to read the building's water. Odorous house ants surge after each significant rain.

**September and October.** Foraging drops off, and satellite colonies consolidate. Work done now is still worthwhile, but confirmation of success has to wait for spring.

**November through February.** The colony is inactive inside the structure and largely undetectable. Ants seen indoors in deep winter mean a nest inside the heated part of the building — there is no other explanation — and that is worth acting on rather than waiting out. Winter is also the best time to read the envelope for the underlying moisture faults, because on a cold day the building shows where warm air is escaping, and those leaks track the same joints that let water in.
