## Why does a county mosquito program stop at the Kings Point property line?

Because everything that program is built around is public water, and hardly any of the water producing mosquitoes in this village is public.

The county effort is real and it is not small. Mosquitoes are collected from forty-four trap sites spread across Nassau and sent on for viral testing. The Department of Public Works watches for larvae in thousands of street basins, sumps and ponds, and along hundreds of miles of freshwater stream. Aerial larviciding exists, and it is restricted to salt marsh with no population on it. Read that as an inventory rather than a reassurance: street basins, public sumps, public ponds, public streams, unpopulated marsh, and not one item on it inside a private boundary here.

Then consider what a boundary in Kings Point contains. The A2 Residence District asks for 40,000 square feet of lot, 150 feet of frontage on an interior parcel, sixty feet of front yard, twenty-eight-foot side yards and forty feet at the rear. A village built to those figures is a run of small private estates, each with a residence, a detached garage, usually a pool, frequently a pool house, a boundary of mature planting, and a great deal of ground nobody crosses between October and May.

That is where the productive water is. A pool cover with a sag in it. A tender pulled up behind the garage. Twenty terracotta saucers on a terrace. A tarpaulin over stacked furniture. A hundred and sixty feet of gutter under canopy the village protects by ordinance. An irrigation head throwing water into the same shaded hollow since the system was commissioned. None of that appears in a county survey and none of it is going to.

So the division of labor here is cleaner than almost anywhere else in Nassau. The public half of the inventory has trapping, monitoring and larviciding behind it. The private half — which on a lot of this size is nearly all of it — has whoever owns the lot and whoever they ask. The general method is set out under [mosquito management](/pest-control/mosquito-management/). What follows is what it runs into behind a Kings Point gate.

## What does forty thousand square feet do to a water survey?

It turns a walk into a grid, and it puts most of the candidate sites out of anybody's daily line of sight.

A quarter-acre property can be surveyed from four standing positions, and the owner sees the whole of it every day on the way to the car. An acre lot with a sixty-foot setback is a different proposition. The strip between the garage and the boundary hedge. The ground behind the pool equipment. The back of the pool house. The far end of a planted bed that runs a hundred feet. Those places get visited twice a year, if that.

Production does not need to happen anywhere interesting. It needs about a week of standing water in something that holds it, and the chance of a container sitting undisturbed for a season rises directly with how seldom anyone walks past. On a property laid out to this zoning, that describes most of the perimeter.

The buildings multiply as well. Permitted uses here run to single-family dwellings, private garages and greenhouses, and in practice a parcel carries more — a pool house, a workshop, sometimes a converted outbuilding. Each has its own roof, gutter, leader and termination. Nobody thinks of a pool house gutter as a gutter. It is twelve feet of trough with leaves in it, under trees, holding water from April to October.

Then add irrigation, which almost every lawn of this size is on. An automatic system defeats the one natural process that interrupts development. A container drying out in a hot week kills what is inside it; a container topped up at five every second morning never dries, so every cohort in it completes. The sprinkler schedule is a variable in this work, and it is set by whoever manages the grounds rather than by us.

The answer to a large lot is not a bigger machine. It is a longer walk, divided into zones, written down and repeated.

## Which water on these lots actually produces the biting?

Rarely the thing the owner is worried about. In rough order of how often each one turns out to be the answer:

**The gutter run.** The most productive site we find in this village, and it is geometry rather than neglect. The tree ordinance keeps heavy canopy over these roofs, so the gutters reload with leaf litter every autumn, and a long run has a sag, a pulled hanger or a half-blocked outlet somewhere along it. A twenty-foot section holding an inch of water is shaded, warm, sheltered from wind and rich in organic matter. A house with four roof planes offers several candidates, and none can be assessed from the ground.

**The pool cover.** A winter or safety cover collects rain on its surface and holds it in the dish at the center. The pool is closed, so nobody counts the cover as water, and by the middle of May it is frequently the largest single body of standing water on the property.

**Boat covers, tenders and their gear.** With Manhasset Bay east, Little Neck Bay west and a marina at Steppingstone Park, a large share of these properties store something that floats. A tender on a rack. A dinghy not quite inverted. A cover with a pleat in it. A cockpit with the plug still seated. A kayak stored right side up. Boat equipment is kept where it is out of the way, and out of the way is also out of sight.

**Planters and saucers.** On a terrace designed to be looked at, the planting is in pots, and pots sit in saucers. Self-watering planters have a reservoir as a feature. Sealed decorative urns hold a column of water with nowhere to go. One terrace can carry thirty separate sites.

**Tarpaulins and covers generally.** Furniture, the barbecue, the woodpile. Water sits in the folds, and the folds are the part people forget.

**Irrigation hollows and shaded beds.** A low spot that holds for three days after a storm is not a problem; one kept wet by a timer through July is.

**Tree holes and stumps.** Here the tree code has a direct effect. A standing dead stem or a cut stump is not removed on a whim in this village, and a rot pocket in a mature trunk holds water for months, protected from sun and wind. It cannot be seen from a driveway.

**Basins and dry wells on private drives.** A sixty-foot setback means a long drive with drainage of its own, and private drainage belongs to the owner and to no program. When a basin silts up it stops dispersing water and starts storing it.

The species that make this list worth working through are the container breeders — the [Asian tiger mosquito](/pest-library/asian-tiger-mosquito/) and its relatives. They bite by day, low and around ankles, and they do not travel far from where they emerged. On a property this size that means the insect bothering you on the terrace most likely developed within a couple of hundred feet of it.

## What do the pool, the boat and the water on three sides add?

Three separate things, and only one of them is what people expect.

**The pool is two different sites depending on how it is run.** Circulating, chlorinated and skimmed, it is not habitat at all; nothing holds position in moving treated water. The same pool with the pump off for a fortnight in August while the household is away is among the most productive breeding sites that exists on a residential property. The survey question is never whether there is a pool but what the pool is doing this month, and the answer changes four times a year.

**The boat is a container with a lid on it.** Covered storage keeps rain off the hull and holds it everywhere else — in the cover, in the cockpit, in the tender beneath, in the bucket under the transom. Winterized gear stays untouched from October to April, which is exactly the window in which overwintering eggs sit on dry container walls waiting to be rewetted. A cover drained and refitted properly in March is fifteen minutes with an outsized effect.

**Salt water on three sides is mostly a different insect.** Sixteen percent of the village's area is water, with Long Island Sound north, Manhasset Bay east and Little Neck Bay west, and hardly any parcel is far from a tidal edge. Brackish and marsh habitat produces its own species, and they behave unlike the container breeders: they emerge in broods after a high tide or a heavy rain, several travel a good deal further, and the pressure arrives in waves rather than steadily. That is also the habitat the county's aerial larviciding addresses, where no one lives on it.

So a shoreline property often has two overlapping patterns, and separating them changes what is worth paying for. Biting that is roughly steady from June into September, worst in the shaded parts of the garden and concentrated where people sit, is being produced on or beside the property. Biting that spikes three or four days after a spring tide or a storm and then falls away came off open water, and no work inside the gate will touch that cohort.

Telling them apart costs nothing: note the date, the weather, the tide where it is known, the part of the property and the time of day. Two weeks of that beats any amount of argument in August.

There is also the matter of what adjoins you. Broadlawn Harbor, the Kennilworth Pool Club and Shelter Bay Yacht Club sit inside this village, as does the Academy campus, and large neighboring parcels carry pool water, boat storage and planting of their own. A property beside one is beside an inventory it has no right to inspect, which sets the ceiling at that address rather than excusing the result afterward.

## Why can the vegetation not simply be cut back?

Because in this village cutting it back is an application, and that changes the order of the work rather than the work itself.

Adult mosquitoes are weak fliers and they dry out quickly. Between blood meals they sit somewhere cool, humid, shaded and out of the wind — dense evergreen planting against the house, ground cover under specimen trees, the low mass inside a boundary hedge, the underside of a deck, the shaded face of a pool house. Opening that habitat up in the parts of the garden people actually occupy reduces how many adults are standing around there, regardless of where they bred.

Chapter 147 attaches a procedure to it. Any tree of six inches diameter or more, measured four and a half feet above ground, is covered, and the chapter is not only about removal: cutting, pruning, topping, elevating and burying roots are all in scope. An application wants the owner's and applicant's details, the purpose and location, site plans showing tree positions, and photographs with the trees physically marked with ribbon or tape, and the inspector may ask for further survey. Replacement runs at 110 percent of the diameter removed, with a three-inch caliper minimum for each tree taken, maintained for five years. A first violation runs from $1,500 to $20,000.

That is not an obstacle to mosquito work, but it does mean nobody here should be told to go and cut the shrubs back. What it governs is sequence. Shrub mass under six inches at the trunk, ivy, pachysandra and herbaceous planting sit outside the ordinance, and thinning those is usually all that resting habitat requires. Where a protected stem genuinely is in the way, the application goes in over the winter, and the water work proceeds meanwhile.

Turf and ornamental planting belong to category 3A, which this office does not have, so nothing applied by us goes onto a lawn or into a bed in any case. That half of the conversation belongs with whoever maintains the grounds, and we are content to have it with them directly.

## Does Kings Point Park produce what gets blamed on it?

Partly, and considerably less than its acreage implies.

The park runs to more than 175 acres between Steamboat Road and Redbrook Road, leased to the Great Neck Park District in 1938 and converted from marshland into a recreational facility. That history is why residents on the boundary streets assume it is the source of everything. Two things about it are true: made ground over former wetland holds water in places, and a long wooded edge against residential rear boundaries is excellent daytime shelter for adults whatever produced them.

What the acreage does not tell you is production. A wooded park with established drainage is not a marsh. The container breeders responsible for most terrace biting want small, still, organically rich, sheltered water, and they find far more of it in a gutter, a saucer and a cover than across 175 acres of trees. A property on the park boundary usually has excellent resting habitat on one side and its own container inventory on the other, and the second is the one anyone can act on.

The same holds for Steppingstone Park and Marina, bought in 1942 and enlarged after 2000: public ground with public drainage, in the same category as the street basins, actively managed and not yours to correct.

What we do about land we cannot reach is write it down. A survey here ends with a list in three parts — water to be removed, water needing a physical fix, and water that is a known producer nobody on this job can correct, each named, with whose it is beside it. A street basin belongs to the county, the park to the Park District, the club ground to the club. A named uncorrected source is more useful to own than a treatment interval nobody audits.

## What actually gets applied on a lot like this, and under what credential?

Two questions, and the trade usually answers neither.

Ryan Katz is a New York certified applicator, credential C1822141, and the relevant category is 8, public health, which is where vector work belongs. The company holds New York pesticide business registration 03298. Both numbers check against the state register, which is why they are printed. Arnold Katz founded the firm in 1983 and holds a B.S. in Entomology from the University of Georgia; he is still out on jobs as senior technician and staff entomologist, and identification questions go to him. Ryan's side of it is field experience and training rather than a degree, and we would rather state that plainly.

Where anything is applied at all, it is a minimum-risk product exempt from federal registration under section 25(b) of FIFRA — plant oils and food-grade ingredients from the schedule at 40 CFR 152.25(f). New York does not register these products, but it does require that commercial application be done by a certified applicator and that daily use records be kept as for anything else.

Three limits follow, and they shape this whole page.

**Exempt is not stronger.** A 25(b) material is a short-lived contact product. It affects what it directly reaches and stops mattering quickly, faster than a conventional synthetic residue. The honest consequence runs the other way: because it does comparatively little for a comparatively short time, it cannot be the program, and the water has to be.

**It cannot claim disease control.** A condition of the federal exemption is that an exempt product makes no claim about disease vectors. Arbovirus surveillance in this region is county and state work, and it stays there.

**It cannot go into water.** New York does not permit aquatic use of minimum-risk pesticides, so there is no lawful route to larvicide the ornamental basin, the drive dry well or the hollow that holds after every storm. That water gets removed, drained, re-pitched, screened, circulated or engineered away.

There is accordingly no fogging calendar here and no barrier subscription, and nothing on this page should be read as a claim to clear the insect off a parcel. Three sides of this village are open water. The work is pressure reduction and source removal, and the part that lasts is the part where something physical about the property changed.

## How is a compound lot walked, and in what order?

Roof drainage first, then the parts of the ground nobody visits, then the house. The house is genuinely last.

**Roofs and leaders, every building on the parcel.** Residence, garage, pool house, greenhouse, workshop, gate lodge. Gutters checked from a ladder rather than from the lawn, because a run holding water looks identical from below to one that is not. Every downspout followed to wherever it ends, and on these lots a good number end at a basin, a dry well or a buried line nobody has checked in decades. A hose run into a gutter with someone watching the outlet settles in ten minutes what an argument cannot.

**The drive and its drainage.** Basins, trench drains and sumps along a long private drive, each tested for whether it still takes water or merely holds it.

**The pool enclosure.** Cover, coping, the equipment pad, the backwash discharge point, and the condition and schedule of circulation. Then the pool house gutters, which nobody has cleared.

**Boat and equipment storage.** Covers, hulls, tenders, racks, the tarpaulin over the outboard, and the ground beneath all of it.

**The storage edges.** Behind the garage, behind the pool house, beside the compost, under the deck, along the hedge line, and the side yard nobody walks through. Containers gather at the edges of a large property because the edges are where things get put down.

**The landscape and the irrigation.** Hollows that hold, best read a day or two after a storm. Heads throwing into shade or onto hard surface. Shaded beds kept permanently damp on a timer. Tree holes, stumps and hollow ornamentals.

**Water features.** Ornamental basins, fountains, bird baths, rain barrels, and whether each is moving, screened or standing.

**Then a look outward, as far as can be seen from inside the line.** The park edge, the club boundary, the neighboring pool, the boat under a tarpaulin next door. We cannot work where we have no access, but knowing what is there lets us say honestly what is achievable at this address.

The output is the three-part written list, with the items that are work for a gutter contractor or a grounds crew identified as theirs. A good deal of the most valuable work on a property like this is not ours to sell.

## When does each piece of this happen through the year?

Biting here runs from late May through September, which means the useful work happens before that.

**Winter.** Nothing is flying and this is the planning window. Tree permit applications go in now if a protected stem is involved. Drainage corrections get designed — a basin cleaned out, a dry well replaced, a leader run re-pitched, a hollow regraded. Containers in storage get emptied and, better, wiped out, because several container species leave eggs on the dry walls above the waterline and those eggs survive the winter there.

**April into early May.** The inventory, before the canopy closes and while the property is still legible. Pump the water off the pool cover and get the pool circulating. Clear and check the gutters on every building. Trace the leaders. Test the drive basins. Invert the containers, drain the boat, refit the covers. Every site removed in April produces nothing for the rest of the year, and the work is pleasanter because nothing is biting while it is done.

**Late May into June.** The first generations complete and overwintered eggs hatch with the warm rains. The weekly walk starts here, and on a lot this size it is a real fifteen minutes rather than a notional one.

**July and August.** Peak, and development is fastest in the heat. The lag people notice is real — a warm wet stretch produces a surge roughly a week to ten days later, because that is how long egg to adult takes. This is also when the pump gets switched off for a vacation and the cover sags, so the mid-season check earns more than it sounds.

**September.** Pressure holds later than most people expect, and a warm September keeps it. The discipline has to outlast the enthusiasm.

**October and November, after leaf fall.** The clearing that decides next spring. A gutter blocked by November leaves is a gutter full of water in April, and clearing it late rather than early is the version that works.
