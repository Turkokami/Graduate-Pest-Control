## Why do the garden-suburb villages produce most of the carpenter ant work here?

Because their walls hold water in places that cannot dry, and carpenter ants nest in wood that water has already softened.

Kensington and Russell Gardens were laid out as covenanted garden suburbs in the 1920s and early 1930s. That produced masonry and stucco walls, tile and slate roofs, deep overhanging eaves and generous attic volumes — a good, durable way to build, and one with a specific vulnerability. A rendered wall is a closed assembly. It resists water well until it is breached, and once water is behind it, the wall cannot dry outward the way a lapped timber or vinyl wall can. The moisture goes into the sheathing and the framing behind the lath and stays there.

That is the condition carpenter ants are looking for. They do not eat wood; they excavate galleries in it for nest space and push the debris out, and they choose timber that is decaying or water-softened because it is far easier to work and because the moisture keeps the brood alive. So a colony in a stucco wall on this peninsula is not a hygiene event or bad luck. It is a report on a building detail that has been failing quietly, often for years.

The deep eaves cut both ways. They keep rain off the field of the wall, which is why these houses often look immaculate and why owners are so surprised. But they concentrate what does get in at the details the eaves do not protect: parapets, chimney flashings, the junction where a lower roof meets a wall, and any elevation where a later addition was tied in.

The rest of the peninsula's housing contributes too. Roughly a quarter of the stock dates from 1939 or earlier and about two fifths from the 1940s and 1950s, so there is a large body of frame construction alongside the masonry, with the ordinary failures of that construction: porch framing, band joists, window sills and the wall behind a leaking gutter. But it is the rendered stock that produces the jobs where the ants are visible and the cause is not, and those are the ones that get treated repeatedly without ever being resolved. The [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) profile covers the insect; what follows is the wall.

## What is actually happening behind a cracked transition?

A slow, invisible wetting cycle that ends in soft sheathing and a colony.

Render cracks where the substrate moves differently on either side of a line. That means changes of plane, the head and jambs of window and door openings, the junction between an original wall and an addition, the base of a parapet, and anywhere a dissimilar material meets stucco — a stone sill, a timber beam end, a metal flashing.

Water enters at the crack in wind-driven rain, runs down the back of the render, and reaches whatever horizontal surface it meets first: a header, a sill, a shelf angle, a band joist. If flashing was installed and is intact, it goes back out. On a house of this age, flashing has frequently been painted over, corroded, or was never installed at that junction in the first place, so instead the water sits in the assembly. Each rain adds a little; each dry spell removes less than was added because the assembly cannot breathe outward. Over a decade the wood at that point moves from occasionally damp to permanently damp, decay fungi begin work, and the timber softens.

Then a queen finds it, or a satellite colony from a parent nest outdoors moves in. Neither event is the cause; both are consequences.

The practical consequence for the work is that treating the ants without correcting the transition can only ever be temporary in the sense that matters — the site stays attractive, and foraging ranges of around three hundred feet mean the peninsula's density puts several potential parent colonies within reach of any given wall. That is why our proposals on this stock routinely contain flashing, render repair and drainage items alongside anything to do with insects, and why a proposal that contains none of them has not looked at the building.

## Why does one apartment's ant problem belong to the whole building?

Because in a co-op the apartment is a set of rooms, not an envelope.

Around seventeen per cent of the peninsula's housing sits in buildings of fifty units or more, concentrated in and around Great Neck Plaza, which grew up around the 1925 railroad station as the peninsula's apartment and co-op core. In those buildings the pathways are internal and vertical: plumbing chases running floor to floor behind stacked kitchens and bathrooms, electrical risers, cable and conduit runs, the gap where a radiator riser passes through a floor slab, the void above a dropped ceiling, refuse rooms and compactor areas.

Small ants use all of it. A trail crossing a kitchen counter on the fourth floor typically arrives through the gap around a supply line under the sink or behind the escutcheon at a riser, and the nest is in the chase — which belongs to nobody in particular and to everyone collectively.

Treat that apartment alone and three things happen. The visible trail stops, which reads as success. The colony in the chase is untouched. And, depending on what was applied, the population may redistribute to the units above and below. Six weeks later two neighbours report ants, the original unit sees them again, and the building concludes that the pest control is not working. It worked exactly as far as its scope allowed.

What resolves it is treating the pathway: sealing pipe penetrations at the escutcheons on every floor of the affected stack, closing the chase where it opens into cabinetry, dealing with the refuse room, and placing material where the colony travels rather than where the complaint came from. That is a building decision made by a board or a managing agent rather than by a resident, which makes it a governance question before it is a technical one. Responsibilities are outlined at [co-op and condo board obligations](/compliance/coop-condo-board-obligations/), and the programme structure is described under [co-op and condo pest control](/commercial/coop-condo-pest-control/).

## Which ants are actually in a Plaza kitchen?

Three candidates, and the identification changes the entire approach.

**Odorous house ants.** Small, dark, moving in defined trails, with a faintly rotten smell when crushed. They nest in shallow, unstable places and relocate readily, which is why a trail can appear within a day of heavy rain and why an established colony may occupy several nest sites at once. In an apartment building the nest sites are often inside wall voids and under insulation rather than outdoors. The critical operational point is that spraying a trail with a repellent product can split the colony into more nests than existed before. See the [odorous house ant](/pest-library/odorous-house-ant/) profile.

**Pharaoh ants.** Very small, yellowish to light brown, and unable to survive a New York winter outdoors — which means a colony that is present is permanently indoors, living in warm voids in the building itself. Multi-unit residential buildings, and any building with continuous heat and a kitchen, are their natural habitat. They also bud aggressively in response to repellent treatment, fragmenting into multiple satellite colonies distributed through the structure. This is the species where the wrong first move does the most lasting damage, and it is the reason a competent operator will insist on baiting rather than spraying and will want a building-wide picture before starting. The [pharaoh ant](/pest-library/pharaoh-ant/) profile covers it properly.

**Pavement ants.** Coming up through joints in a slab, at the edge of a terrace, through cracks in a courtyard or a basement floor. Around the Plaza, where buildings meet paved surfaces on all sides, they are common at street and cellar level. See [pavement ant](/pest-library/pavement-ant/).

Carpenter ants also occur in these buildings, usually where a roof or terrace detail has been leaking into a top-floor wall or a parapet, or where a planter has kept a courtyard wall permanently wet. Top-floor apartments on the older Plaza-area buildings are the ones to watch, because a parapet or a roof-edge detail that has been admitting water for a decade produces exactly the softened framing the species wants, and the resident below the roof is the only person positioned to notice.

Ground-floor units add their own version. Where a terrace, a planter or a raised bed sits against the building, foragers have a covered route from soil to the wall and the soil itself holds moisture against the structure. A ground-floor apartment reporting large black ants in spring, on a building with planting up against the courtyard elevation, is a predictable combination rather than a coincidence.

Getting the identification right is not academic. Three of these four respond to different materials, two of them get materially worse if sprayed, and one of them tells you there is a structural moisture fault to find.

## Where is the nest on an estate lot in Kings Point?

Frequently outdoors, and the survey has to go and look.

Kings Point, Saddle Rock and the wooded parts of Great Neck Estates have depth, mature canopy and long-established planting. On lots like those, the parent colony is often not in the building at all: it is in a tree with heartwood decay, a dead limb still up in the canopy, a stump left after a storm loss, an old timber retaining structure, a woodpile, or a garden building with a rotten sill. What is inside the house is a satellite — workers and brood moved into a convenient interior void, connected to the parent nest by a route the ants follow consistently.

That changes what a survey covers. Trees within reach of the building, and specifically any limb touching or overhanging the roof, because a limb in contact with a gutter is a bridge that renders every treatment at ground level irrelevant. Outbuildings, pool houses, garden walls and pergolas. Timber edging and retaining structures. Firewood, which on these properties is often substantial and often stored against a wall.

It also changes the timing of the work. Carpenter ants are largely nocturnal, so a torch after dark along the fascia, the soffit line and the trunks of nearby trees shows trails that are invisible at midday. Following that trail outward on an estate lot regularly ends at a tree fifty feet from the house, and treating the tree resolves what six visits to the kitchen would not have.

The other estate-specific finding is ivy. Established ivy on a masonry wall holds moisture against the render, hides the entire wall from inspection, and provides a sheltered vertical highway from the ground to the eaves. Removing it is unpopular and it is frequently the single most effective item on the list.

## What does working across nine villages involve in practice?

More administration than a single-jurisdiction market, and it is worth knowing before the first visit.

The peninsula's nine incorporated villages have their own codes, and they are not identical. What requires a permit, what may be altered at a foundation line, how trees and landscaping are regulated and what hours work may be carried out in all vary by address. On a peninsula where a five-minute drive crosses three village boundaries, confirming which one a property sits in is a genuine first step rather than a formality.

Layered on top of that is New York's neighbor notification law, in force in Nassau County. Under it, a commercial applicator has to notify the owners and occupants of abutting properties in writing ahead of a covered outdoor lawn application, and mark the site visually. Where a village lot backs onto three or four other gardens, that list of neighbours is not short, and the notification has to be built into the schedule rather than handled on the day. The detail is at [the New York neighbor notification law](/compliance/ny-neighbor-notification-law/), and the licensing framework is summarised at [New York State pesticide applicator requirements](/compliance/nys-pesticide-applicator-requirements/).

There is a practical conclusion in that. A programme built on repeated outdoor application in a dense village generates notification, cost and neighbour friction every time it runs. A programme built on finding the nest, treating it directly and correcting the water that made the site attractive generates none of them after the work is done. On this peninsula the regulatory environment and the correct technical approach happen to point the same way.

## How does the work run, and in what order?

Identify, locate, treat what the colony carries home, correct the water, then verify.

**Identify.** Species first, always, for the reasons above. A photograph on a pale surface with something for scale is usually enough to narrow it, and a specimen is better.

**Locate.** For carpenter ants: frass — coarse pale sawdust with insect fragments in it, falling directly below a gallery slot — plus sounding, plus nocturnal trail-following. In a rendered house the frass often appears at the base of a wall, on a terrace, or on a windowsill below the affected assembly rather than anywhere obvious. For trailing ants in a building: follow the trail to where it enters the room and then find where that void goes.

**Treat what gets carried back.** Baiting or direct treatment of a located gallery, not a contact spray along a visible trail. A spray kills the ants on the trail, leaves the colony, and with two of the species above actively makes things worse.

**Correct the water and the openings.** Re-flash the transition. Repair the render properly rather than skimming over the crack. Clear the gutter that has been overflowing onto the same corner for six years. Cut the planting back off the wall. Seal the escutcheons and the chase penetrations in a building. This is the half that decides whether the problem returns.

**Verify.** In a house, the following spring. In a building, by watching the units adjoining the one that complained, because that is where a displaced colony shows up first.

## What should a resident or a board do first?

Different things, and confusing the two wastes months.

**A resident in a co-op or a rented apartment** should document rather than treat. Photograph the trail where it enters the room, not just the ants on the counter — where it comes through the wall is the only detail that helps anyone. Note the time of day, the date, and whether it followed rain or a change in the weather. Collect a few insects on tape or in a sealed container; identification from a photograph of a moving trail is often impossible and the species decides the treatment. Then report it to the managing agent in writing, and say specifically where the trail enters. A report that says "ants in the kitchen" produces a visit to the kitchen. A report that says "ants entering at the gap behind the escutcheon under the sink, every evening since the rain last Tuesday" produces work on the riser.

What a resident should not do is buy a spray and use it along the trail. With two of the species common in these buildings that reaction fragments the colony, spreads it through the structure, and turns a contained problem into a building-wide one. It also destroys the evidence needed to find the source.

**A board or managing agent** should treat a single unit's report as a sample rather than an incident. Ask whether the units directly above and below have reported anything in the last year, because a vertical pattern in a single stack is the clearest signal there is that the colony is in the chase. Ask for the identification in writing. Ask where the technician believes the nest is, and treat any answer that names a product rather than a location as an incomplete answer.

**A homeowner in one of the village houses** should walk the outside before calling. Look at where each downspout discharges and whether the ground falls toward or away from the wall. Look for cracking in the render, particularly above openings and at changes of plane, and for staining below those cracks. Look for coarse pale sawdust at the base of walls, on terraces and on window sills, and photograph it where it lies rather than sweeping it up — the pile is the only thing that says which side of the building the gallery is on. And look up: any branch touching the roof is worth noting before anything else is discussed.

## What does the ant calendar look like on the peninsula?

Four phases, with one that is specific to the multifamily stock.

**March to May.** Activity resumes as structures warm. Indoor sightings ahead of any outdoor foraging point to a nest inside the heated envelope, which is a useful diagnostic in both a house and an apartment building. Winged carpenter ant reproductives may appear and are routinely reported as termite swarmers, and on a peninsula with this much multifamily stock the report usually reaches us through a managing agent rather than through the person who found the insect, which is all the more reason to settle it early. The pinched waist and the elbowed antennae decide it, and the [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile shows what the other insect looks like. Termites are not work this firm takes, so an owner who has genuinely found them needs a different contractor; a carpenter ant reproductive means the rest of this page applies.

**June to August.** Peak foraging, and the period when frass accumulates visibly. Odorous house ants surge after each heavy rain as their shallow nests flood. On terraces and in courtyards, pavement ant activity peaks at the same time.

**September to October.** Foraging tails off and satellite colonies consolidate. Work is still worthwhile; confirmation has to wait.

**November to February.** Here is the multifamily exception. In a detached house, ants seen in deep winter mean a nest inside the heated part of the building. In a large heated apartment building there is no seasonal lull at all — pharaoh ants in particular run continuously, because the building never gets cold and the food supply never stops. A Plaza co-op reporting ants in January is not unusual and should not be dismissed as an anomaly; it is the normal behaviour of an indoor species in an indoor habitat.
