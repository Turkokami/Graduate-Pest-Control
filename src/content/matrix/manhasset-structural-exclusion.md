## What is the envelope of a pre-war Manhasset house, exactly?

Not a surface. A stack of construction eras that meet each other at seams, and the seams are the work.

On a house built in the last thirty years, the envelope is a defined assembly: a continuous foundation, a sealed sill, a sheathed and wrapped wall, a soffit and fascia system, a roof with manufactured closures. You can point at it. Exclusion on a building like that is finding the handful of places where the assembly was interrupted for a service and closing them.

Manhasset does not have many houses like that. The census area's median construction year is 1942, and roughly 47 per cent of units date from 1939 or earlier — the oldest profile on this part of the North Shore. On a typical house here the foundation is rubble, fieldstone, early block or early poured concrete. The frame sits on a timber sill laid across it. The wall is board sheathing with no membrane in the modern sense, clad in clapboard, shingle, stucco or a later re-siding. The roof edge is a built-up cornice with applied trim, sometimes with the gutter formed inside it. And the whole thing has been altered — a porch enclosed, a kitchen extended, a dormer cut, a bathroom added, a system replaced — repeatedly, over eighty or ninety years.

Each of those alterations meets the original building at a junction where the framing does not align, where flashing was improvised, and where a void got left because closing it properly would have meant opening more of the house than the budget allowed. That is what exclusion here is actually addressing. The general method is set out under [structural exclusion](/pest-control/structural-exclusion/); what follows is the version of it that a house of this age requires.

The governing point, and it is worth stating before any of the detail: on this housing stock the work is masonry and carpentry with some sealing at the end. A contractor who arrives with a case of sealant and a bag of foam is equipped for a different building.

## Why does repointing come before sealing on these foundations?

Because the failure is inside the joint, not on its face, and anything applied over it fails with it.

A rubble or fieldstone wall is irregular stone with mortar packed into what the mason could reach. It was never monolithic. Eighty years of freeze and thaw have cracked, softened and emptied a proportion of those joints, sometimes right through the wall. On a cold clear day it is common to see daylight through one of these walls in several places.

The correct repair is the traditional one. Rake the joint back until you reach sound mortar — which may be an inch, may be four — brush and damp the surfaces, and repack it in lifts with a mortar that suits the wall. That last part matters more than people expect. A hard, dense modern mix packed into a soft old wall does not move with it, and it drives the stress into the stone instead of the joint. On a wall of this age the mortar should be the sacrificial element, softer than what it sits between.

What does not work is sealant across the face. It bonds to loose material, it seals in moisture that the joint was previously letting out, and the first serious freeze takes the bead and the remaining loose mortar off together, leaving a larger opening than there was to begin with. The same objection applies to a skim of surface render over a failing wall.

Two further points specific to these foundations.

**The wall has an interior.** Where a stone wall is thick and its core is loose rubble, animals move inside it. Repointing the cellar face closes their exit into the room without closing the route. On the worst of these walls, the work has to include closing the top of the wall where it meets the sill so the wall's interior stops connecting to the building's.

**Drainage decides how long it lasts.** Mortar in a permanently wet wall fails again. If a leader discharges at a corner, or a terrace turns water back at the foundation, the repointing there has a service life measured in a few seasons. Correcting the water is part of this scope, not a separate trade's problem to raise later.

## What has to happen at the sill line, and why is it the hardest part?

Because it runs the whole way round the building, it is concealed, and on a house of this era it has been opening continuously since it was built.

The sill line is the junction where the wooden frame meets the top of the foundation: sill plate on masonry, band joist above, siding lapping down over both. On a modern house it is sealed and capped. Here it was timber laid on stone with the cladding brought down over it. Settlement, seasonal movement and two or three re-sidings later, it is a horizontal opening running around most of the perimeter, hidden behind the lowest course of siding and generally buried in planting that has been growing there since the 1940s.

Doing it properly requires three things people are reluctant to authorise.

**Access.** Foundation planting has to be cut back to expose the wall. Where a terrace or a walk has been laid up against the house, the top of the foundation may be below the paving, in which case the junction cannot be closed at all until the grade relationship is corrected. This is the single most common reason an exclusion scope on this stock arrives with a landscaping item at the top of it.

**A decision about the cladding.** Reaching the junction sometimes means removing the bottom course of siding or shingle and refitting it afterwards. On a house where the siding is original, that is a conversation to have before the work, not during.

**The right closure.** The gap gets a mechanical barrier — mesh or sheet metal, fixed and bedded — with sealant only as the finishing layer over it. Where the timber has lost section, the timber gets replaced. There is no version of this that works as a bead of caulk pushed into a gap and tooled smooth.

A note on what this achieves. Closing the sill line is the highest-value single item on most Manhasset houses, because it removes the openings at exactly the height animals are already travelling, and because it is the route that connects the outside of the building to the wall cavities. In much of the pre-1940 frame stock those cavities run uninterrupted from sill to roof, which is why an entry at grade turns into noise in an upstairs ceiling. The consequences of that are covered under [rodent control in Manhasset](/pest-control/manhasset/rodent-control/); the [house mouse](/pest-library/house-mouse/) profile covers the animal.

## How do you close a cornice return without rebuilding the roof edge?

Usually you cannot, and pretending otherwise is where this work goes wrong on Colonial Revival houses.

The cornice return is the small boxed section where the eave trim wraps the corner and stops against the gable end. It has a flat or near-flat top, a mitred external corner, and junctions to the wall on two planes. It collects water, the mitre opens as the timber moves, and the interior of the box stays damp for months at a time. Given eighty years, a proportion of them are soft.

That produces two problems at once. It is an entry — into the cornice, and from there along the top plate and into the wall cavity or the attic. And it is prime nest material for the insects described under [ant control in Manhasset](/pest-control/manhasset/ant-control/), because damp softened wood is what a carpenter ant colony excavates.

Closing it honestly means opening it. A return that sounds hollow or gives under an awl is not sealed shut; it is cut out, the framing behind it is assessed, the rotten material is replaced, and the box is rebuilt and flashed so water leaves rather than sits. Closing the visible gap on a soft return produces a sealed box of decaying wood with a colony in it, which fails within a couple of seasons and is worse than doing nothing because everyone now believes it is finished.

The same logic applies at the rest of the roof edge on these houses:

**Fascia and drip edge.** Older cornices frequently have no drip edge at all, so the gap between the fascia top and the roof deck is open along its whole length.

**Where a lower roof plane dies into a wall.** A porch or a bay roof meeting a wall is a flashing junction, and old step flashing that has been roofed over twice usually has gaps at the top course.

**Gable louvers.** Decorative on a Colonial Revival elevation and rarely screened on the inside face, or screened in a material that has rusted through.

**Built-in gutters.** Where the gutter is formed inside the cornice and lined with metal, a failed liner puts water directly into the framing. The exclusion work and the roofing work are the same work at that point.

Roofline closures are also where timing becomes a biological question rather than a scheduling one. Sealing a roof void in late spring or early summer can trap flightless young inside the structure. Where bats are present that is both inhumane and a considerable odour problem, and the sequence is set by the animal — see the [big brown bat](/pest-library/big-brown-bat/) profile and the approach under [wildlife management](/pest-control/wildlife-management/).

## What do you do with a stucco wall that has no clean termination?

Establish where it stops, and if it does not stop cleanly, make it.

The Spanish-influenced houses in Plandome Heights, built on what had been the Benjamin Duke estate, carry white stucco over frame under red tile. A rendered wall is a reservoir: it takes water in and releases it slowly, and it cannot dry outward at any speed. Where it cracks — at changes of plane, window heads, parapets and the junction where render meets a timber element — the water goes to the sheathing and stays.

For exclusion purposes the critical detail is the bottom edge. A rendered finish should stop above finished grade over a screed or termination bead, leaving a visible, inspectable gap between soil and wall. On a house of this era that detail is frequently absent, or it existed and has since been buried by a raised bed. Where the render runs into the ground, there is no junction anybody can see and no way to tell what is travelling behind it.

The remedy is not to seal along the bottom of the render, which traps water in the wall and accelerates everything you were trying to prevent. It is to lower the grade back, expose the termination, and if there is no termination, cut one and detail it so water behind the render can leave.

Parapets need the same discipline. A parapet is exposed on both faces and on top, and its coping is the only protection its core has. Coping joints on these houses have generally been repointed at least once and are open again. Water entering the top of a parapet arrives at framing at the roof line, which is exactly where the exclusion work is most difficult.

And cracks in the field of the render get filled because they are water entries, not because anything is walking through them. Keeping those two purposes separate is what stops a scope from turning into cosmetic work billed as pest work.

## Which openings must stay open?

Several, and closing them is one of the more expensive mistakes available on an old building.

**Foundation and crawlspace vents.** Where a crawlspace is ventilated by design, blocking the vents changes the moisture regime of the framing above. The correct treatment is a robust metal screen in a frame that can be removed for inspection, not a plug.

**Any drainage detail.** Weep paths, a stucco screed, the outlet of a French drain, the gap under a threshold that lets a porch deck drain. These exist to let water out. Excluding insects and animals from them means mesh sized to the animal while passing water.

**Flue and appliance terminations.** A chimney needs a proper cap with a spark arrestor screen, not a board. A dryer vent needs a working damper and a guard, not a blocked hood — a blocked dryer vent is a fire risk and an obvious one.

**Combustion and mechanical air.** Anything supplying air to an appliance stays open and gets screened.

**Attic ventilation.** Ridge and soffit ventilation is doing a job. Screened, not stopped.

The general rule is easy to state and constantly ignored: exclusion changes what can pass through an opening, not whether the opening exists. Where an opening has a function, the work protects the function.

## How does the work respect a Colonial Revival elevation?

By keeping almost all of it out of sight, and by treating the visible remainder as a finishing question.

Munsey Park was laid out by the Olmsted Brothers in 1927 with building standards specifying Colonial Revival, and 162 houses were complete by 1930. The streets are named for American artists and the neighbourhood still reads as a designed whole. Owners here are protective of the elevations, reasonably so, and a proposal that involves visible galvanised mesh across the front of a house is a proposal that will be declined.

It rarely needs to be visible. The productive closures are at grade behind planting, at the sill under the siding, inside the cornice, in the attic behind a louver, at the back of a garage, and around service penetrations that are already painted out. Where something must show, three things make it acceptable: set the mesh back from the face rather than surface-mounting it, use a dark or copper finish that reads as shadow, and paint sheet metal to match the trim it sits in.

Repointing is the same discipline in masonry. Colour and profile can be matched to the surrounding joints, and a joint struck to the same profile as its neighbours disappears at ten feet. A wide, proud, light-coloured band of new mortar across a stone wall does not, and it cannot be undone.

There is a practical reason to care beyond appearance. Work that looks acceptable gets authorised. On this housing stock, the closures that matter most are frequently the ones an owner has previously refused because the last contractor's version of them was ugly.

## Who approves exterior work here — the hamlet or the village?

It depends which side of a boundary the house is on, and Manhasset is unusually mixed in this respect.

The hamlet itself is unincorporated and administered by the Town of North Hempstead. Attempts to incorporate ran through the 1940s and again as recently as 2016 and failed, which is part of why the building fabric here was never shaped by a single village architectural review board and why the housing is more varied than in the villages that ring it.

Those villages — Munsey Park, Plandome, Plandome Heights, Plandome Manor, Flower Hill and North Hills — are separately incorporated, and each has its own administration. So two houses a few hundred yards apart can sit under different arrangements for anything that counts as exterior alteration.

Most exclusion work is repair and maintenance and does not raise these questions. Some of it can: replacing a section of roof edge, altering a chimney termination, changing grade or drainage, removing or rebuilding a masonry element, or work on a property inside a designated historic area — the Valley Road Historic District being the obvious local case. The sensible habit is to establish which jurisdiction a property is in before scoping anything structural, and to be candid when an item is likely to need someone else's sign-off rather than discovering it after the scaffold is up.

## What does the sequence look like when the building is this old?

Longer than on a modern house, and the first two steps are the ones people want to skip.

**Survey, with the perimeter cleared.** Stored goods off the cellar walls, planting cut back, and time to read the whole wall with a raking light. Every elevation, the roofline, the outbuildings, the garage. On a property with a garden store, a pool house or a detached garage, those get surveyed too, because that is frequently where a population lives before it moves into the house.

**Reduce the existing interior population before closing anything.** Sealing a building with animals inside removes their way out and concentrates them in the wall voids, and in a house with continuous stud bays that means something dying in the middle of an exterior wall on the second floor.

**Then the masonry.** Repointing, filling redundant openings with masonry, closing the tops of walls at the sill.

**Then the carpentry.** Cornice returns, fascia and drip edge, soffit closures, sill replacement where section has been lost, garage door seals that actually meet the jamb, and the abandoned service openings — coal chutes, milk doors, thimbles, redundant fuel lines — cut out and made good rather than boarded over.

**Then the water.** Leader extensions, grade correction, gutter and liner repair. This is regularly the largest item and it is the one that decides how long the rest lasts.

**Then the screens and the finishing seal.** Vents, flue caps, weep and drainage details, and sealant used as the last layer over a mechanical closure rather than instead of one.

**Then verification**, under conditions that actually reveal failures — after heavy weather, after settlement, and after the first freeze-and-thaw cycle on repointed masonry.

## How do you tell whether the work was actually done?

By what the record says, and by what happens in the spring.

A defensible record on a house like this is not a service ticket. It is a set of locations: a plan or an annotated set of elevations marking every opening found, what it was, what it was closed with, and dated photographs at each one — ideally before and after, because after alone tells you nothing about what was behind it. It also names the openings that were found and not closed, with the reason: inaccessible under a terrace, requires siding removal, needs the owner's decision on a planting bed, sits behind a neighbour's boundary structure.

That last list is the honest part of the document and the part most likely to be omitted. A perimeter that is ninety per cent closed is not ninety per cent effective, and an owner is entitled to know which ten per cent remains and what it would take to finish it.

Then the return visit. Old masonry moves. A repointed joint that has been through a winter, a sheet metal closure that has been through a wet autumn, and a sill detail that has settled are all things you inspect after the fact rather than assume. On this housing stock a spring check is part of the job rather than an additional service.
