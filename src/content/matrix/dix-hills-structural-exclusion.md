## What is the actual perimeter of a Dix Hills property?

Considerably longer than the outside of the house, and running round four or five separate buildings.

This is the first correction most scopes on this stock need. On a compact lot it is reasonable to treat the exterior wall of the residence as the boundary: walk it, close what you find, finish. On an acre in a hamlet where 7,647 of roughly 8,067 housing units are detached houses, that describes a fraction of the job.

**The house.** Late-suburban rather than post-war: two stories more often than one, a full basement, multiple roof planes, an attached two-car garage, bay or box window projections, and fifty years of additions on top.

**The attached garage.** Two cars wide on most of this stock, sharing a framed wall with the house on one side and carrying a wide overhead door on another.

**The detached structures.** A pool house, a workshop, a shed on blocks or a settled slab, a barn on the older parcels, a generator enclosure, a pool equipment pad behind a screen. None heated, most visited seasonally, and each capable of holding a population through a summer.

**The ground plane.** On an acre this holds more cover than the buildings do: the void beneath a raised deck, the gravel under a shed, the log store, the compost bay, brush along the boundary, and beds that have gained an inch of mulch every April since 1972.

**The wood line,** which on this ground is not scenery. It is the staging area, and the margin between it and the mown lawn is the most productive strip on the parcel.

Close the residence alone and you have finished a fifth of a parcel, and next October the shed restocks the house. That is why the survey here walks the lot before it touches the house, and it is why exclusion on this stock is priced from the property rather than from the building.

## Why does the roofline carry as much of this as the foundation?

Because the canopy delivers animals to it, and a late-suburban roof has far more places to arrive than a ranch does.

These subdivisions were laid out through existing woodland on the Harbor Hill moraine rather than across cleared farmland, and fifty years later the mature canopy that makes the lots desirable stands at and above roof height. Where a limb touches any part of the structure, what Cornell calls a natural bridge exists and the roof becomes reachable without climbing a wall. Once an animal is up there it can walk the whole covering, and on these houses that is a great deal of covering.

The openings it finds are consistent enough to inspect in a fixed order.

**Soffit returns.** The small boxed section at the base of a dormer cheek or where a roof edge dies into a wall. Frequently a void with a thin board across it and nothing behind, and invisible from inside the building.

**Valleys and roof-to-wall junctions.** A house with multiple planes has many. Each concentrates water and debris, and the flashing at the bottom of one is the part that has been quietly failing.

**Gable louvres,** where the screening behind a decorative vent has perished.

**Ridge vents,** where the closure strip at the ends has compressed or the product's own baffle has degraded.

**Fascia and drip-edge gaps,** and the point where a gutter has pulled the fascia away from the rafter tails.

**Chimney terminations** without a proper cap, on houses that mostly have one and sometimes two flues.

The work up there has to be rigid, mechanically fixed and non-corroding, because a bead of anything flexible on a moving joint at height has a short life. It is also ladder work at a genuine height on a two-story house, which is an argument for doing the whole roof edge in one visit — closures, vent screening, flashing checks and terminals — rather than returning three times for three findings.

And the cheapest item on the list is not closure at all. Pruning branches back off the roof line removes the access rather than sealing the destination, and unlike a closure it does not need maintaining fifty feet up. What arrives at a roof edge on this ground is more often a wildlife matter than a rodent one; the [eastern gray squirrel](/pest-library/eastern-gray-squirrel/) profile describes how that reads from inside an attic, and the service is [wildlife management](/pest-control/wildlife-management/).

## What has to be closed on the outbuildings?

The same details as the house, on buildings nobody has looked at since they were put up.

A detached structure on a large lot fails in a short list of places, and because it is unheated and unfinished the failures are usually visible without opening anything.

**The slab edge or the base course.** Where a wall plate sits on a slab, the joint was rarely sealed and the slab has moved. On a shed set on blocks or on gravel, the whole underside is open ground.

**The door.** A service door with a worn or missing sweep, a threshold that has dropped, or a pair of barn-style doors that no longer meet. On a detached garage the bottom corners of the overhead door are the dependable failure. Fifty winters have moved the apron, and a seal that reads as tight along the middle of the opening is standing clear of the slab at each end of it.

**The roof-to-wall junction,** which on a simple outbuilding is often a single line of daylight along the top of a wall.

**Gable ends and vents,** frequently unscreened because the building was never conditioned.

**Service penetrations,** because these buildings have power, often water, sometimes gas, and on a pool house a full set of plumbing.

What makes the outbuildings worth doing properly rather than tidily is what they store. Seed, feed, fertiliser, bulk pet food, cushions, timber offcuts and cardboard are food, nest material and cover, and a building that provides all three within a hundred feet of a house will keep supplying it. Closing the structure and clearing its floor are one item, not two.

## What do six trades leave in one corner of a house?

A cluster of penetrations, which is the highest-yield twenty feet on most of these properties.

The buried infrastructure gives a sense of the scale. Seventeen wells and three storage tanks feed a network of 169 miles here, carrying 1,282 hydrants and upward of 2,386 valves, and serving something like 8,400 properties. Every service off it ends at a hole in somebody's foundation, and that hole was cored or sleeved rather than sealed.

Then five decades of additions concentrate around the same corners. Central air with a refrigerant line set and a condensate drain. Irrigation supply, controller feed and valve boxes. Pool circulation and heater lines. A standby generator with its gas run and transfer switch feed. A car charger. A replacement condenser on a new pad. Camera and network cable. A propane line to a range. Each one cut on its own afternoon by a different firm, none of whom came back.

They cluster because convenience clusters. The plant is in one corner of the basement, the meters and the panel are near the drive, and every subsequent trade went where the services already were. So on a house with a perimeter measured in the low hundreds of feet, a disproportionate share of the actual openings sit within a few paces of each other.

Closing them is specific work. The annulus around each pipe or conduit packed and closed at the wall face rather than smeared over. Sheet metal where a chewable timber edge is exposed. Hydraulic cement at masonry. A proper terminal, with a working flap, on anything that exhausts. And the condensate and pump discharges re-routed away from the foundation rather than closed, because they have to keep working and because a condensate line that has been wetting the same square yard of soil for twenty years softens the baseboard and the sill above it, and soft wood at a foundation corner is a decay finding before it is a pest one. It is also the entry a carpenter ant colony prefers, which puts that half of the job under [ant control](/pest-control/ant-control/).

## What happens where an addition meets the original frame?

Nothing good, and on this stock almost every house has at least one.

Family houses on large lots, held by the same owners for long periods, get altered rather than replaced. A rear extension. A sunroom. A converted garage. An enclosed porch. A dormer. A finished basement. New windows. Added insulation. Each of them lands on a frame put up in 1968 or 1972, and the line where the two meet is the part that matters.

Reading them roughly in the order they happen:

**Replacement windows.** The old rough opening is left in place and a narrower frame dropped inside it. Whatever the fitter had in the van went into the space between the two, and behind the casing that space runs right round the opening and lets into the stud bay.

**Added insulation.** A warmer wall, unchanged openings, and a covering over every defect a later survey needs to see.

**A finished basement.** Studwork, insulation and board now stand in front of the band joist inside while planting stands in front of it outside, so the building's most important junction is hidden on both faces. What is left to look at is the plant room, whatever access panel exists, the space under the stair and the outside of the wall, and the scope should name the elevations nobody managed to see.

**A rear extension or a dormer.** Four seams in a single job: fresh framing let into old, one roof plane dying into another, a soffit run terminating against the original, and two pours of different ages meeting below grade.

**A converted garage.** The overhead door opening filled with a stud wall and cladding on the outer face, the slab left where it was, and the joint between old slab and new wall the least-considered detail in the building.

**A sunroom or screened porch,** usually on its own shallow footing, frequently with a floor structure close to grade and a shallow crawl void beneath that nobody has been into.

Work it by date rather than by elevation. Ask what has been altered and in which decade, then go and look at every seam that answer implies. On a stock this consistent, the likely alteration list can be predicted before the owner has finished describing the house.

## Which openings have to stay open?

Every one that moves air or discharges something, and on a big complex roof getting this wrong is expensive.

Owners who have just found something living in an attic want the whole building shut. On a big roof carrying a deep insulation layer, shutting the vents puts winter condensation on the back of the sheathing, and wet sheathing is a decay and carpenter ant problem worth considerably more than the squirrels were.

**Ridge, gable and soffit ventilation** is a system, and it is what keeps a roof structure dry and a covering at a sensible temperature. Where the soffit panels are perforated, those perforations are the intake. Anything fitted there is a rigid screened closure set behind the panel run, never a panel packed solid.

**Crawlspace and foundation vents,** where this stock has them, take metal screening in a gauge that lasts, mounted so somebody can check it in five years without dismantling anything.

**Dryer, bathroom and range terminations** get the right terminal with a flap that moves. A dryer duct with mesh across it packs with lint in one winter, at which point it is a fire question as well as a moisture one.

**Combustion air openings** are not available for closure at all. They belong to the heating contractor and they keep people alive.

**Condensate and pump discharges** keep running; where one is wetting a wall it gets taken further out, never capped.

**Weep holes in brick veneer,** on the houses here with masonry fronts, are drainage. They get stainless or copper mesh, never sealant.

The principle is short enough to hand to an owner: tighter against animals, never tighter against air that has a job to do.

## What material survives on a 1970s house?

Rigid material, matched to the substrate and fixed mechanically — which rules out most of what gets used.

This stock presents four surfaces and each wants something different. **Poured concrete** is the easy one. Cracks and sleeve annuli take hydraulic cement, and because each opening has edges you can see when it is finished. **Timber** — the sill plate, the band joist, a rafter tail, a fascia — wants rigid closure driven into sound stock, with metal over any edge a [house mouse](/pest-library/house-mouse/) could get its teeth around. Animals start on edges, not faces, so removing the edge beats sealing across it. **Aluminum** at soffit and fascia takes a fixed rigid closure rather than sealant, because the panel moves seasonally. **Cladding** — cedar, vinyl, aluminum or brick veneer on this stock — sheds rain and stops nothing else, so the work goes in behind it against the sheathing and the studs. Running a bead along the outer face only removes the evidence.

What goes on instead is a short list. Galvanised sheet metal in 26 gauge, fabricated for the opening it is closing rather than trimmed from stock and made to look near enough. Mesh in copper or stainless where an annulus needs packing and a rusting fill would run a stain down the elevation. Hardware cloth on anything that has to keep breathing. Mortar and hydraulic cement into masonry. Xcluder sweeps at doors and Xcluder fill fabric around services. Xcluder GEO below grade wherever something is digging. And screws into sound material on all of it, because glue is a bet on a surface nobody cleaned.

Foam is the one product that needs saying out loud: it does not go on a job of ours, in any position in any assembly. Cured foam is chewed through without difficulty, and the greater damage is what it does to the record — an opening filled with it photographs as complete, so nobody returns to it. On a parcel with five buildings and a rotating cast of trades, that misreading survives for years. We removed the argument by removing the product.

## When is closure the wrong move?

When there are dependent young inside, and on wooded ground that is a live question twice a year.

Closure on its own does nothing about animals already living inside, and applied in that situation it is actively worse, because what gets sealed is the way out. On a house with a finished basement, boxed chases and a room above the garage, anything that dies in one of those voids has to be cut out of it. Reduction comes first, and indoors it belongs where the result can be picked up afterwards.

Wildlife adds a timing constraint with real force here. Cornell notes that some species enter the upper part of a building while others come in at the foundation, and that a one-way door will often just move the entry point rather than resolve it. More importantly, closing an entry when flightless or immobile young are inside a wall or an attic creates a considerably worse problem — for the animals and for the building — than the one you started with. That is checked before scheduling rather than assumed, and where it applies the correct answer is to sequence around it.

There are two other honest limits. Where the real source is next door — a neighboring woodpile, an unsecured outbuilding, an excavation — closing your buildings protects them without reducing what is outside them. And where the substrate is no longer sound, closure is not worth doing until the substrate is repaired; a fixing into decayed timber is a fixing that will let go.

## In what order does the work run on a wooded acre?

Ground, then outbuildings, then the house at grade, then the roof. Complaints arrive in precisely the opposite order.

**The ground plane.** Cut planting back off the base of every building. Shift the log store, the compost bay and anything leaning on a wall onto hard standing well clear of the building. Put what is lying on the floor of the garage, the basement store and each shed onto shelving. Reduce bed heights where they have closed the clearance at the cladding. Extend leaders so roof water leaves the building. Widen the mown margin at the wood line and clear the brush at the boundary. Every item there is a Saturday rather than a trade, and each one lowers what the parcel can support.

**The outbuildings.** Slab edge, doors and sweeps, roof-to-wall junction, vents, penetrations, and the floor cleared.

**The band joist line and the penetration cluster.** With the base of the house visible, this becomes a survey rather than a search.

**The attached garage.** Door seal and the apron beneath it, service door threshold, the top plate of the shared wall, and every penetration through that wall.

**The seams,** taken in the order the alterations were built: extension, dormer, filled opening, replacement windows.

**The roof edge,** all of it in one visit.

**Then the canopy,** pruned back from every plane.

**Then verify in the cold.** A December morning on a closed property shows whether the closure was real, and it is the only test that matters.

The administrative note is brief. Closing a gap, screening a vent, packing a sleeve, hanging a sweep and replacing a duct terminal are all maintenance. Cutting out a rotten sill, forming a vent where there was none, rebuilding a deck or a porch, holding back grade with a wall, opening a roof — those are construction, and construction is filed. Two attempts at incorporation, one in the 1950s and one in 2001, came to nothing, so there is no village authority here and the Town of Huntington issues every permit. One desk, which is a simplification compared with markets that straddle a town line.

## What does the record have to show, and what undoes it?

Located items with materials and photographs, an honest list of what could not be reached, and a household that knows what to watch.

The document you should get is a schedule rather than an essay. Openings numbered and located, with the finding, the material and a photograph against each. Ventilated openings with the screen type and gauge written down. Drainage and clearance items with the trade that owns them written beside them. And a closing list of everything the survey could not reach, and why not. On a property with five buildings and a wood line, a report showing nothing unreachable has usually not been everywhere it claims.

Four things take this apart over a year, and the material is not one of them.

**Somebody put a new hole in the wall.** A standby generator, a car charger, a fresh cable run, a replacement condenser, an irrigation controller. Made good by the person holding the drill, exactly as it was in 1972.

**The mulch went back.** A bed cut down in October to restore clearance is topped up in April by whoever does the garden, and nobody connects the two.

**Something new was built against a building.** A terrace extension, a deck, a shed in a new spot, a raised bed, a pad for equipment.

**The canopy grew back.** Branches pruned off a roof plane are touching it again in three or four seasons.

The household sees all four months before any contractor would, which is why the schedule belongs to them. On a hamlet where fewer than four per cent of occupied homes are rented, that person exists and has the authority to act.
