## Who is allowed to close the hole?

In this hamlet that question comes before every technical one, and getting it wrong wastes a survey.

On a detached house the answer is trivial: the owner. On the 22.8 per cent of Melville's roughly 7,489 units that are attached, and the 960 more in buildings of five units or more, it is not. Under most declarations the individual demise stops at the interior finishes. The foundation, the exterior wall assembly, the roof, the soffit and fascia, the party-wall separation, the common service trench and the ground around the building are common elements, with items like a patio, a stoop, an assigned parking bay or a private terrace held as limited common elements attached to a unit but still governed by the association.

Every productive opening on this stock is therefore on somebody else's side of that line. The soffit return at a unit break. The joist pocket in a party wall. The riser penetration on the floor below. The trench where the services cross the frontage. The garage sill. The refuse room door. An owner who commissions exclusion on their own dwelling can lawfully have very little of that touched, and the work that is available to them — an interior seal at a cabinet, a door sweep, a vent terminal — will not stop an animal already travelling behind the row.

So the deliverable that actually moves an attached-housing job forward is not a service visit. It is a written specification the board or the managing agent can price, approve and check: the row described as one structure, the openings located, the material stated against each, the units that need access, the sequence, and a clear split between what is association work and what sits with individual owners. That is a document rather than an appointment, and it is what [exclusion consulting](/pest-control/exclusion-consulting/) exists to produce. Boards and agents come at the same problem from the management side at [co-op and condo pest control](/commercial/coop-condo-pest-control/) and [property management pest control](/commercial/property-management-pest-control/).

The related mercy is that the regulatory question here is genuinely easy. Melville tried to incorporate in the 1950s and again in 2001 and failed both times, so there is no village board, no separate code and no architectural review. The entire hamlet answers to Town of Huntington code and a single building department. Sealing, screening, re-terminating and fitting hardware are maintenance. Replacing decayed framing, cutting a new vent, rebuilding a stoop or altering grade with a wall becomes construction and is filed — with one department, at one desk, for every address in Melville.

## What is the real perimeter of a townhouse block?

The whole block, plus the parking court, plus whatever the association mows.

A row of eight dwellings is one building with one envelope, and treating it as eight envelopes is the commonest scoping error we see here. Read as a single structure, the work divides cleanly.

**The horizontal roof-edge run.** One soffit and fascia line crosses every unit. At each unit break the roof plane usually steps, and each step creates a return, a mitre and a termination that was cut on the ladder. Those junctions are identical, repeated and invisible from the ground unless you stand back in the court and look along them.

**The party-wall line.** Where the floor structure lands in or beside the separating wall, there are joist pockets. Where services cross it there are sleeves. The separation was built and inspected against fire performance, which says nothing about a quarter-inch annulus around a cable tray, and site work leaves those behind finished surfaces.

**The garage bank.** Attached garages on this stock are frequently a continuous range with a shared rear wall and a common slab. A gap at one door corner opens onto a space that touches several dwellings.

**The base of the building.** On slab construction that is the joint at the slab edge, the veneer weep course and the sleeve for every service. On the crawled buildings it is the access hatch, the vent line and the sill.

**The common trench and the meter bank.** Dug once for the whole row, entered at multiple points, and re-entered every time a utility upgrades anything.

**The ground plane the association controls.** The bed against the wall, the screening planting along the boundary, the bin standing, the bicycle store, the mulch depth, and the canopy that has grown into the roof edge. Cornell notes that many animals reach roofs by natural bridges from branches contacting a building, and on managed grounds the pruning decision belongs to a contract rather than to a resident.

Scoped that way, a block becomes finite. Scoped as eight houses, it becomes a rolling complaint pattern that moves one address at a time and never resolves, which is what most associations here have already experienced.

## What does newer construction concentrate the work on?

Fewer openings, larger, and almost all of them serving a function.

About 2,054 Melville units were built between 2000 and 2009, with 556 more from 2010 to 2019. Those buildings are tighter than anything else in the hamlet, air-sealed to a standard the post-war stock never approached, and the failure list contracts accordingly.

**Masonry veneer weep courses.** They exist to drain the cavity behind the brick and they must keep doing it. The correct answer is a fitted stainless or copper closure that passes water and blocks an animal. A filled weep is a wet cavity, and a wet cavity is a decay problem that costs more than the mice did.

**The attached garage.** Bottom door corners where the seal lifts from the jamb, the service door threshold, the joint at the slab edge, and every penetration through the wall between the garage and the conditioned space. On a stacked or over-garage arrangement the top plate of that wall is very often open into the floor structure above.

**Appliance terminals.** Dryer, range hood and bathroom outlets with a moulded plastic flap that is the entire barrier. A snapped flap is a warm duct into a floor cavity, and mesh across a dryer outlet is not the answer — it lints up within a season and becomes a fire consideration as well as a ventilation one. It takes a proper terminal with a working damper.

**Sleeves for services.** Refrigerant line sets, condensate, generator feeds, low-voltage runs. Nearly all of them are cored and sleeved; very few are packed and sealed.

**Post-occupancy holes.** Everything drilled since handover. This is the biggest single category on newer housing and the least documented, because each hole belongs to a different trade on a different date and none of them was thinking about the envelope.

The older Melville layer — the 1950s and 1960s houses and the 279 units standing since before 1940 — presents the familiar post-war list instead: an unsealed band joist above the foundation, field-cut penetrations, a shallow soffit past the end of its fixings, a jamb corner at the garage and beds pushed up against the siding. Both lists are workable. Running one on the other is how a building gets reported clear.

## How is a corridor building closed?

At its moving parts, its pit and its roof — none of which appear on a residential checklist.

A campus or warehouse building on Route 110 is not a large house. The openings that matter are the ones that open on purpose, several hundred times a day.

**Overhead and dock doors.** The bottom seal is a wear item on a surface that has been through decades of freeze-thaw. Where the apron has settled or the slab edge has lifted, the seal stops meeting the concrete at the corners while still looking sound across the middle. Side seals and the head seal fail the same way, and a dock door left open through a shift is an open building.

**The dock pit and the leveller.** A pit is a below-grade box with a drain in it, hydraulic or mechanical equipment inside, and a lip that never quite closes. It is the most reliable rodent harbourage on a commercial site and it is almost never on a pest report, because it is under the leveller and nobody lifts it.

**The refuse enclosure.** Base detail, gate gaps, the slab joint, the drain and the ground around it. An enclosure with a two-inch gap under its gates is a feeding station with a roof.

**Personnel doors.** Every elevation has them, staff use the ones nearest their cars regardless of the drawing, and their sweeps are the single cheapest item on the whole scope.

**The roof.** Equipment kerbs, duct and refrigerant penetrations, roof drain sumps, the coping joint along the parapet and the hatch itself. On a flat roof the only regular visitor is a mechanical contractor, so conditions persist for years without being recorded.

**The interior drainage.** Floor drains that have dried out, an abandoned line capped with something temporary, a broken lateral under a slab. Rats commonly work below grade through deteriorated traps and fractured drain lines, which is invisible from outside and untouched by anything applied to the perimeter.

Where food handling, catering or an audited standard is involved, that work runs through the [commercial programme](/commercial/) with the documentation built accordingly, and [food safety and SQF](/commercial/food-safety-sqf/) sets out how the record is constructed.

## What does a lease event do to a building that was already closed?

It reopens it, and in this hamlet lease events are the dominant rhythm rather than an occasional interruption.

The corridor has cycled tenants continuously for half a century. Headquarters have arrived and departed, floor plates have been carved up and recombined, and buildings have stood part-let between occupancies. Every one of those transitions is a construction event on an existing envelope.

A fit-out puts new services through walls and slabs: data, power, gas for a pantry, refrigerant for supplementary cooling, drainage for a new washroom core. It cuts new door openings and blocks old ones. It brings a contractor waste stream that lives in a skip by the dock for months, and it props doors open for deliveries in a way an operating building does not. Every hole is made good by the trade that drilled it, to the standard that trade cares about, which is a fire and acoustic standard rather than an envelope one. At the same time the demolition phase evicts anything living in the ceiling voids and the risers, and it moves within the building rather than out of it.

The practical rule that follows is simple and almost nobody applies it: closure work on a commercial building is re-checked at every lease event, not annually. The moment a fit-out completes is the cheapest moment in the building's life to close it, because the ceilings are still down, the risers are still open, the roof is being visited weekly and there is a contractor on site who can carry out the builder's-work items. Six months later the same work needs night access, ceiling tiles lifted room by room and a tenant's permission for every one.

The residential version of the same effect is a renovation. A kitchen refit, a basement finish, a new HVAC system or a solar installation each put fresh penetrations through a closed envelope, and on attached housing they do it in a wall shared with somebody who was not consulted.

## What material goes into each opening?

Rigid, mechanically fixed, and matched to the substrate — and there is one product that never appears.

Poured concrete and masonry take mortar or hydraulic cement at cracks and around sleeves. Timber takes a rigid closure fixed through into sound material, with custom-fabricated 26-gauge galvanised sheet metal wherever a chewable edge is exposed, because a house mouse works at an edge and an edge it cannot grip is worth more than any bead across a face. Annular gaps around services take copper or stainless mesh packed properly, with Xcluder fill fabric where the gap is irregular and the surface will not take a fixing. Thresholds and door bottoms take Xcluder sweeps sized to the actual gap rather than to the door. Anything that has to keep passing air takes hardware cloth or a purpose-made screen. Where a barrier has to sit below grade against burrowing, Xcluder GEO does that job. Fixings are mechanical throughout, because an adhesive bond is a guess about a surface nobody prepared.

The product that does not appear is expanding foam, and the position is worth stating plainly because the rest of the trade uses it constantly. We do not. Not as the closure, not as backing behind a better material, not run over the top of a finished detail. Rodents chew straight through cured foam. Its worse failure is presentational: a foamed opening reads as completed work, so the next person to walk the building does not check it, and a scope built largely from it will look finished and behave as though nothing was done. Keeping the product off the job removes the argument about how much of it is defensible.

On attached housing there is an additional reason for rigidity. A closure inside a shared assembly may not be inspected again for a decade, because reaching it needs access to somebody else's dwelling. Whatever goes in has to be right the first time.

## What must not be closed?

Anything moving air or discharging a fluid, and this is the most expensive mistake available in the work.

Weep courses in veneer. Crawl and foundation vents where the building has them. Gable, soffit and ridge ventilation. Dryer, range and bathroom terminations. Combustion air openings, which are a life-safety item belonging to the heating trade. Condensate and pump discharges, which should be re-routed away from the wall rather than blocked. Dock pit and trench drains. Roof drain sumps and scuppers.

Each of those gets a screen or terminal in a non-corroding material, fitted so a later inspection can confirm it is intact. The rule is easy to state and constantly broken: closure work makes a building tighter to animals and never tighter to air where the air is doing a job. Block attic ventilation on a townhouse row and the sheathing gets wet in winter, which produces exactly the damp timber that carpenter ants excavate — a bigger bill than the animals ever were.

## What does the record have to prove, and to whom?

That each opening was found, closed with a stated material, and photographed — and on shared property, that the reader can tell whose item was whose.

On a detached house the record is for the owner. It lists each located opening, what was found there, what went into it and an image, names anything that could not be reached and why, and sets out the ground and drainage items with the responsible trade against each. A report on this stock with no unreachable items has usually not accounted for the finished basement or the roof plane behind a dormer.

On an attached building the record is a governance document as much as a technical one, and it has a second job: it has to survive a change of board and a change of managing agent. That means unit numbers rather than descriptions, a plan of the row with the openings marked, an explicit column for whether each item is association work or owner work, the units where access was granted and the units where it was not, and the sequence in which the block was worked. An association that holds that document can pick the work back up in three years with different people in the room. An association that holds a stack of service tickets cannot.

On a corridor building the record has to satisfy people who never met the technician: a facilities manager, an incoming tenant's consultant, an insurer, and on food-handling sites an auditor. A site plan with numbered structural findings, conditions observed, corrective actions requested of the building, and a trend readable across quarters is what answers those questions. Graduate built that documentation habit for food-safety work in the mid-1990s, and it predates the audited standards that later required it.

There is one thing none of these records should contain, and it is worth saying because the trade routinely puts it in. No promise about the future. What a closure record properly asserts is what was found, what was done, what was left open and when it should be looked at again. Everything beyond that is a claim about a building nobody has seen yet.

## In what order does the work run, and when does it get undone?

Ground, base, garage, seams, roof edge — and then it gets undone by whoever holds the next drill.

The sequence is the same on a detached house and a townhouse block, with the difference that on a block each step needs access across several dwellings and is worth doing in one mobilisation. Clear the ground plane first: cut the planting off the wall, get stored material onto a hard base away from the building, reduce the bed height, extend the leader discharges, and prune the canopy back from the roof edge. Then the base of the building and its penetrations, which becomes a survey rather than a search once the wall is visible. Then the garage bank. Then the seams — every alteration, replacement window, converted opening and addition, worked in the order the alterations happened. Then the roof edge, in one ladder visit, with the vent screening, terminals and flashings done at the same time.

Then re-check in the cold, because a December morning on a closed building tells you whether the closure was real.

What undoes it here is specific and it is worth handing to the owner or the manager as a list. A grounds contract that re-mulches the beds up the wall in April. A new pad, store or terrace built across the closed perimeter. A utility trench across the frontage. A tenant fit-out that puts a dozen new penetrations through a wall in a fortnight. And, at the Maxess Road end of the hamlet, the demolition and excavation the overlay adopted on 10 December 2024 will bring, which evicts established harbourage and sends it toward the nearest occupied building. Close before the machines arrive.
