## Why does a shingled wall stop working under closed canopy?

Because a shingled wall was never waterproof. It was designed to get wet and then dry, and out here only the first half still happens reliably.

That is worth taking slowly, because it is the whole argument.

A wood shingle sidewall sheds most of what lands on it at the surface. What gets past — driven rain at a butt joint, water wicking up a cupped shingle, moisture pushed into end grain — is not a failure of the system. The system assumes it and disposes of it by evaporation: air moves at the face and in the small gaps between courses, sun warms the wall, and the assembly returns to a safe moisture content between events. The same logic runs through the roof. It is a good way to build and it has been the vernacular here for three centuries: the Matinecock Friends Meeting House went up in 1725 on shingles under a steep gable and it is still standing.

Now take away the drying. Locust Valley's residential ground sits under old, closed canopy on estate-scale parcels — Bailey Arboretum alone, on the Lattingtown edge, carries roughly two hundred registered tree species across forty-two acres, and the private grounds around it are planted to a similar standard. A north or east elevation under fifty feet of oak and beech in a humid August is not receiving sun and is barely receiving moving air. A rainstorm that an open lot sheds in an afternoon takes days to leave a wall here, and if the next front arrives first, the wall never gets back down.

That is not a breach and it does not look like a defect. It is a correctly built assembly running permanently at the wrong moisture content, and it is exactly the condition a carpenter ant requires. The insect does not eat wood — it cuts galleries for nest space and carries the spoil out — and it takes the timber decay has already opened up, because chewing through sound wood is a poor use of a colony's effort. The Northeastern IPM Center puts the requirement plainly: a colony gets far more nest space for the same effort out of rotted timber, and the water still in it is what the brood needs. Penn State Extension gives the threshold from the other side: below about fifteen per cent moisture content, sound timber is left alone.

So the ants here are not a hygiene event and they are not bad luck. They are a reading of how long a wall is staying wet, on a stock where 42.6 per cent of the roughly 1,254 housing units were standing before 1940, at a median construction year of 1945, and where 92.6 per cent are detached houses on their own ground.

The species page is [carpenter ant](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) and the treatment logic is [ant control](/pest-control/ant-control/). What is particular here is the ground and the number of buildings standing on it.

## Which part of the building actually goes first?

The bottom of the wall and the edge of the roof, and on this construction they fail for opposite reasons.

**The sill line, behind the shingles.** A hand-cut sill sits on rubble or early block that was never sealed against water in the first place and has spent a hundred winters freezing and thawing at every joint. Masonry wicks, so the sill runs at an elevated moisture content by default. Then a hundred years of gardening raises the bed, the shingle course terminates somewhere near the new soil level, and the whole junction disappears from view behind a mature planting. It is not a series of gaps on a wall like this — it is often a continuous line. Getting a hand on it means moving soil and cutting back, which is why it so rarely gets checked and so often turns out to be the answer.

**The roof edge.** A steep roof with a deep overhang buys an enormous amount of edge for the area it covers. Dormers cut valleys into the main planes, every gable finishes in a boxed return, the eaves run well past the wall, and every one of those meetings is a flashing joint. The deep overhang protects the field of the wall very effectively, which is part of why these houses look sound. What it does not protect is the edge itself, and the edge is where the water that does get in ends up. A blocked valley, a lifted flashing at a dormer cheek or a gutter that has spilled over the same corner every autumn since the trees closed in wets framing at the top of a wall, inside a boxed return, in a void with no ventilation at all.

Between the two, the reliable finds on this stock are: the sill and the band of framing immediately above it; the bottom courses of shingle on a north or east elevation; a porch beam and the posts that carry it; window sills, and the framing hidden under an exterior casing that has been letting water past; the boxed soffit return at the base of a gable; and the framing behind a valley that has been holding leaf litter every autumn since the trees matured.

There is one more that belongs to old houses specifically. A building that began as a farmhouse, spent forty years as staff accommodation and is now a family house carries a seam from every one of those changes, and none of them lines up with the frame underneath. An enclosed porch, a kitchen wing, a dormer punched through a roof plane, a bathroom cut into what was a landing — each of them lands on the older structure at a junction the original carpenter never planned for, and whoever did the work generally left a cavity behind rather than dismantle sound fabric to close it. Those junctions are where nests turn up, and they are found by working out the sequence of alterations rather than by tapping at random.

## Why does the survey start in the stable rather than the house?

Because on an estate-scale holding the parent colony is usually in another building, not in a tree.

The split is the thing to hold onto. A colony runs a parent nest holding the queen and the eggs, which cannot survive anywhere that dries out, and satellite nests holding older brood and workers, which manage in far drier conditions. The satellite is what shows up in a window sill, a porch post, a length of insulation or a soffit return. The parent is somewhere wetter, and traffic runs between them constantly. Kill the satellite and the parent repopulates it.

On a suburban lot the parent nest is normally a stump or a woodpile. Here the list is longer and it is mostly architectural. A property of this kind carries a detached garage, and frequently a stable, a gate lodge, a potting shed, a greenhouse, a pool house, a garden store, sometimes a boathouse. None of them is heated. Most get opened twice a year. A number went up alongside the main house, shingled the same way, and have been maintained at a fraction of the rate ever since. Their sills sit closer to grade than the house sill does, their gutters have generally not been cleared in years, and their floor framing is frequently the wettest timber on the whole holding.

That makes them better parent-colony sites than anything in the garden, and it makes a survey that covers the residence and skips them close to useless. Foraging runs to several hundred feet, which on a parcel of this size comfortably links a stable at the edge of a paddock to a kitchen in the house.

The rest of the outdoor inventory still matters and it is unusually rich: edging and retaining work in timber on ground that has been gardened without a break for a hundred years, buried stumps, old fence posts, derelict garden steps, split logs stacked against a wall, and the deadwood cycle of very old specimen trees. But the buildings come first, and they come first in October as well — an established population found in a stable in autumn explains what the house is going to report in November.

The other differentiator on these holdings is occupancy. Where a property is used seasonally or intermittently, nobody is in the room at eleven at night to hear a colony working inside a wall, spoil accumulates in an unused wing for a season before anyone notices it, and a soft sill goes another two winters before somebody leans on it. Problems found here are older than problems found on a house that is lived in every day, and the scope reflects that.

## What does the water on the Beaver Lake side do?

Holds the ground up, which is a different mechanism from canopy and it stacks on top of it.

Oak Neck Creek carries roughly a hundred and twenty acres of relatively undisturbed salt marsh and tidal creek at the eastern edge of the hamlet, draining out through Mill Neck Creek. Beaver Lake, a freshwater impoundment of about sixty acres and roughly three feet deep, empties into the same creek over a weir along Cleft Road.

A shallow sixty-acre standing water body does two things to buildings near it. It keeps soil moisture high on the ground around it year-round rather than seasonally, so masonry near the valley bottom stays damp and the timber bearing on it never gets a dry spell long enough for decay to stop. And it produces a great deal of humid air in exactly the season when the canopy is closed, which slows drying on those properties further than shade alone would.

Combine the two and the buildings on that side of the hamlet are the ones where a sill still reads damp to a meter in the third week of August. That is the test worth applying, because a member that is still wet halfway through a dry month is being fed rather than remembering, and it is the difference between a repair that holds and one that does not.

Away from the water the mechanism reverts to canopy and roof drainage, which is more tractable. A big roof puts a great deal of water through very few downpipes, and on a property of this age those pipes usually run into buried leader lines nobody has rodded in living memory. A blocked leader gives no sign at all. It simply keeps one corner of the building wet, year after year, and that corner is where the spoil eventually turns up.

## Which other ants show up out here?

Fewer than in a dense village, and each means something different.

**Odorous house ants.** Small, dark, moving in a defined trail; crush one and it smells faintly of something gone off. Their nests are shallow and do not survive being flooded, so a saturated bed or a soaked mulch layer moves the entire colony — and on grounds with heavy planting and irrigation that happens more often than the rainfall alone would suggest. The [odorous house ant](/pest-library/odorous-house-ant/) profile has the identification. The operational rule is not to spray a trail of them: a multi-queen colony under pressure buds and splits into new sites, several of them further into the building than the original.

**Pavement ants.** Small, dark, associated with hard surfaces, nesting in the bedding under a terrace, a walk, a stable yard apron or a set of steps and entering through the joints, with a small crater of excavated grit at the joint as the signature. See the [pavement ant](/pest-library/pavement-ant/) profile. Indoors they are a foraging nuisance and they say nothing whatever about your framing.

**Acrobat ants** deserve a mention on this stock specifically, because they frequently move into voids that decay or a previous carpenter ant colony has already opened. That makes them carriers of the same message: something in that assembly is wet, or was.

**Carpenter bees** are not ants and get blamed for far more than they do, but they belong on the list because on shingled and unfinished trim they leave a clean round entry hole that owners here find and misread. The [carpenter bee](/pest-library/carpenter-bee/) profile settles it.

Getting the identification right decides the method rather than the label. A carpenter ant finding is a report on the building's water. A trailing-ant finding is bait, patience and a closed entry point, with a firm rule against anything repellent. Treat the two identically and the property acquires a permanent service visit that alters nothing.

## What if it is a termite?

Then it is not our job, and the useful thing this page can do is help you find that out quickly.

Every spring this identification gets missed in both directions, because both insects fly in the same weeks, at the same windows, out of buildings carrying the same underlying fault. Three features settle it. On the ant the middle is nipped in to a thread, each antenna bends once near its base, and the leading wing outruns the one behind it. On the termite there is no nip anywhere, the antenna is a straight run of small beads, and all four wings match and drop off readily. Wings on their own answer it: four that match belong to the termite, a mismatched set to the ant.

The workings settle it too, and more permanently. The gallery walls tell you as well: University of New Hampshire Extension contrasts the carpenter ant's clean, smooth-sided workings with termite galleries packed out with a mud-like deposit. Carpenter ants throw their spoil out; the termite brings earth in with it, both as shelter tubes across masonry and as packing inside the workings. Pale shredded wood underneath means the ant. Mud inside the workings means the termite. The full comparison sits on the [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) page.

Old estate landscaping does supply the conditions the other insect wants — buried stumps, landscape timbers, terraces and plantings built up against rubble and early masonry, closing the clearance between soil and wood that Cornell Cooperative Extension asks for. That is worth correcting on its own merits, because it is also the correction that dries the sill. But it is a description of the ground rather than an offer of work.

## Who signs off the repair, and why is nobody sure?

The Town of Oyster Bay, usually — but the postal name is wider than the hamlet, and there is no local record of what has been done to your building.

The hamlet is unincorporated and governs nothing itself. Permits, zoning and enforcement are the Town of Oyster Bay's, and no local body reviews what anyone does to an exterior. Several places next door are incorporated villages that write their own code and send their own inspector, and because this name became the address of reference for the surrounding estate country, two properties on the same postal line can answer to two different authorities.

That is a treatment-irrelevant, repair-critical distinction. Nothing about baiting a trail or treating a located gallery needs a permit. Replacing a section of sill, rebuilding a porch, altering grade with a retaining wall or opening and reclosing a roof to correct a wet valley may. Establishing which desk the application goes to is ten minutes and it saves a stalled job. For an estate office or a business, [choosing a pest control contractor](/compliance/choosing-a-pest-control-contractor/) sets out what a proposal ought to contain, and [New York's neighbor notification law](/compliance/ny-neighbor-notification-law/) governs any outdoor application made commercially.

The second consequence is subtler and it affects the diagnosis rather than the paperwork. Because nothing local reviews an exterior alteration, there is usually no file anywhere describing the additions, siding repairs, re-roofing and window replacements a pre-1940 house has absorbed. Nobody can tell you when the porch was rebuilt or which decade the rear wing was clad. So the age of a decay pocket has to be read out of the wood itself — the extent of the soft zone under an awl, the color and compaction of the spoil, whether the gallery walls are freshly cut or weathered — rather than inferred from a file.

## In what order does the work run across a whole holding?

Outbuildings, then contact, then water, then wood, then treatment — and the treatment is the smallest item on the list.

**The outbuildings first**, for the reasons above. The garage and the stable, then the gate lodge, the potting shed, the greenhouse and whatever is standing at the back of the kitchen garden. Sills, floor framing, and where each roof discharges.

**Then the contact between canopy and building.** Not the trees as a whole — the specific branches touching or overhanging a roof, a gutter, a valley or a flat over a bay. A limb resting on a gutter is a trail that renders every ground-level measure irrelevant, and it also holds one elevation in permanent shade. Selective pruning is cheap and it changes the wall's drying rate, which is the mechanism at the top of this page.

**Then the water.** Put a hose in each gutter and watch where it comes out. Open and rod the buried leader lines. Extend discharges well clear of the wall. Re-flash the valleys and the dormer cheeks. Take the soil and the planting back off the sill so the bottom of the wall gets air and can be looked at.

**Then replace what has lost section.** A sill needing a splice, a beam that gives under an awl, a boxed return gone to nothing, decayed shingle courses along the bottom of a wall. No treatment restores a member; once section has gone the site stays open, and something will take it.

**Then treat, where the nest is.** Bait on live routes, carried back along the trail and shared out, including to a parent nest in an outbuilding nobody has opened — or a treatment placed into the gallery itself once its position is known. A contact spray run along a visible trail accounts for the workers standing on it and nothing else.

**Then check twice.** Once in the same season, and once the following April, because carpenter ant colonies are slow and quiet in July is not the same as gone.

**The seasons that suit each part.** March and April for the diagnostic sighting, since ants indoors while nothing is moving outside mean a nest inside the heated envelope. May and June to read the walls before the planting closes in. June to August for locating a nest and for the honest moisture test, and for night trail-following, since these insects forage between dusk and midnight. September and October for the carpentry, roofing and drainage, so the work gets tested by the same autumn's rain. November to February for reading the envelope on a cold still morning, when the air leaking out of an old shingled wall marks the same joints that let water in.
