## What does exclusion mean when the whole perimeter is equally exposed?

That the survey cannot take a shortcut, and that the work is longer and more honest as a result.

Most exclusion work is triaged by moisture and topography. On a graded lot, water leaves in a direction, collects at one or two places, and produces a building that is dry on most of its perimeter and reliably damp somewhere specific. That damp place is where the deterioration is, where the openings are widest and where the survey spends its time. It is efficient and it is usually right.

Garden City removes that. The village sits on the Hempstead Plains, a flat glacial outwash plain, with no harbour, no bluff, no tidal creek, no moraine ridge and no natural surface water inside it. The soils are coarse, sandy meltwater deposits that drain quickly and uniformly. Every elevation of a house here has broadly the same ground beneath it, the same drainage behind it and the same exposure.

Four consequences for this work.

**No elevation can be down-weighted.** All four sides get examined to the same standard, at grade and at height.

**The grade line is rarely generous anywhere.** On flat ground, the clearance between soil and the bottom of siding tends to be tight all the way round rather than good on three sides and bad on one. That is the measurement that decides a great deal, and here it is measured at every elevation, at the patio, the bed, the walk and the garage apron, because it is rarely consistent even so.

**Water needs a gradient to leave and there isn't one.** A leader discharging two feet from a wall has nowhere to send its water except into the soil beside the foundation. On sloping ground the same discharge would be carried away.

**Everything wet is artificial, therefore fixable.** With no natural water source, every damp condition on a Garden City property was made by a leader, an irrigation zone, a patio that pitches the wrong way, a bed built up against siding, a failed flashing or a slow plumbing leak. That is a genuinely better starting point than a house with a high water table, because each item has an owner and a repair.

The compensating advantage is the tenure. Around 83 per cent of units here are detached houses and about 94 per cent of occupied units are owner-occupied. There are no party walls, no shared block interiors and no neighbour's contractor in the way. One person owns the whole envelope and the whole decision, which is why a Garden City exclusion job can reach a defined end and an attached city building cannot.

## What is the finite list on a 1950s house?

Short, repeatable, and genuinely completable — which is the most useful sentence on this page.

The post-war expansion is the larger half of the village by volume and it arrived with a standard kit: attached garage, shallow foundation or slab, simple roof geometry, and utility penetrations sleeved through the wall for water, gas, electrical service, air-conditioning line sets and irrigation. Nothing at the time required those sleeves to be sealed, so they were not, and seventy years later most of them still are not.

The list, in order of what it achieves:

**The sleeved penetrations.** Water service, gas, electrical, condensate lines, air-conditioning line sets, irrigation feeds, low-voltage and cable drops. On a house of this type these are the most productive single item, and they are closed by packing the annulus properly around the pipe in mortar or hydraulic cement at masonry, or in metal and rigid material at frame, rather than by running a sealant bead over the front of the hole.

**The garage overhead door seal.** As the rubber ages and lifts, a triangular gap opens where the seal meets the jamb at each bottom corner. It is present on a very large share of houses with an overhead door and it is a dependable entrance. A seal that actually meets the jamb at the corners is the fix.

**The wall between garage and living space.** Frequently open at the top plate, which turns an animal in the garage into an animal in the house's wall cavity. This is the detail that converts a garage problem into a house problem and it is invisible from either side.

**Door thresholds,** particularly at side and rear doors that have settled or whose sweeps have worn.

**Dryer vents** with failed or missing flappers, replaced with a proper terminal rather than blocked.

**Hose bibs** with an unsealed annulus.

**Gaps behind corner boards and where siding terminates,** at the bottom course and at any change of material.

**Foundation and crawlspace vents** where screening has corroded through — rescreened, never closed.

**The roofline:** soffit returns, gable louvres, and the junction where a roof plane meets a wall on a split-level or an addition.

**Utility chases and any plumbing penetration through a slab** where a cabinet base or access panel allows reach.

That is the whole of it on most houses of this type. The substrates are cooperative, the details are accessible, and the work stays done. This is the version of the job we would rather sell, and it is why exclusion sits at the centre of what we do rather than at the end of a price list.

## Why is the 1870s villa never actually finished?

Because it has concealed volume that cannot be fully inventoried, and an inventory you cannot complete is not a list you can close.

The A. T. Stewart Era Buildings — a National Register thematic group of fifty structures, with 44 residences built between 1871 and 1878 to designs by John Kellum and Henry G. Harrison — are Italianate frame houses ranging from modest cottages to three-storey villas. The best-known carry mansard roofs, cupolas and twelve-foot ceilings. Garden City Estates, from 1907, adds a further pre-war layer.

What that stock contains that a ranch does not:

**Mansard cavities.** A mansard is a wall pretending to be a roof: steep, clad in slate or shingle over timber framing, with an unventilated or barely ventilated void behind it and no access. Dormers add cheeks, small roofs and flashings. The curb where the mansard meets the flatter upper roof is another junction.

**Cupola framing.** A structure at the highest point of the building with a base flashing, glazing or louvres in its sides, a roof of its own and often a hatch. Every one of those is an opening that has had to be maintained for a century and a half.

**Deep cornices and heavy applied mouldings,** which have a joint at the top where water gets behind and stays, and hollow framing behind them.

**Box gutters and gutters concealed behind cornices.**

**Continuous wall cavities.** Twelve-foot ceilings and frame construction of this era frequently give a void running from sill to top plate with no blocking. That is a shaft, and rodents use shafts. An entry at grade produces noise at a third-floor ceiling with nothing audible in between.

**Porch and veranda structures** with their own footings, steps and floor framing, frequently rebuilt.

**Joist pockets** where floor framing is let into masonry.

**A long list of abandoned services.** A redundant water service, a disconnected oil fill line, an old sleeve where a feed came in. On a house of this age these are a substantial share of the whole scope and each is permanently closable once found.

None of that can be reduced to a checklist that a survey completes on a single afternoon, because a good deal of it is behind cladding that would have to come off. So the honest position on this stock is different: it is managed and re-inspected rather than declared closed. Openings that can be reached get closed. Concealed volumes get their coverings maintained by a roofer, because a sound covering is the only exclusion a mansard cavity gets. And the property goes on a re-inspection footing rather than a completion footing.

Anyone told that a house of this type has been comprehensively sealed in one visit should ask which mansard cavities were opened.

## What does the absence of salt air change about materials?

It widens the choice, which is a real practical difference from the North Shore markets a few miles north.

On a waterfront or near-waterfront property, salt-laden air corrodes galvanised coatings and accelerates any dissimilar-metal junction, so exclusion specifications there push toward stainless and copper for anything expected to last. Inland, on a flat plain with no tidal shoreline, the exposure is ordinary. Galvanised hardware cloth is a reasonable long-term choice in most positions here, which changes cost and availability on a large job.

That aside, the material discipline is the same everywhere and it is worth being explicit about it, because material choice is where most of this work fails.

**Mesh and hardware cloth** wherever ventilation or drainage has to continue. Cut generously, set back inside the opening where it can be concealed, and fixed so an animal cannot work an edge.

**Mortar or hydraulic cement** at masonry, packed properly around a sleeve rather than smeared over the front of a hole. On the older stock, an appropriate mortar rather than a hard modern mix, because a mortar harder than the surrounding material damages the material.

**Sheet metal** at any chewable edge: timber corners, door bottoms, the base of a garage jamb, the edge of a soffit.

**Rigid closure cut to fit** at soffits, returns and cornice ends, rather than material stuffed into a gap.

**Proper terminations** for dryer vents, extract fans and combustion appliance flues, which are appliances rather than holes and should be treated as such.

What fails:

**Cured expanding foam, in any position on any detail.** Rodents chew straight through it, and it fails a second time by looking like completed work, so a live opening gets recorded as closed. We do not carry the product, which means the question of how much of it is acceptable never has to be argued on one of our jobs.

**Plain steel wool,** which corrodes and stains whatever is beneath it.

**Silicone and acrylic caulk doing the structural job.** Acceptable dressed over a real barrier; worthless standing alone.

**Hard cementitious patching over a moving joint,** which cracks in the first season and then holds water in the crack.

## Which openings must stay open?

More than people expect, and closing them is the most common way this work does harm.

The distinction between sealing and screening is the one most often got wrong in this trade, and it matters here because the post-war stock has a lot of designed openings.

**Foundation and crawlspace vents** exist to move air through a space that would otherwise hold moisture. They get rescreened in mesh, not blocked.

**Soffit vents** are part of the roof's ventilation path. Blocking them causes condensation in a roof space and, in winter, ice damming at the eaves.

**Weeps** in masonry veneer let water that has got behind the veneer drain out. Sealing them traps water in the cavity.

**Dryer terminations** must exhaust. A blocked one is a fire and moisture problem.

**Combustion appliance flues** must draw. Nothing about this work goes anywhere near them except to make sure a termination is properly fitted and screened where the manufacturer allows it.

**Roof drainage** — scuppers, outlets and downspouts — must run clear. On the old stock, box gutters and concealed gutters likewise.

**Sump and drainage discharge points** where they exist.

The rule is simple: if the opening has a job, it keeps its job and gets a screen. If it has no job, it gets closed permanently. Working out which is which is a survey task, and it is one of the places where somebody who thinks in buildings differs from somebody who thinks in products.

## How does a long, low apartment building differ from a tall one?

The pathways run sideways, so the sealing runs sideways too.

A village restriction prevents any building from exceeding the Cathedral of the Incarnation, completed in 1885. Around six per cent of the village's units sit in buildings of twenty to forty-nine units and another five and a half per cent in buildings of fifty or more — a real multifamily population, extended horizontally rather than stacked.

In a tall building, exclusion concentrates on vertical elements: riser chases, stacked plumbing penetrations, compactor and refuse chutes, and the cellar. A problem on one floor propagates above and below.

In a long, low building, the vertical elements are short and the horizontal ones are long. What matters is:

**Horizontal service runs above ceilings,** passing through party walls at high level, frequently unsealed where they penetrate.

**Continuous soffits** running the length of an elevation, which connect units at the roofline.

**Shared roof or attic spaces** over several units, which may or may not have been compartmentalised.

**Party wall voids at joist pockets,** which run a long way.

**Long ground-floor perimeters,** with a proportionally larger number of thresholds, penetrations, service entries and refuse enclosures than a tall building of the same unit count.

A contractor working from a generic multifamily template will seal the vertical chases, which does relatively little here, and leave the lateral runs, which is where the traffic is. The scope has to name the axis.

## Does a National Register listing restrict what you can do to your own house?

Generally not in the way people assume, and this is worth clearing up before anybody scopes exterior work.

The A. T. Stewart Era Buildings were listed on the National Register in 1978. National Register listing is a federal recognition of historic significance. It is an honorific and a planning tool, and it does not by itself impose on a private owner the kind of design review that a New York City historic district imposes through the Landmarks Preservation Commission. That is a genuine and useful difference from the way this subject works across the city line, where a scope touching a visible facade can attract a review that changes the timetable of a job.

What that does not mean is that there are no local requirements. Any permitting, zoning or architectural requirement here comes from the village, and it is the village's office that should be asked before exterior work is designed — particularly for anything structural, anything that alters an elevation, or anything involving a porch, a roof or a chimney. We are not in a position to advise on that and we do not try to; what we do is design the scope so the question can be answered before anybody is committed.

There is a practical point underneath the legal one. On a house of this quality, the material discipline that a preservation-minded owner would want is the same discipline that makes exclusion durable: appropriate mortar rather than hard modern mixes, metalwork remade rather than sealed over, mesh set back and concealed rather than fixed across the face of an opening, and decayed timber replaced rather than treated in place. Doing the work well and doing it sympathetically are the same instruction here, which is not always the case in this trade.

## Where does the drainage work sit in the sequence?

After the sealing, before the verification, and on flat ground it is frequently the largest single item.

The full order:

**Survey the building and the ground together.** The perimeter at grade on all four elevations. Every penetration. The roofline. On the older stock, the concealed volumes and the height work. Then the water: where every leader discharges, where the grade falls, where beds have been built up, where the ground stays dark after rain, and what each irrigation zone actually hits when it runs.

**Reduce any existing interior population before sealing.** Closing a building that still holds an active population takes away the animals' way out and concentrates them in wall voids. On a slab house with no basement that is a particularly unpleasant version of the problem, because there is nowhere to reach them from. The reduction side is [rodent control in Garden City](/pest-control/garden-city/rodent-control/).

**Close the openings, matched to substrate,** as set out above, and record every one.

**Then fix the water.** On flat ground this usually means extending leaders — six or eight feet away from the wall, to somewhere with even a slight fall. It means turning irrigation heads off the building and running the controller responsively. It means lowering beds that have risen above the top of the foundation and cutting mature planting back so the base of the wall can dry and be seen. It means re-pitching or re-bedding a patio or walk that pitches back toward the house. None of that looks like pest control. It is the item that decides whether any of the rest of it lasts, and it is the largest line on a great many proposals here.

**Then verify,** after weather and after a freeze-thaw cycle has tested the exterior.

The same drainage list is the wood-destroying-insect list, which is why the moisture findings on a survey here usually also explain whatever [ant control in Garden City](/pest-control/garden-city/ant-control/) turned up: on the older half of the village, a bed that has risen above the top of the foundation and a leader that was never extended are between them the reason a colony found timber soft enough to excavate.

## When in the year should this be done here?

Late August to November for the sealing, spring for the ground, winter for the diagnosis.

**Late August through November is the exclusion window and the highest-value time of year.** Cooling nights move [house mice](/pest-library/house-mouse/) toward buildings, and a house closed before that happens does not acquire the winter population it would otherwise carry through to spring. The weather is still good for ladder work, grade work and roofs.

**December to February is diagnostic.** On a cold still morning a building shows where warm air escapes — soffit returns, rim joists, unsealed penetrations, the garage door corners — and those are the same holes the animals use. It is also when noise in a wall is most audible.

**March to May is when the ground is readable.** Before planting fills in, burrows, runs, harbourage and grade problems are visible, and the freeze-thaw record on masonry, porches and slabs is fresh.

**April to June is the right window for the drainage corrections,** because the ground is workable and any change can be tested against the same season's rain rather than waiting a year to find out whether it helped.

**Summer is for the water inventory** — running each irrigation zone and watching, dealing with condensate discharge, rebuilding bin storage, and lifting and re-bedding any slab that ponds.

## How do you verify it a year later?

Against a written list, and by looking again after a winter — because on this stock the most common cause of failure is somebody else's work.

An exclusion scope on a house here should read as a set of individual items, each with a location precise enough to find again, a description of what was found, the material used and a photograph. Anything unreachable is listed alongside the rest, with a note explaining why it could not be got at. Conditions referred elsewhere — a suspected slab leak for a plumber, a failed mansard flashing for a roofer, a settled patio for a mason — appear as referrals rather than being quietly dropped.

The re-check a season later asks four questions.

**Has anything opened?** Freeze-thaw moves masonry, slabs settle on sand bedding, and sealant ages.

**Has anything been undone?** This is the local one. Irrigation trenching, a new patio, a fence line, a drainage retrofit, a landscaping job, a line set replaced for new air conditioning, a plumbing repair under a slab. Every one of those routinely removes sealing work, and nobody connects a landscaping job with a pest treatment. If any exterior contractor has been on the property, the exterior sealing is worth re-checking.

**Has the ground moved back?** Mulch top-ups raise a bed a little every year, and after five years the clearance you established has gone.

**Has anything new grown into the roof?** Limbs reaching a roof edge are a wildlife route, and they were not there when the survey was done.
