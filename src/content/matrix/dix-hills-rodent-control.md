## Why does the work here start in a building nobody heats?

Because on a lot this size the population has somewhere better to be than your kitchen, and it uses it for years.

Some 8,067 housing units sit on 15.9 square miles here, which is a density of about 1,662 people to the square mile. Some 7,647 of those units — 94.8 per cent — are detached houses, and 7,499 of the occupied ones are owner-occupied against 317 rented. That is a hamlet of large parcels with a house in the middle and, on a great many of them, three or four other structures scattered around it.

A detached garage. A pool house. A pool equipment enclosure. A shed on blocks or on a slab that has settled. A workshop. A generator on a pad. A play structure with a boxed base. None of them are heated, most of them are visited seasonally, and several of them hold exactly what a rodent population wants: a slab edge that has parted from its wall plate, a door with a sweep worn through at the corners, an open junction where a roof deck meets the top of a wall, and stored grass seed, bird seed, pet food or fertiliser in bags on the floor.

That is why the order of a survey here is inverted compared with a dense hamlet. On a small lot you walk the house and the house is the story. On an acre you walk the outbuildings first, because a residence that has just reported its first mouse has usually been the last building on the property to be colonised rather than the first.

The consequence for scope is direct. Closing the house alone will stop the reports for a season and leave the reservoir intact, so the following autumn is a repeat. Closing the house and the outbuildings, and clearing what the outbuildings store on their floors, removes the population's base as well as its route. That is a longer list of items and a shorter list of years.

## What does an acre do to the geometry of the problem?

It stretches every line the work runs along, and it hides the productive parts of them.

**The perimeter is long.** A large two-storey house on a full basement presents a continuous horizontal junction where timber lands on concrete, running the whole way round. On this stock that line is measured in the low hundreds of feet, not the tens, and it was rarely sealed when the house was built.

**The roof run is longer still.** These are late-suburban houses: multiple planes, dormers, projections, garage wings. Where a dormer cheek dies into a main plane there is a valley, and at the foot of that valley a small boxed return that is usually hollow behind a single board.

**The ground line is planted.** Foundation beds installed in 1972 have had fifty years of growth and fifty springs of mulch. The base of the wall — the most productive strip on the whole building — is behind a yew that is now eight feet across.

**The distances are real.** The wood line may be a hundred feet from the house, the shed sixty feet the other way, the pool equipment behind a screen wall. Nobody walks all of it in winter, which is when the evidence is easiest to read.

The practical effect is that a survey here takes longer and produces a longer list than the same survey in a compact hamlet, and that a proportion of the list has nothing to do with the residence. It also means nobody can price this from a photograph or a description over the phone. The consultation costs nothing and usually happens by phone. A service fee attaches to the written proposal and plan, because on a parcel this size the document is what is being bought, and it comes off the project cost if you go ahead.

## What has been drilled through these walls since 1970?

More than the builder ever put there, by more trades, on more dates, with nobody owning the result.

The scale of the buried infrastructure is worth stating, because it explains the pattern. The district behind these services runs 169 miles of main from 17 wells, with 1,282 hydrants and better than 2,386 valves on it, reaching roughly 8,400 properties and a population of 41,000. Every one of those services terminates inside a building, and the point where it passes through a foundation is a cored or sleeved opening with an annulus around the pipe.

Then fifty years of additions layer on top. Central air with its refrigerant line set and condensate drain. An irrigation system with its supply, its controller feed and its valve boxes. Pool circulation and heater lines. A standby generator with a gas run and a transfer switch feed. A car charger. A replacement condenser on a new pad. A security or camera cable. A propane line to a kitchen. Every one was made on site, on a separate day, by somebody whose work ended when the equipment ran.

The result is a cluster. On most houses of this era the penetrations are not distributed evenly around the building; they concentrate in one or two corners — the utility corner where the plant is, and the corner nearest the drive where the meters and the later equipment went. Half the entries on a Dix Hills survey are within twenty feet of each other, which is good news, because it means the highest-yield part of the closure work is compact even when the perimeter is not.

Closing them properly means the annulus packed with mesh and closed at the wall face, sheet metal where an edge is chewable, and hydraulic cement at masonry — not a bead of sealant run round a pipe. ## Why is the noise never where the entry is?

Because a big house connects its ends, and a mouse uses the connections rather than the rooms.

The complaint on this stock is almost always the same shape: scratching overhead in a bedroom at the far end of the house, or in a ceiling above a finished basement, or behind a wall in a room with no exterior door. The owner reasonably concludes the animals are getting in near where the noise is. They are almost never getting in near where the noise is.

A house of this era gives an animal a straight run. Platform framing puts a plate at each floor level, which helps, but the plates have been drilled repeatedly — for plumbing, for wiring, for a bathroom that was added, for a run of ductwork. Joist bays run the depth of the house. A plumbing wall is a continuous vertical void from the basement to the roof space. A boxed chase beside a stair or over a stair landing does the same. Soffited runs over kitchen cabinets connect one side of a house to the other above the ceiling line.

So the useful diagnostic here is not the location of the noise but its character. Whether the sound sits above a ceiling or inside a partition. Whether it is after dark, which points to rodents, or in daylight and heavy, which points at [grey squirrels](/pest-library/eastern-gray-squirrel/) and a [wildlife management](/pest-control/wildlife-management/) job with a different seasonal and legal framework around it. Whether it moves along a line or stays in one place.

Then the search runs from the evidence rather than from the sound. Rodents run with one side touching something upright, and the coat leaves an oily smear where they do it often. A defined smear along a joist, a pipe run or the foot of a basement wall is a corridor rather than a coincidence. Walk that smear to each of its ends and one gives the way in while the other gives the nest, which on a house this size beats an hour of searching at random.

## Does the hamlet have a rat problem, and where?

A limited one, and it is geographic rather than general.

The residential interior of Dix Hills is a house mouse hamlet. There is no harbour, no creek, no river and no pond in 15.9 square miles, no public sewer network of the kind that supports a city rat population, and lot sizes that spread everything out. The density that makes a [Norway rat](/pest-library/norway-rat/) problem self-sustaining is not present across most of the place.

Where it does show up, it shows up in three situations.

**The Long Island Expressway corridor.** The road runs almost straight through the middle of the hamlet, and its service roads and interchanges carry the commercial frontage and the hamlet's largest drainage catchment. Service yards, refuse enclosures and drainage structures along that line are a different environment from the streets a quarter of a mile away.

**The Jericho Turnpike frontage.** The hamlet's second commercial edge, with the same arrangement of yards, bins and service doors behind the shopfronts.

**Institutional buildings.** A low-density residential hamlet still carries schools across the Half Hollow Hills district, a college, three fire stations and a library, and every one of them has a commercial-scale kitchen or canteen, a plant room and a refuse arrangement. Those run on a documented programme rather than a residential visit, which is the model described at [commercial pest management](/commercial/) and, for the schools specifically, [schools pest control](/commercial/schools-pest-control/).

For a house on one of those edges we say the same thing we say everywhere. Sealing your own buildings is still the first money worth spending, because it is the only part you control, and it will not thin out whatever is living behind the fence. Where something is suspected to be burrowing under a deck, a pool surround, a shed or a run of hardscape, [canine rodent detection](/pest-control/canine-rodent-detection/) locates it before anybody starts lifting slabs.

## What does the canopy actually do?

It delivers animals to the roof, which is the half of this work that a ground-level survey never sees.

This is wooded ground. The hills are glacial — the Harbor Hill moraine forms the northern spine of the island — and the large-lot subdivisions of the 1960s and 1970s were laid out through existing woodland rather than across cleared farmland. Fifty years on, the mature canopy that made these lots desirable is at roof height and beyond.

A branch resting on a gutter or a roof plane is what Cornell describes as a natural bridge, and it is how most of the roof-level arrivals here get up there. On a long, complicated roof a limb touching any part of it puts an animal on a surface it can then walk. From there the list is the standard one for a 1970s roof. A boxed soffit return under a dormer. A gable louvre whose screen has gone. A ridge vent with a flattened closure strip. The line where a lower plane runs into a wall. An uncapped flue.

Two things follow.

The first is that pruning is a pest control item, and it is the cheapest one on most scopes here. Removing the bridge is better than closing the destination, and unlike closure it does not have to be maintained on a fifty-foot ladder.

The second is that the wood line counts for as much as the trees near the house. Cover collects exactly where cut grass gives way to rough: brush, the log pile put at the boundary because the boundary is furthest from the house, offcuts abandoned after a deck went in, and forty autumns of leaves raked to the same line. Mowing a wider margin and lifting stored material clear of both the ground and the boundary buys more on a wooded lot than any product put on a building.

## What is a large house actually storing, and where?

Food, warmth and cover, in the three rooms nobody thinks of as rooms.

Sanitation matters on this stock, but not in the form people expect. Kitchens in Dix Hills are not the problem; they are cleaned daily and occupied constantly. The problem is the storage that a big house on a big lot makes possible, and it sits in three places.

**The garage.** On a house of this era the attached two-car garage is effectively exterior space with the family's bulk goods in it. A second refrigerator or a chest freezer, running warm at the back where the motor is, which is precisely the position Cornell describes rodents favouring — dark, undisturbed, warm, beside an appliance motor. Bags of dry pet food. Grass seed and fertiliser. Recycling held between collections. Cardboard stacked flat against a wall, which is nest material and cover in one object.

**The basement.** A finished basement is a heated floor of the house with an unfinished mechanical room and storage area attached to it. That unfinished part holds boxes on the floor, spare furnishings, holiday storage and often a bulk pantry. It is warm, dark, undisturbed and directly against the perimeter wall the animals came through.

**The outbuildings.** Bird seed in a shed, feed on a floor, pool chemicals and cushions in a pool house, and a workshop with sawdust and offcuts.

None of that is a hygiene verdict and it should not be presented as one. What it changes is carrying capacity and the competitiveness of the work. A property whose garage floor is clear, whose dry goods are in rigid sealed containers and whose stored material sits on shelving with a visible gap beneath it supports fewer animals and lets the reduction phase finish faster, because a device is competing with less.

The instruction is short and it belongs to the household rather than to a contractor. Clear the floors — garage, basement store, every outbuilding — and put what was on them onto racking. Put seed, feed and dry goods into rigid containers with lids. Break down and remove cardboard rather than stacking it. Pull the chest freezer far enough off the wall that the space behind it can be seen. And leave the evidence alone until somebody has read it — droppings, marks and gnawing are the map, and sweeping them up before a survey removes the only record of where the animals actually go.

## What does the winter tell you on a 1970 house?

Where it leaks, which is the same thing as where they come in.

December to February is the most informative stretch of the year on this stock, for a reason that sounds counter-intuitive. On a cold still morning the points where warm air leaves a building are the points animals use, because they are the same openings. Ten minutes with a bare hand along the head of the foundation wall in an unheated corner of the basement beats an hour spent walking the outside in July. It is also when anything moving inside a floor structure is most audible, because the house has gone quiet and the animal has nowhere else to be.

The rest of the calendar has its own uses here.

**Late August to November** decides how the winter goes. Close the house and every outbuilding before the animals move and the property simply does not acquire a winter population. The alternative is opening a finished ceiling in February to reach one.

**March to May** reads the ground before planting fills in. Burrow entrances along a fence line or under a shed, the winter's freeze-thaw record on a drive apron and a garage slab, and the grade faults at the base of the wall are all visible for a few weeks and then are not.

**June to August** is when the outside gets inventoried, and on an acre that is genuinely a season's work: under the shed, under the deck, the log store, the compost, the pool plant enclosure, the boundary brush, and every irrigation zone that has been soaking a wall four mornings a week for a decade.

One further seasonal item is specific to a place still being renovated continuously. Site disturbance moves rodents. An excavation next door for a pool, an extension or a drainage scheme evicts whatever was living in that ground, and the displaced animals go to the nearest available harbourage. If there is machinery on the neighbouring lot, that is the month to look at your own ground line rather than the following spring.

## Why does owner-occupancy change the shape of the job?

Because the person who can authorise every part of the work is standing next to you, which is rarer than it sounds.

Fewer than four per cent of occupied units here are rented. In practice that means one decision-maker who holds the house, the garage, the shed, the pool house, the yard, the wood line and the boundary — every element the work has to touch. In the dense parts of the town that authority is split across an owner, a managing agent and several tenants, and a scope that cannot reach the whole structure cannot finish the job. Here it can.

That produces a genuinely finite piece of work, and it is worth saying what finite looks like.

Reduce the population where the result can be retrieved. That qualification carries weight in a house with a finished basement, boxed chases and rooms sitting over a garage. Anything that dies inside one of those voids comes out only when the assembly does, so indoor reduction belongs where the result can be picked up.

Close the openings, on the residence and on every outbuilding, with material matched to the substrate.

Correct the ground: planting cut back off the base of the wall, stored material lifted and moved away, the woodpile relocated onto a hard base, the leaders extended so roof water leaves the building, and the branches pruned back from the roof line.

Record the lot as a schedule of located items with the material against each, anything unreachable named, and a date to look again.

Then stop. Two years of the same arrangement on a detached house here means the buildings were never closed. The reduction step is not the product; it is the first part of it.
