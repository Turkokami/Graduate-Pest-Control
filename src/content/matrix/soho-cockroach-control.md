## Why does the ground floor usually decide the outcome?

Because it is the same building, and because it is the only part of it with a commercial kitchen in it.

Nearly every residential building in this district began as a store-and-loft structure: a shop at street level, working or storage floors above, and a single set of services running from the cellar to the roof. Conversion put apartments on the upper floors and left the street-level tenancy exactly where it had always been. In SoHo that tenancy is very often food service, or retail with a back-of-house area, a staff room and a small kitchen.

That arrangement produces the most common and most frustrating pattern in the neighborhood. A residential unit reports [German cockroaches](/pest-library/german-cockroach/). The unit is treated. It goes quiet for a few weeks. It comes back. Nothing failed in the apartment. The source was never in the apartment.

The mechanism is unremarkable once it is stated. A commercial kitchen supplies warmth, grease, organic waste and standing water continuously, at a scale no household matches, and it discharges into the same house drain and runs beside the same risers as the apartments above it. A population fed at ground level does not stay at ground level, because the building offers it a vertical route with a warm, damp void at every stage.

What follows is a scoping decision rather than a product decision, and it is unwelcome for the reason that most correct answers in this trade are unwelcome: it costs more up front and it is the only version that ends.

**Monitor the whole vertical section** — the tenancy's prep and dishwash areas, the cellar, the vault access, the riser closets and every residential unit on the affected line, not just the one that called.

**Treat where the counts are, not where the complaints are.**

**Close the floor collars** where retrofitted plumbing crosses between the commercial floor and the residential ones. That is the highest-value single repair in a mixed-use building of this type.

**Put the tenancy on a program built for a food premises**, which is what [the commercial program](/commercial/) and, specifically, [restaurant pest control](/commercial/restaurant-pest-control/) exist to do.

For the general approach, see [cockroach control](/pest-control/cockroach-control/). For the rest of this neighborhood's building stock, [pest control in SoHo](/locations/soho/).

## What does the lease decide that no technician can?

Whether the survey is allowed to see the source.

This is the practical obstacle in SoHo far more often than anything structural, and it is worth naming directly because it determines what a proposal can honestly promise. A residential board or owner controls the apartments, the common parts and, usually, the cellar. The commercial tenant controls the back-of-house, the waste practice, the loading arrangement and the hours during which anybody can get in there. Whether the residential side can inspect the tenancy — or require it to hold a program, or see the records of one — is a matter of what the lease says.

Where the lease is clear, this is administration. Where it is silent, which is common in buildings converted decades ago under commercial leases written for a wholesale district, it becomes a negotiation. Three things make that negotiation go better.

**Frame it as shared drainage rather than as blame.** The tenant did not design the building either, and a tenancy that is treated as the accused will produce access grudgingly and late.

**Offer a single program rather than two contracts.** Two contractors working on opposite sides of the ground-floor slab, neither of them looking at the riser, is a way of paying twice for a problem neither can see. One program covering the whole structure, with separate monitoring regimes for the commercial and residential parts, is cheaper and it works.

**Write it into the next lease.** A pest management and access clause costs nothing at renewal and settles the question permanently. So does an alteration agreement that requires penetrations to be sealed when a shopfit opens a floor.

For a board weighing what to ask of a contractor and of a tenant, [choosing a pest control contractor](/compliance/choosing-a-pest-control-contractor/) sets out the questions, and [co-op and condo board obligations](/compliance/coop-condo-board-obligations/) the directors' frame.

## Where do the risers actually run in a converted loft?

Not where a purpose-built apartment house would put them, and finding out is a physical exercise.

These buildings had no domestic plumbing. Conversion cut new soil, vent and supply lines through nineteenth-century floors, avoiding girders and columns, reaching a house drain laid for a completely different purpose, and doing it in whatever line was cheapest at the time. Buildings reconfigured more than once have two or three generations of that work, each cut through the last.

The result is the void this insect actually lives in. It is warm, because hot supply and heating runs share the space. It is damp, because a cold line sweats, an old joint weeps, and a slow leak inside a boxed chase never reaches a ceiling and so never gets reported. And it is never disturbed — nobody opens a chase, cleaning does not reach into it, and applied material does not either unless somebody has deliberately decided to treat the void rather than the room.

To find it:

**Start at the house drain in the cellar.** Every riser in the building lands there. Following what leaves it upward tells you how many lines there are and roughly where they go, which no drawing in the building's file will.

**Look for the boxing.** Retrofitted plumbing in a loft is hidden in furring, a boxed column, a purpose-built closet or a fabricated bulkhead. Those are the chases, and they are almost always open at the top and the bottom, because the trade that built them was concealing a pipe rather than closing a cavity.

**Check the escutcheons, the toe kicks and the cabinet backs.** An escutcheon plate is a cover, not a seal. The gap behind a cabinet run and under its toe kick is frequently open to the void behind the wall.

**Look at the ceiling line.** Partitions in a converted loft usually stop at a dropped ceiling rather than at the structure. Above that line the floor is one space, and the deep cavity above the ceiling carries every service run since conversion.

Sealing those points is not cosmetic work. It is the part of the job that changes the building rather than the moment, and it belongs in the same list as everything under [structural exclusion](/pest-control/structural-exclusion/).

## Which species is it, and what is the vault contributing?

Two different problems, one of which is a plumbing problem and the other of which is a drainage problem.

**German cockroaches** are the apartment and kitchen problem: small, light brown, two dark stripes behind the head, permanently indoors, close to food and water, reproducing quickly, with the female carrying the egg case until shortly before hatching. That last detail is why populations rebound after treatment that did not plan a return visit. If the sightings are small insects in a kitchen or a bathroom at night, this is the species and the work is the riser, the harborage and every unit on the line.

**Oriental and American cockroaches** are the below-grade problem, and here they are structural rather than incidental. The cellar extends out under the sidewalk as a vault, takes surface water through every failed joint in a covering that has been driven over and patched for a century and a half, and drains through a nineteenth-century connection. It is damp by construction. [Oriental cockroaches](/pest-library/oriental-cockroach/) specifically indicate standing water and organic sludge — a blocked drain, a cellar floor that holds water, a failed floor drain, the ground around a sump. [American cockroaches](/pest-library/american-cockroach/) travel drain lines and can surface in an upper-floor bathroom having come up a stack from below, which is why they occasionally appear in apartments with no local source at all.

The reason to separate them before writing a scope is that the money goes to different places. A German cockroach program is monitoring, targeted material in harborage, sealing and access across a line of units and a tenancy. An Oriental or American problem is drainage repair and moisture correction, and if the drainage is not corrected nothing applied above it holds. The below-grade work overlaps almost entirely with [rodent control in SoHo](/pest-control/soho/rodent-control/), because the vault boundary and the house drain are the same features in both scopes.

One further organism is worth naming because it exploits the same voids and behaves differently: [pharaoh ants](/pest-library/pharaoh-ant/) are an ant, not a cockroach, and they are the case where the wrong material makes the problem structurally worse by splitting the colony. Small yellow-brown ants trailing in a bathroom are not a cockroach question.

## Why is monitoring worth more here than another round of product?

Because the building will not tell you where the population is, and because complaints are a poor sample in a building of small units.

The stock here is overwhelmingly studios and one- and two-bedroom units in large converted buildings, with a high vacancy and turnover rate. That combination means a substantial proportion of the apartments on any line are either empty, recently occupied by somebody who does not know what to look for, or occupied by somebody who has been managing quietly with retail products and has said nothing. Complaint-driven service reads that as an absence of infestation. It is nothing of the kind.

The alternative is not more material. It is a different unit of work.

**Map before you treat.** Small passive monitors in the kitchen and bathroom of every unit on the line, plus the riser closets, the shaft base, the cellar, the vault access and the commercial back-of-house, left for a defined period and then read. That produces counts by location rather than complaints by resident, and it sets a baseline — the only honest way to know afterwards whether anything worked. Work on proactive integrated pest management in multi-unit housing has repeatedly found severe infestations that complaint-driven service had never seen, precisely because every unit was examined.

**Weight the treatment to the source.** Treating six apartments evenly, the source apartment among them, is a way of spending a budget without changing an outcome.

**Return for the eggs.** The nymphs that hatch after the first round are why a single visit never finishes this insect.

**Read the monitors again at the end.** If counts collapsed on the treated line and stayed down through a return cycle, the work held. If they collapsed where you treated and rose two floors away, the scope was too narrow.

And a word about what makes the job harder: repellent retail sprays and foggers drive insects out of concentrated harborage into places that are more difficult to reach, which in a building like this means inside a chase or in the neighbor's kitchen. Residents who have used them should say so. It changes the approach; it does not cause offense.

## Why do the empty apartments matter as much as the occupied ones?

Because nobody is watching them, and because the work done in them creates the openings.

A quarter of the residential units in this district are unoccupied at any given time, and the units themselves are small — the stock is overwhelmingly studios and one- and two-bedroom apartments. An unoccupied unit contributes three things to a cockroach problem and none of them is obvious.

**It is unmonitored.** No one reports what they do not see, and a vacant apartment in the middle of a treated line is a gap in the map. If the monitoring layout only covers occupied units, the building has surveyed the part it could reach rather than the part that matters.

**It is dry and quiet, which changes where the insects go rather than whether they exist.** A vacancy removes the food but not the harborage. The population on that segment of the riser shifts toward the units above and below, which is why a building sometimes sees complaints intensify next door to an apartment that has just been vacated.

**It gets worked on.** Between tenancies is when a kitchen is replaced, a bathroom is redone, a floor is opened. That work is an opportunity and a hazard in equal measure. It is the cheapest possible moment to collar a stack properly and close the top and bottom of a chase, because it is all open. It is also the moment at which a contractor cuts a new penetration and closes it with whatever is in the van.

The board-level fix is a clause rather than a treatment. An alteration agreement that requires penetrations through floors and party structures to be closed in durable, non-gnawable material, inspected before the ceiling goes back, converts every renovation in the building into a piece of exclusion work paid for by somebody else. Buildings that adopt that get most of their riser sealed over a decade without ever commissioning it as a project.

## Does the season change anything in a building like this?

It changes the urgency and it changes what you can see.

Cockroach activity in converted loft buildings is year-round, but it concentrates indoors in winter for the straightforward reason that the building is warm and the outdoors is not. Heating season also warms the chases and the risers, which is exactly where the population lives, so the void becomes more attractive at the same time as the apartments do.

That makes late summer and early autumn the sensible moment to map a building. The correction work then happens as pressure rises rather than after it peaks, and the return visit lands during the season when the insects are most concentrated and therefore most findable.

Winter has one compensating advantage that is worth using. On a cold morning a converted loft shows you its air paths. Where warm air is escaping into a chase, a shaft or a floor cavity, that is a route, and it is visible in a way it never is in July. A survey done in January produces a better opening inventory than the same survey done in June, even though the sealing work itself is easier in mild weather.

Spring belongs to the parts of the building that took a winter of freeze and thaw: the vault covering, the roof and parapet, and any masonry that has been holding water. Correcting those removes the damp that supports the below-grade species before the warm months let them move upward. Summer is when the drainage and waste work gets done — rebuilding storage, clearing a vault drain, correcting a threshold that has settled — and when a commercial tenancy has the best chance of deep-cleaning back-of-house without shutting.

## What has to be repaired rather than treated?

A short, finite list that a building can actually schedule.

**Floor collars on every retrofitted stack**, closed at each floor in a material that cannot be gnawed or pushed past — and particularly at the slab between the commercial floor and the first residential one.

**The top and bottom of every boxed chase.** A chase closed at both ends stops being a corridor.

**Escutcheons, cabinet backs and toe kicks**, fitted and closed rather than resting against a wall.

**The leaks.** A weeping joint inside a chase, a sweating cold line in uninsulated boxing, a slow drip under a sink. A warm cavity with water in it sustains a population; the same cavity dry does not.

**The cellar and vault drainage.** Where the below-grade species are present this is the entire job — a floor drain that holds water rather than having dried out, a repaired house drain, a cleanout with its cover on.

**Service door thresholds**, with sweeps closing gaps to no more than a quarter of an inch.

That list is capital work rather than service work, which matters more than it sounds. A repair schedule can be planned against a building's own cycle and budgeted once. A service contract simply runs.

## Who has to authorise it, and what does the building take on?

The board or the owner, and the obligations arrive with multiple dwelling status whether or not anyone has noticed.

The Housing Maintenance Code puts the duty to keep the premises free of insects and other pests on the owner; in a cooperative that is the corporation. The substance is at [NYC Housing Maintenance Code — extermination and rodent eradication](/compliance/nyc-housing-maintenance-code-pests/). [Local Law 55](/compliance/nyc-local-law-55/) treats cockroach infestation as an indoor allergen hazard and adds a scheduled duty: periodic investigation of units and common areas, remediation by integrated pest management, and sealing of holes, gaps and cracks in durable materials. It states directly that pesticide use does not substitute for those measures.

Any application in a residence must be made by an applicator licensed by the state ([NYS pesticide applicator requirements](/compliance/nys-pesticide-applicator-requirements/)), and applications in a multiple dwelling carry notification duties — [Local Law 36 pesticide notification](/compliance/nyc-local-law-36-pesticide-notification/) and [the New York neighbor notification law](/compliance/ny-neighbor-notification-law/). An owner letting a loft rather than living in it should read [landlord and tenant pest responsibilities](/compliance/landlord-tenant-pest-responsibilities/); the duties surprise people who think of themselves as apartment owners.

Access is the constraint that decides whether any of it works. One apartment on a line that admits nobody can sustain a population for the whole building. Buildings that manage this well write their access and notice procedure before a case exists, present the request as ordinary maintenance, and state plainly that reporting is confidential. A resident who fears being identified to their neighbors does not report, and silence is the most expensive thing that can happen to a building with this insect.
