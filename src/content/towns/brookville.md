## Which Brookville is this, and why does the answer matter?

The Village of Brookville, incorporated in 1931 — and the question is not pedantry, because there are three of them.

Old Brookville incorporated on 7 November 1929. Brookville followed in 1931. Upper Brookville incorporated on 28 July 1932, the last village in Nassau County to do so until Atlantic Beach in 1962, and it took the name Upper only because Hope Goddard Iselin refused to live in a place called Lower Brookville. All three sit in the Town of Oyster Bay, all three are separate incorporated villages with their own boundaries, their own governments and their own codes, and the postal geography does not respect any of it.

For anyone doing physical work on a building, that has consequences. Permit thresholds, setback rules, tree and clearing provisions and property-maintenance standards are village-level instruments here, not town-level ones. One house on Wheatley Road and another a quarter of a mile along it can answer to two different building departments and two different sets of tree and clearing provisions. Where a job involves anything a village wants to see drawings for — a below-grade barrier, foundation repair, a structural closure at a roofline — the first question is which village, and the answer sets how the work is sequenced.

The village also runs its own police department, which is unusual at this size and revealing about the place. Brookville stood the force up on 1 June 2022, after the Old Brookville department ended its service, under an inter-municipal agreement now shared with Matinecock, Mill Neck and Cove Neck. Mayor Daniel H. Serota's explanation was that these are "very rural people on large pieces of property" and that "some of our roads are private lanes and fire lanes" — officers who drive them daily find addresses that a county car would not. That is a fair description of the working conditions for any trade here, ours included.

The village covers about 4.0 square miles, roughly 2,650 acres, and more than half of that arrived late: what incorporated in 1931 was a narrow tract along Cedar Swamp Road, and the addition of Wheatley Hills in the 1950s nearly doubled the land area. The boundary is younger than a good deal of the housing inside it.

## Why is an estate village mostly a 1990s village?

Because the estates were sold, subdivided and built over, and the buildings that replaced them are the ones we actually work on.

The Gold Coast history is genuine and documented. By the mid-1920s roughly 22 large estates stood here. Broadhollow was 108 acres with a 40-room manor for Winthrop W. Aldrich. Hillwood was a 70-room house on 178 acres for Marjorie Merriweather Post and Edward Francis Hutton. A medieval French chapel, St. Joan of Arc, was dismantled and re-erected in the village in 1926 before eventually moving to Marquette University.

Now look at the housing table. Of roughly 852 units at a median construction year of 1967, just 89 — 10.4 per cent — date from 1939 or earlier, with 23 more from the 1940s. The largest single cohort by a wide margin is the 1990s: 272 units, 31.9 per cent. The 1950s contributed 221 units and the 1960s 135. The 1970s produced 21 and the 1980s 58. Some 822 units, 96.5 per cent, are detached houses, and of 780 occupied units 724 are owner-occupied.

So the reputation rests on 89 buildings and the workload rests on the 272. What went up in the 1990s is a distinct and demanding building type: mansion-scale houses on two-acre lots, with multiple roof planes and valleys, dormers and turrets, deep overhangs, attached three- and four-car garages, finished lower levels below grade, pool houses, guest wings, generator and pool-equipment enclosures, and long runs of irrigation and low-voltage lighting entering the envelope. Each of those is a penetration. On a footprint of that size the perimeter to be surveyed runs to several hundred feet before you count the outbuildings, and the roofline has more linear feet of soffit, valley and flashing than three ordinary houses put together.

The 89 older buildings behave nothing like them. Masonry and heavy timber, rubble or early poured foundations, service wings, cellars with areaways and coal chutes, slate and tile roofs with complex flashing, and mortar joints that have taken eighty to a hundred winters. On estate ground, pre-war fabric often survives as secondary structures rather than as principal residences: gatehouses, stable blocks, carriage houses converted to living space, garages with quarters above. The DuPont-Guest Estate is the National Register example, listed in 2009: built between 1916 and 1918 by Carrère and Hastings in Georgian Revival, begun as White Eagle for Alicia Heyward Maddox, second wife of Alfred I. du Pont, complete but never occupied when it was auctioned in 1921 for $470,000 against a valuation over $1.5 million, then bought by Frederick Guest and renamed Templeton. It has belonged to the New York Institute of Technology since 1972.

There is one honest caveat on the numbers. The 2020 decennial census recorded 661 housing units in the village and 2,939 residents; the American Community Survey estimate puts the housing count at 852. A village this small sits inside wide margins of error, and neither figure should be treated as precise. The shape of the distribution is the reliable part.

## What does a ban on commercial development do to the rodent picture?

It removes the usual source and replaces it with two less obvious ones.

Brookville zoning requires a minimum of two acres for a residential property and prohibits commercial development outright. There is no retail strip in the village, no restaurant row, no supermarket loading dock, no shopping-center service yard. In almost every other market we work, those are where the standing rat population lives, and residential work amounts to protecting houses from a source that is a few hundred yards away and belongs to somebody else.

Here the source is somewhere else entirely. The first substitute is institutional, and it is discussed in the next section. The second is the property itself, and it is the one owners tend not to think of as a food source at all.

On a two-acre lot the reliable rodent sites are a short and consistent list. The compost heap, which is a heated food source with cover on top of it. Bird feeders, which distribute seed onto the ground continuously and which we see more of here than almost anywhere. The pool equipment pad and its enclosure, warm and undisturbed. The void beneath a raised deck or a hardscaped terrace, which is a ready-made burrow chamber with a roof already fitted. Stacked firewood against an outbuilding. Sheds and stables holding feed. A generator enclosure. On an estate-scale property these are spread out enough that no single one looks like a problem, and collectively they support a population indefinitely.

That changes the shape of the work. In a village with a commercial strip, the honest advice to a homeowner is often that exclusion protects the building but does not lower the population outside it. Here, a very large proportion of what is driving pressure on a property is on that property, and can actually be removed. Source reduction is not a token gesture in Brookville; it is the larger half of the result.

## What do three campuses inside the village boundary change?

They supply the concentration a village with no commerce would otherwise lack, and they do it at the wood line rather than at the street.

LIU Post occupies 307 acres of rolling hills in the village — the former Hillwood estate, which Marjorie Merriweather Post sold to Long Island University in 1951 for $200,000 and which was renamed for her father three years later. It carries residence halls, the Hillwood Commons student center, the Winnick Student Center dining hall and the 2,200-seat Tilles Center, and several of the original estate houses have been repurposed as academic buildings. Half of the NYIT Old Westbury campus falls inside the village. Long Island Lutheran Middle and High School is here as well. Postal geography confuses the picture further, since the LIU Post campus is frequently addressed as Greenvale.

Read that as a pest surveyor rather than as a prospectus. Dining halls and dormitories mean continuous food handling and continuous refuse handling on a fixed daily rhythm. Repurposed 1920s estate buildings mean masonry, cellars, service tunnels and roof details that were never designed for institutional occupancy. Grounds maintenance means composting, mulch storage and equipment yards. It is, functionally, the food premises the village zoning does not permit — and it operates twelve months a year.

The important structural point is the interface. A commercial strip presents to a road, with fences and rear yards between it and the nearest house. A campus of this kind presents to woodland. Animals moving off it do not have to cross tarmac and gardens; they move along a continuous canopy and understorey onto adjoining large-lot properties, arriving at the rear of a house rather than the front. That is why surveys on properties adjoining campus ground start at the treeline and work inward, and why roofline closure matters more here than perimeter work at grade.

## What is the spring line doing under these houses?

Keeping the ground wet at shallow depth, in a place whose own name records the fact.

Dutch settlers bought land at Wheatley and Brookville Roads in 1732 to build a church, and the district was called Wolver Hollow because wolves gathered at the spring-fed Shoo Brook to drink. The area was variously Cedar Swamp, Wolver Hollow, Jericho and Wheatley, then Tappentown around 1800 after a local family, and became Brookville officially by 1873 — informally, because the countryside had so many brooks. The Brookville Reformed Church, rebuilt in 1924 on that original 1732 site, is one of the oldest existing church congregations in the country.

A spring is a place where the water table meets the surface, and a spring line is a permanent shallow water table along a contour. A building sited on or beside one has a damp cellar or crawlspace irrespective of how carefully its gutters and grading were set, because the water is not arriving from the roof. It is already there. On a village where the 1990s houses have finished lower levels below grade, that distinction is expensive: a finished basement over a spring line hides the evidence rather than removing it, and the first sign is usually a carpenter ant trail on a stair stringer.

The wider ground is well-drained. Soils around the 550-acre Muttontown Preserve — Nassau County's largest, all fields, woodland, ponds and estate grounds — are described as an acidic, well-drained brown loam of the Montauk series. Structurally that is mixed news: water moving through soil rather than ponding on it never announces itself with a puddle. It goes down, along the foundation, into the sub-slab fill, and the owner has no surface symptom to point at.

The village keeps a four-acre bird sanctuary and a 19-acre nature park, and the James Preserve runs toward Greenvale. Next door, the North Shore Land Alliance holds the eight-acre Louis C. Clark Sanctuary in Old Brookville — upland forest and freshwater wetland with Cedar Swamp Creek through it — and the 42-acre Hope Goddard Iselin Preserve in Upper Brookville. Those are the reservoirs the wildlife pressure comes out of, and they are not going anywhere.

Water supply is pumped from underneath all of this rather than piped in from elsewhere: the Jericho Water District draws from 26 wells in the Magothy and Lloyd aquifers, stores over 12 million gallons in six tanks and distributes through more than 350 miles of mains. On two-acre lots the summer irrigation draw is the largest single household demand — and irrigation is also the most efficient way ever devised to keep the soil against a foundation permanently damp through the months when termites and carpenter ants are working.

Wastewater generally goes back into the same ground. Nassau County states that tens of thousands of homes and small businesses in the county run on cesspools and septic systems, that nitrogen from them is the largest single cause of degraded water quality on Long Island, that an average residential system discharges about 40 pounds of nitrogen a year, and that its grant program of up to $20,000 is written for properties outside a designated sewer area. Where a large-lot property is on an on-site system, a saturated or failing leaching field is a permanent moisture source under the yard and a structural pest driver in its own right.

## Which pests set the year on two-acre wooded ground?

Wildlife leads, insects follow the water, and rodents live in the outbuildings.

**[Gray squirrels](/pest-library/eastern-gray-squirrel/), [raccoons](/pest-library/raccoon/) and [big brown bats](/pest-library/big-brown-bat/) arriving off the preserve and campus woodland, denning late winter and late summer.** Continuous mature woodland meeting complex rooflines with deep overhangs, multiple valleys and dormers, alongside hundreds of acres of preserve and a campus of repurposed 1920s buildings. Cornell notes that bats and flying squirrels typically enter the upper portion of a building while others enter around the foundation, and that an animal evicted through a one-way door will often chew back in at a different point on the same building. On a roofline with this many feet of soffit and valley, "a different point" is not a hypothetical.

**[Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/), April through September.** The Northeastern IPM Center sets out the mechanism: nests in hollow, decaying wood including wood piles, tree stumps and wooden framed buildings; a preference for moist wood because it is easier to excavate and helps larvae survive; a damp window or door sill as a favored entryway; and foraging up to 300 feet from the nest. On two-acre lots that radius sits entirely within the property, which means the parent colony is very often in a stump, a log pile or a landscape timber and not in the house at all.

**[House mice](/pest-library/house-mouse/), October into December.** The population usually establishes in a detached garage, pool house, stable, workshop or generator enclosure and lives there for years before anything reaches the residence a hundred feet away. A single mouse produces enough droppings in a day — Cornell puts it at 50 to 60 — that a colony which has been in a stable block since spring announces itself in the main house within about seventy-two hours of moving.

**[Norway rats](/pest-library/norway-rat/), year-round.** Burrow systems under decking, terraces, pool surrounds and hedge lines, sustained by the compost, the feeders and the equipment pads described above.

**[Eastern subterranean termites](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/), swarming March through June.** Cornell Cooperative Extension asks for at least three inches between wood siding or baseboard and soil and eight inches of clean concrete between baseboard and soil, warns that adding topsoil often builds exterior grade up to sill level, and notes that yard wood debris and buried stumps will feed a colony until it is large enough to attack the structure. Estate-era planting leaves a great deal of buried wood behind, and thirty years of mulch beds on a 1990s house does the rest.

**Mosquitoes, late June through September.** Spring-fed low ground, decorative water features, pool covers and the container water that accumulates around outbuildings and stored equipment. Nassau County runs 44 surveillance trap sites and monitors thousands of street basins, sumps and ponds, but its instruction to residents is to eliminate standing water on and around their own property, which on a lot this size is a real piece of work rather than a slogan.

**Wasps and hornets, July through October.** Long eaves, porte-cocheres, pool houses and outbuildings give [eastern yellowjackets](/pest-library/eastern-yellowjacket/) and [bald-faced hornets](/pest-library/bald-faced-hornet/) places to build that nobody walks past daily, so nests here are usually found at full size rather than early. [Carpenter bees](/pest-library/carpenter-bee/) work unpainted pergola, rail and outbuilding timber.

**[Cluster flies](/pest-library/cluster-fly/), December into March.** On warm interior surfaces in midwinter, indicating an attic or wall-void overwintering site.

## What work does a Brookville property actually need?

Roofline closure first, source reduction second, treatment third — and on this stock the roofline is genuinely the expensive half.

**[Structural exclusion](/pest-control/structural-exclusion/)** carries the program. On the 1990s houses that means closing soffit returns and the junctions where roof planes meet, screening ridge and gable vents rather than blocking them, capping chimney flues, sealing the penetrations for irrigation, generator, pool and low-voltage runs, and correcting garage jambs on doors that are often three or four bays wide. On the older estate fabric it means repointing masonry with mortar and cement, closing areaways and coal chutes, and dealing with sill lines on foundations that were never flat. Everything installed is specified to the substrate: 26-gauge galvanised sheet metal fabricated to the shape of the opening, mortar and cement at masonry, Xcluder sweeps, fill fabric and below-grade barrier where a seal has to flex or sit against burrowing, and copper mesh, stainless mesh, hardware cloth and mechanical fixings for the rest. Expanding foam has no place in it — rodents chew through cured foam without effort, and a foamed opening looks like completed work to everyone who inspects it afterwards. Where an owner or an architect wants a written specification to put out for competitive pricing, that is [exclusion consulting](/pest-control/exclusion-consulting/).

**[Wildlife management](/pest-control/wildlife-management/)** is the service this village generates most, for the reasons above. Access is a real cost driver: roof heights, complex geometry and long runs mean the work is properly staged rather than improvised off a ladder. The animal itself is dealt with by a licensed wildlife partner firm, and the staged roofline closure that follows is the scope Graduate carries.

**[Rodent control](/pest-control/rodent-control/)** begins at the outbuildings and the landscape features, not in the kitchen. Where a burrow system is suspected under a terrace, a pool surround, decking or hardscape, [canine rodent detection](/pest-control/canine-rodent-detection/) narrows the search before anything is lifted, which on stone terracing is the difference between a targeted repair and a demolition.

**[Ant control](/pest-control/ant-control/)** is largely carpenter ant work, and here the nest is frequently off the building entirely. Finding it is the job; the treatment is the short part.

**Termite work is not something Graduate takes on, on two acres or anywhere else.** Saying that plainly is fairer than letting the grade line and the irrigation imply otherwise, because both conditions are genuinely present on this ground. Buried estate timber, thirty seasons of topped-up mulch and a summer irrigation draw that keeps foundation soil damp are what an owner reads about and then telephones about. On lot sizes like these the insect actually found is usually a carpenter ant, and the Northeastern IPM Center's foraging figure of three hundred feet means the parent colony may sit in a stump or a woodpile nowhere near the house — which is why [ant control](/pest-control/ant-control/) on this village is a search before it is anything else. The [eastern subterranean termite](/pest-library/eastern-subterranean-termite/) profile is the place to settle which insect is on the sill.

**[Wasp and hornet removal](/pest-control/wasp-hornet-removal/)** deals with the late-season nests on outbuildings, porte-cocheres and long eaves. **[Mosquito management](/pest-control/mosquito-management/)** on two acres is a survey of every container, cover, feature and low spot that holds water between rains, followed by removing them. Ornamental and turf work is outside what we take on.

Two useful references before any of this is commissioned: [choosing a pest control contractor](/compliance/choosing-a-pest-control-contractor/) sets out what to verify about whoever you hire, and the [neighbor notification law](/compliance/ny-neighbor-notification-law/) page covers what New York requires around residential pesticide applications — which on lots this size means the neighbor is often two hundred feet away and still entitled to know.

## Why does a four-hundred-foot perimeter change the survey?

Because it makes completeness a discipline rather than an outcome, and because the source is usually not on the building that called.

On an ordinary suburban house a competent surveyor can hold the whole envelope in their head. On a two-acre property with a mansion-scale principal building, a pool house, a garage block, a shed and a generator enclosure, that is not possible, and pretending otherwise is how openings get missed. The survey has to be systematic: elevation by elevation at grade, the roofline in sections from the ground, each outbuilding as a structure with its own perimeter, the landscape features that support populations, then the interior where evidence has been reported.

The sequence matters as much as the coverage. **Outbuildings before the residence**, because that is where a rodent population establishes first and where the food usually is. **The treeline and the ground between it and the house**, because branches touching a roof plane are a bridge and a log pile against a wall is a nest site and a termite reservoir at once. **Where water actually arrives after rain**, which on private lanes with private culverts is frequently not where the owner assumes. **The masonry-to-timber junction at grade**, which on a landscaped property is behind a planting bed and below the level anyone looks; moving soil back with a hand is often the most productive ten minutes of the visit. **The roofline last, in sections, with optics**, because a roof with this many valleys and dormers cannot be read in one pass, and the entry point is nearly always somewhere no interior inspection would ever find.

We will not price exclusion work on a property of this kind from photographs, because on two acres the source is routinely not on the building that called. The consultation itself costs nothing and is usually a phone call, since the age of the house and the position of the outbuildings tell us most of what we need. What carries a service fee is the written proposal and plan, because on a property with this many structures the document is the deliverable and it is detailed enough to be worth building from. If the project goes ahead, that fee comes off its cost.

## Which parts of Brookville do we cover?

The whole village, and the two Brookvilles either side of it.

**The original Cedar Swamp Road tract.** The narrow strip that incorporated in 1931, and the oldest part of the village as a legal entity.

**Wheatley Hills.** Added in the 1950s and nearly doubling the village's land area, which is why so much of the housing here postdates the boundary rather than the other way round.

**Wolver Hollow and the Shoo Brook ground.** The spring line, the low contour, and the properties where the water table is shallow enough to decide what happens in a cellar.

**Brookville Road and Wheatley Road.** The 1732 church site and the Reformed Church rebuilt on it in 1924, at the historic crossroads of the settlement.

**The LIU Post campus edge.** Two acres of woodland between a private house and 307 acres of institutional ground, addressed in places as Greenvale.

**The NYIT and Old Westbury boundary.** The DuPont-Guest Estate, on the National Register since 2009, and the campus that has held it since 1972.

**The private lanes and fire lanes.** Private drainage, private culverts, no municipal maintenance record, and access that has to be planned rather than assumed.

**The Jericho and Locust Valley school-district split.** Most of the village is served by Jericho, part by Locust Valley Central, and the line is another of the boundaries that runs through the middle of a place this small.

Around it: [Oyster Bay](/locations/oyster-bay/) to the north-east and its downtown block structure, [Syosset](/locations/syosset/) to the east on post-war subdivision ground, [Locust Valley](/locations/locust-valley/) to the north through the estate belt, [Glen Cove](/locations/glen-cove/) north-west with a very different half-rental housing profile, [Roslyn](/locations/roslyn/) south-west in its ravine, and [Manhasset](/locations/manhasset/) beyond that. Because a village that has zoned away its commercial pest sources has also removed the excuse, and what is left is the building and the ground it stands on.

Rodent exclusion is the spine of the company rather than one line on a service list, and the programs that prove it run in audited food premises, managed property and institutional buildings. In a village whose largest neighbors are campuses with dining halls and dormitories, that is not an abstract credential — it is the same kind of building, half a mile away.

A two-acre property rewards the approach because everything relevant is inside the boundary line and almost all of it is fixable. The compost heap can move. The feeder can move. The log pile can come off the wall. The grade can be cut back from the siding. The roofline can be closed properly once, in the right materials, instead of patched every second spring. None of that depends on a neighbor, a managing agent or a municipality, which is a position most of our markets do not enjoy.
