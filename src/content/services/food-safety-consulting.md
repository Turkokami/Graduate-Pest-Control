## What this engagement is, and what it is not

This is program development. Graduate builds the pest management program for a facility that is
certified — or preparing to be certified — to a food safety standard, and writes the pest portions of
the food safety plan so that they read as part of the plan rather than as an attachment to it.

It is an advisory engagement. It is taken with or without the service contract, and the two are
priced and scoped separately on purpose. A facility that keeps the contractor it has and wants the
program built properly around them is the more common client, not the exception. Consulting here is
not a route into selling service work, and the page says so because the alternative is a client
wondering, halfway through, what is actually being sold.

## Why the pest program is where audits go wrong

Pest control sits underneath a food safety plan as a prerequisite program. That word does a lot of
quiet damage: it makes the program sound preliminary, when what it actually means is that everything
above it assumes the program is working. An auditor does not open the pest file to see whether the
floor is clean. They open it to see whether the site knows what is happening in its own building.

The failure is almost never an infestation on audit day. It is a binder of service tickets that
records attendance rather than findings, a device map that does not match what is on the wall, a
corrective action closed with the word "monitoring", and eighteen months of catch data nobody has
ever plotted. Each of those is a documentation failure rather than a pest failure, and every one of
them is fixable before an auditor arrives.

## What gets built

**The device layout and the site map.** Numbered devices placed against the building's actual risk
— entry points, dock doors, drains, voids and the routes between them — rather than spaced evenly
around a perimeter because a template said so. The map has to match the floor exactly, because the
first thing an auditor does is pick a number off the map and go find it.

**The record structure.** What gets written down at each device, in what units, by whom, and where
it lands. Records that can be aggregated later have to be designed to be aggregated; a free-text box
cannot be trended, and nobody discovers that until the year is over.

**The trend analysis.** Catch data plotted over time, by area and by species, with the thresholds
that trigger a response written down in advance. This is the single most common gap. A site with
good raw data and no analysis has a filing system, not a monitoring program.

**The corrective action route.** What happens when a threshold is crossed, who owns the structural
half of it, who owns the sanitation half, and what closing an action actually requires. Sanitation
and structure meet constantly in pest work, and an action that does not name its owner does not get
done.

**The chemical documentation.** Labels, safety data sheets, application records and the approvals
that go with them, held where they can be produced rather than assembled the night before.

## What the standards actually ask for

Different schemes read the same program through different lenses, and the program has to satisfy the
one the site is certified to rather than a general idea of good practice.

**SQF** benchmarks against the Global Food Safety Initiative and expects a documented system audited
by a licensed certification body, with the pest element assessed as one of the prerequisite programs.
The device map, the service records, the trend analysis, the corrective actions and the chemical
documentation all have to stand up unedited.

**AIB** scores in bands, treats integrated pest management as one of five categories, and sets the
category score by the most significant observation rather than the average. One serious structural
condition can pull the category down regardless of how tidy the rest of the plant is — which is why
a program built against AIB has to be built against the structure, not the housekeeping.

**FSMA** is the regulatory floor underneath all of it in the United States. Preventive controls sit
with a qualified individual, and the rule at 21 CFR 117.180 is explicit that this person need not be
an employee of the facility. That is the provision this engagement works inside.

## The PCQI boundary, stated plainly

Ryan Katz holds PCQI, HACCP and AIB certification. Those are what make it possible to write pest
content into a food safety plan in the plan's own language and have it survive being read as part of
it.

They do not make Graduate the signatory preventive controls qualified individual for a facility. That
person owns the whole plan — allergens, sanitation, process controls, the lot — and the role stays
with the plant, where the responsibility for it belongs. Graduate writes the pest portion. The
signature is not for sale and would not be a good idea if it were.

## Where this work happens

The service business is New York City and Nassau and Suffolk counties. It goes no further, and the
service area pages say so.

Consulting is not bounded that way. Ryan travels for it, regularly and internationally, including to
offshore assets — which is what the TWIC, HUET and Tier 1 offshore credentials are for. A
Transportation Worker Identification Credential is what allows unescorted access to a secure maritime
facility; helicopter underwater escape training is what allows boarding the aircraft that reaches a
platform. Those are not decorations on the credentials page. They are the reason certain sites can be
worked at all.
