## What is rodent exclusion consulting, and who is it for?

It is advisory work on a building project, aimed at making the finished structure one that rodents cannot get into — done alongside the client's architect, general contractor or engineer rather than after they have all left.

It exists at three stages.

**Preconstruction.** Exclusion is built into the design and the specification before anything is poured. This is drawing review, specification language, materials selection and coordination between the trade sections that will actually create the openings.

**Mid-construction.** Penetrations and details are caught while they are still open and still cheap to correct. This is site observation at defined hold points, findings issued to the contractor, and correction before the assembly is covered.

**Post-construction.** A finished building that was never built to keep rodents out gets remediated. This is survey, evidence reading, retrofit design, and a sequence that has to account for a resident population and an occupied building.

The reader this page is written for is an architect, a general contractor, a developer or a facilities director. All four are used to being sold a service programme. None of them is often offered a design input, and the difference matters, because a pest programme is a recurring operating cost while a detail drawn correctly is a one-time capital item that removes the reason for part of that cost.

Very few firms in this market offer this at all. The reason is structural rather than mysterious: it requires somebody who can read a drawing set and hold a conversation about sequencing, and the pest control industry does not generally train that person. Graduate's owner comes at exclusion from the construction side, which is the whole basis of the practice and is described in more detail on [his page](/ryan-katz/).

## Why is exclusion a design problem rather than a maintenance problem?

Because every rodent entry point in a new building was created deliberately, by a licensed trade, for a legitimate reason, and drawn on a sheet somebody approved.

That sentence is worth sitting with, because it reframes the entire subject. Buildings do not develop holes. Buildings are assembled with holes in them. A plumber's job is to get supply and waste from one place to another, and the passage through the slab, the plate or the shaft wall is a necessary part of doing it. An electrician's job is to get conduit to a panel. A mechanical contractor has to get a duct or a refrigerant line through an exterior wall. A sprinkler fitter has to get a main across a corridor and drops through a ceiling. None of those people has been given responsibility for closing the assembly behind their work, and in most specifications nobody has.

The result is a structure with dozens to hundreds of deliberate passages through its compartments, most of them oversized on purpose because a sleeve must allow movement, and most of them left as an annular space that is either empty or filled with something soft.

A rodent does not need to breach the envelope in a clever way. It needs one below-grade or at-grade opening, and after that it needs the building's own service pathways, which run continuously from the cellar to the roof. That is why a mouse problem in a fifth-floor apartment is so rarely a fifth-floor problem, and why a maintenance response — devices, service visits, a schedule — manages a symptom that the drawings created.

The design-stage version of the same problem costs a fraction as much to solve, because at that stage the answer is a note, a detail and a spec paragraph rather than a demolition crew.

## What actually gets specified at the preconstruction stage?

A review of the whole drawing set against a list of failure points, and then written requirements that survive value engineering because they are stated as performance rather than preference.

**Site and civil.** Grade and drainage against the façade. Planting beds and irrigation directly at the wall, and dense groundcover, which is the most reliable rodent harbourage anyone ever installs on purpose. The waste enclosure — its slab, its gate clearances, its bollards, whether the pad drains. Loading dock geometry, dock levelers, dock seals and the gap under a leveler pit lid. Site utility trenching, which is a continuous soft path from the street to the building unless it is treated. Storm and sanitary structures, and the junction where a lateral meets the building.

**Below grade and at the slab.** Foundation wall and footing junction. Under-slab utility passes. The slab edge and the perimeter joint. Elevator pits, sumps, and grease interceptors, which are all connected to something. Where burrowing pressure is expected, a below-grade barrier specified along the trench line and at the junctions rather than described in general terms.

**Envelope openings.** The door hardware schedule is the single highest-yield document in the set. Sweeps, thresholds, astragals at pairs, and the clearance a door will actually have after it has been adjusted twice. Overhead coiling doors, their guides and the bottom bar. Louvers and their screening, specified by mesh rather than by the word "screened". Weep holes in masonry veneer, which must stay open for drainage and therefore need stainless or copper mesh, not sealant — this detail is drawn wrong more often than any other on the list. Rainscreen cavity terminations at the base and at heads. Roof-to-wall junctions, soffits, ridge and gable venting.

**Mechanical, electrical, plumbing and fire protection.** A sleeve and penetration schedule that says what happens in the annular space, at what size, in what material, and which trade section owns it. Escutcheons where a line enters a finished surface. Duct and line-set passes through exterior walls. Sprinkler mains and drops through rated assemblies.

**Interior compartments.** Open cores in the top course of concrete masonry, which are open shafts unless capped. Cavity and chase walls. Plumbing walls behind fixture banks. Kitchen and process equipment bases and the void beneath them. Cove base detailing at food-handling floors. Casework toe kicks, which are a void nobody inspects for the life of the building.

The deliverable at this stage is not a report. It is red-line comments on the drawings, edited language in the specification sections, and a short set of standard details the design team can incorporate under their own seal.

## What gets caught during construction, and when?

Everything the drawings did not anticipate, which on a real project is a substantial share of it — and the value depends entirely on being there in the window where the assembly is still open.

The windows are predictable enough to schedule against.

**Before backfill.** Site utilities are visible, the foundation penetrations are visible, and any below-grade barrier has to go in now or not at all. Once the trench is closed, the opportunity is gone for the life of the building.

**After rough-in, before insulation and board.** This is the most productive site visit on any project. Every plumbing, electrical, mechanical and fire protection penetration through plates, shaft walls and floor assemblies is open and visible at once. Corrections at this stage are handed to a trade that is still mobilised, still has the material, and can execute the detail in minutes.

**After masonry and veneer, before final grade and paving.** Weep holes, the base of the cavity, the slab edge and the finished relationship between grade and the wall are all still reachable.

**Before the ceiling grid and soffits close.** Horizontal service runs and the tops of partitions become permanently inaccessible when the grid goes in.

**At substantial completion.** Doors get adjusted, sweeps get installed, and hardware gets its final tuning. A door that closed correctly in September frequently does not in January.

What comes out of a site visit is a findings list keyed to location, with the detail required and the trade section responsible, issued to the general contractor's superintendent through whatever channel the project already uses. Nothing here needs a new process. It fits into the same coordination meeting and the same punch mechanism that every other discipline uses.

The other product of these visits is a photographic record of each detail before it was covered. That record is worth more than it looks. Two years later, when something recurs, the difference between opening four walls and opening one is knowing exactly what is behind each of them.

## What can be done to a building that is already finished?

Most of it, but the order changes and the cost profile inverts.

Post-construction remediation starts with a survey rather than a drawing set. That means reading the building the way the animals are using it: rub marks along travel routes, droppings and their distribution and age, gnawing that is pale and fresh at one point and weathered a foot away, damaged insulation, and the moisture conditions that explain a good deal of it. Where the evidence points into a chase or a wall, that is where access gets made.

Then a decision has to be made that new construction never has to make: there is a population inside already. Seal an occupied building without reducing that population first and you have not removed the animals, you have removed their exit — and a colony pushed into the wall voids is a considerably harder problem than the one that prompted the call. The sequence is reduce, seal, verify, and it is not negotiable regardless of what the programme schedule would prefer. That sequencing is set out in more depth under [structural exclusion](/pest-control/structural-exclusion/) and [rodent control](/pest-control/rodent-control/).

The retrofit itself divides into work that is straightforward and work that is expensive. Door hardware, dock seals, louver screening, weep holes, exterior slab edge, roofline and utility penetrations at the envelope are all reachable and are usually the majority of the actual traffic. Interior compartment work — chases, shaft walls, floor penetrations at every level, open masonry cores — requires opening finishes, coordinating with occupants, working off-hours in a residential or hospitality setting, and repairing and repainting afterwards.

For an owner, the honest framing is that post-construction exclusion buys the same outcome as the preconstruction version at several times the price, and it is still usually the cheapest thing on the table compared with an indefinite service programme against a building that keeps refilling. That calculation is what most [property management](/commercial/property-management-pest-control/) and [co-op and condo](/commercial/coop-condo-pest-control/) clients are really deciding.

## Which trades create the penetrations that matter?

Four, and they do not contribute equally.

**Plumbing** produces the highest count and the most consequential passes. Waste stacks run the full height of a riser and pass through every floor. Supply lines pass through bottom plates, shaft walls and fixture walls. Vents pass through the roof. Every fixture bank has a wall behind it that has been drilled repeatedly. The annular space at a sleeve is intentional — it exists so the pipe can move — and it will still be there in fifty years unless it is packed and sealed as a specified detail.

**Electrical** produces the highest count of small openings, which people underestimate because a mouse needs about a quarter of an inch and a rat about half. Conduit through top plates into ceiling voids. Panel and gear penetrations. Service entrance. Low-voltage and data, which on most jobs is drilled by whoever gets there last and inspected by nobody.

**HVAC** produces the largest individual openings. Duct passes through rated and unrated assemblies. Refrigerant line sets through the exterior wall behind a condenser, which is the single most reliably unsealed penetration in residential construction. Condensate lines. Exhaust and make-up air louvers, which have to keep passing air and therefore need screening rather than closure.

**Fire protection** produces penetrations that carry a complication. Sprinkler mains, branch lines and drops pass through rated assemblies, and the firestop assembly at those passes is a listed system. An exclusion detail must sit alongside that listed assembly rather than modifying it, and the packing material must be compatible with the listing. This is the coordination point that most often needs a conversation with the design team rather than a note, and it is the one an unqualified pest contractor will get wrong in both directions — either compromising a rated assembly or leaving the pass alone entirely because it looked like somebody else's problem.

None of these trades is at fault. Each one delivered what its section required. The gap is that no section required the assembly to be closed behind them, and that is precisely the gap a specification can close.

## Why is the same detail almost free now and expensive later?

Because the cost of an exclusion detail is almost entirely the cost of reaching it.

Consider a single annular space around a waste line where it passes through a shaft wall. Handled at rough-in, the work is: the trade is already standing there, the opening is visible, the material is on the cart, and the detail takes a few minutes. There is no access cost, no finish cost, no coordination cost and no disruption cost.

The same detail after the building is finished requires, in order: establishing that the pass exists at all, which frequently means opening a wall to find out; creating access; executing the identical detail; closing the wall; taping, sanding and painting; and coordinating the whole sequence around whoever occupies the space. In a hotel that is a room out of service. In a residential building it is a tenant notification and an off-hours crew. In a food plant it is a production window and a sanitation cycle after.

There is a second cost that does not appear on any invoice, which is the recurring service against the pathway that was never closed. A building that keeps refilling gets managed, and the management continues for as long as the building stands. Priced over even a few years, that operating line typically dwarfs what the detail would have cost at rough-in by a wide margin.

And there is a third cost that is harder to quantify and lands hardest on the people this page is written for: the reputational one. A new building with a rodent complaint in its first year is a problem for the developer, the design team and the property manager simultaneously, and none of them can point at a drawing that says whose scope it was.

This is the entire argument for the preconstruction stage, and it is not a subtle one. The intervention is cheapest at exactly the moment when nobody is thinking about rodents.

## What should a specification section for exclusion actually contain?

Nine things, and it should be written as performance requirements with a materials list attached, so that it survives value engineering and so that a trade contractor can price it properly.

**1. Scope and definitions.** What constitutes an exclusion detail, which assemblies are in scope, and the dimensional criteria: a persistent opening of a quarter of an inch or more in the envelope or between compartments is a defect, and half an inch is a defect for larger species. State the numbers, because "seal all openings" is unenforceable and every trade knows it.

**2. Performance criteria.** No persistent opening at or above the stated dimension. Ventilation openings screened at a stated mesh rather than closed. Materials resistant to gnawing. Details mechanically fastened, not adhered.

**3. Approved materials.** Named, so that a submittal can be reviewed against something.

**4. Prohibited materials.** Named explicitly, and this list matters as much as the approved one. Expanding foam in any role. Ordinary steel wool, which rusts and stains. Sealant or caulk used alone as a barrier. Tape and plastic sheeting. Screening coarser than quarter-inch hardware cloth.

**5. Submittals.** Product data for each material. Shop drawings for any custom-fabricated closure. Sample details for the repeated conditions — the typical pipe pass, the typical louver, the typical door bottom.

**6. Coordination clause.** Which trade section owns which penetration type. This is the paragraph that prevents the whole section from becoming nobody's work, and it is the one most often left out.

**7. Firestopping compatibility.** Where a penetration is in a rated assembly, the exclusion detail is installed alongside the listed firestop system without modifying it, and materials must be compatible with the listing.

**8. Field quality control.** Named observation hold points tied to the construction sequence, a findings and correction mechanism, and a requirement that identified conditions are corrected before the assembly is covered.

**9. Closeout.** An as-built record of the details executed, a photographic log keyed to location, and a short operations note telling the facilities team what was installed, where, and what to look at annually.

On most projects the practical route is a Division 1 requirement plus edited language in the trade sections that actually create the penetrations, rather than a standalone section that the trades never read. MasterFormat does carry a pest control devices heading under Division 10, and it is a reasonable home for the materials list, but a paragraph in the plumbing and electrical sections gets read by the people holding the drill.

## What materials belong in the specification?

A short list, chosen because each one is still a barrier in ten years, and a prohibition list that is just as important.

**Custom-fabricated 26-gauge galvanised sheet metal.** Cut and formed to the actual opening, not trimmed off a stock piece until it looks about right. This is the backbone of the programme and it is what closes larger structural gaps, protects chewable edges, and caps conditions like open masonry cores and soffit returns.

**Cement and mortar.** At masonry, at the slab edge, at foundation penetrations and wherever the substrate is itself masonry. Repointing a failed joint is exclusion work even though it looks like restoration.

**Xcluder material.** Door sweeps at thresholds, fill fabric packed into annular spaces and around services, and Xcluder GEO specified along trench lines and at junctions where the barrier has to live under the finished grade. These are the products that handle the conditions where a rigid closure will not work — a door that has to keep opening, a pipe that has to keep moving, a trench line that has to stay buried.

**Copper and stainless mesh.** For packing an annular space where the fill must not corrode. Ordinary steel wool rusts and leaves a brown streak down a new façade within a season or two; these do not, which is why they are the only fills named in the specification.

**Quarter-inch hardware cloth.** For any opening that has to keep moving air: louvers, vents, weep conditions, crawlspace and foundation venting.

**Mechanical fasteners throughout.** An adhesive bond is a prediction about a substrate nobody prepared, made on installation day about conditions ten winters away.

And the prohibition, stated without hedging: **we do not foam.** Not as a filler, not as backing behind something better, not as an air seal over it, and not anywhere in a specification section we write. The defence usually offered for foam is that a soft fill does no harm so long as a proper barrier sits in front of it. On a drawing that holds up. On a site it does not, for two reasons. The first is that a foamed opening reads as completed work to the next person who opens that assembly, whether that is an inspector next month or a facilities technician five years from now. The second is that foam is quick, and quick material wins the last hour of a Friday against a detail that has to be measured, cut and fastened. Putting it on the prohibited list takes that decision away from everyone on the job, ourselves included.

## How does this coordinate with the construction schedule?

Through hold points, which is a mechanism every project already understands, so nothing new has to be invented.

A typical engagement maps onto the schedule roughly like this.

**Design development.** Drawing review and comments while the design team can still draw the answer rather than note it.

**Fifty to one hundred percent construction documents.** Specification language finalised, standard details issued for incorporation, and the coordination clause settled between the trade sections.

**Pre-installation meeting.** A short session with the general contractor's superintendent and the affected trades, before rough-in, covering what the details are and who owns which one. This meeting is worth more than any single site visit, because it converts a specification into something the people holding the tools have actually heard.

**Hold point one — before backfill.** Site utilities, foundation penetrations, below-grade barrier.

**Hold point two — after rough-in, before insulation and board.** The big one.

**Hold point three — after veneer, before final grade and paving.** Weep conditions, cavity base, slab edge, grade relationship.

**Hold point four — before ceiling grid and soffit closure.** Horizontal runs and partition tops.

**Hold point five — substantial completion.** Doors, sweeps, hardware adjustment, dock seals, final walk.

**Post-occupancy verification.** One visit after the first full season, because doors move, mortar cures, backfill settles and the first winter reveals what the summer hid.

Each hold point produces a findings list keyed to location and trade, delivered into the project's existing coordination channel. On fast-track work the hold points compress and follow the sequence by area rather than by project phase, which is fine — the logic is that observation happens while the assembly is open, and on a phased job that simply means being there more often for shorter visits.

The one thing that does not compress is the pre-installation meeting. A project that skips it generates the same findings three times, because each trade discovers the requirement independently and late.

## Which building types get the most out of this?

The ones where a rodent event is not an inconvenience but a reportable, closable or contractual problem.

**Food manufacturing and processing.** An audit scheme does not care that the building was recently built; it cares whether the pest programme is documented and whether the structure supports it. A plant designed with exclusion in it walks into its first audit with a structural argument rather than a device count. That work is described under [food facility programmes](/commercial/food-facility-ipm/).

**Restaurants and mixed-use buildings with a food tenant at grade.** The tenant fit-out is where most of the damage is done, because a kitchen is drilled repeatedly by trades working fast in a small space, and the building above inherits the result. See [restaurant pest control](/commercial/restaurant-pest-control/).

**Multifamily, co-op and condo.** A new residential building with an internal pathway problem produces complaint chains, code exposure and a board that has to explain it. Building-wide exclusion at construction is dramatically cheaper than the same work retrofitted through occupied apartments.

**Hotels.** Every remediated room is a room out of inventory, and brand standards do not care why. [Hospitality programmes](/commercial/hotels-hospitality-pest-control/) are built around avoiding that.

**Institutional and healthcare buildings.** Settings where a sighting is a reportable event and where the tolerance for retrofit disruption is close to zero.

**Substantial residential renovation.** Not only new construction. A gut renovation opens exactly the assemblies that matter and closes them again in a matter of weeks, and it is the best opportunity a private house will ever get. Older housing stock across [Huntington](/locations/huntington/), [Great Neck](/locations/great-neck/) and [Manhasset](/locations/manhasset/) tends to be renovated in exactly this way, and pre-war city buildings in [the Upper East Side](/locations/upper-east-side/), [Brooklyn Heights](/locations/brooklyn-heights/) and [Park Slope](/locations/park-slope/) go through the same cycle apartment by apartment.

## What does the local building stock change about the specification?

More than a national detail library would suggest, because the failure points follow construction era and type rather than geography in the abstract.

Across [Long Island](/locations/long-island/), the recurring conditions are frame construction over fieldstone or rubble foundations with mortar joints that open with freeze-thaw cycling, crawlspaces that stay damp through the season, attached garages with door seals that stop meeting the jamb, and additions built onto original structures where the junction between the two was never closed properly. New construction there is mostly slab or full basement with a straightforward perimeter, which makes the below-grade and slab-edge details the ones worth the attention.

In [New York City](/locations/nyc/), the specification shifts almost entirely to internal compartmentation. Shared walls, shared spaces and shared sewer systems define the problem. Party walls between adjoining buildings have voids at the joist pockets. Chases run floor to floor and are frequently open at the floor line. Cellar drain lines and house traps deteriorate and become a route from the sewer into the building, which is why so many apparent street-level rat problems are actually interior ones. Compactor rooms, chutes, sidewalk vaults and cellar hatches all connect the building to the street in ways a drawing set rarely presents in one place.

A rat in a city building is usually a [Norway rat](/pest-library/norway-rat/), which burrows and enters from below, and a mouse is usually a [house mouse](/pest-library/house-mouse/), which enters at grade and then uses the risers. The two profiles lead to different priorities in the same specification, which is why the survey precedes the detail list rather than the other way around.

## How is this engaged, and what does it cost?

The first conversation is free and is usually a call: project type, stage, delivery method, and what the owner is actually worried about.

From there, a **written proposal and plan carries a service fee**, because the documented plan is the deliverable rather than a step towards one. The drawing comments, the specification language, the detail set and the hold point schedule are the product, and they are the kind of document that has been handed straight to somebody else to price against. **If you proceed with the project, that fee comes off the cost of the work.**

No prices appear on this site. A published figure for work that ranges from a single-family renovation to a food plant would be either meaningless or misleading, and a per-unit rate for exclusion is the number that gets quoted by firms who have not yet looked at the building.

What we will do on that first call is tell you honestly whether the stage you are at still allows the cheap version. If a building is already closed up, we will say so, and the conversation becomes a remediation conversation with a different price attached.
